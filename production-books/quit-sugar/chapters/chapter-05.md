Chapter 5
THE LIFT THAT MAKES THE LOW

*The lift you feel from BAD SUGAR puts nothing new into you; it lifts for minutes a low the last dose built.*

### THE DESK AT THREE

You know this hour better than any lecture could teach it.

It is three o'clock. The screen has gone bright and dull at once. The morning push is old news. Lunch sits two hours behind — a sandwich, a salad, something plain that should have carried you. Your shoulders have dropped an inch. Your eyes keep leaving the page and coming back to the same line. The drawer in the desk knows it before you do.

"I need sugary somethings to get me through," you think. "I need sugar to function. Just something quick and sweet to get through the afternoon."

We all thought it. We all reached the same way. The hand slides to the drawer before the mind has finished the sentence. A bar. A biscuit from the tin in the kitchen. A bottle that sweats cold. The wrapper cracks too loud in the quiet office and you lower your voice around it, half ashamed to be heard, half eager to begin.

The first bite lands fast. The first sip lands faster. For ten minutes the fog thins. The typing quickens. The joke at the next desk sounds funny again. You sit up. You tell yourself you were right. You took it for a genuine treat or fuel.

Watch what happens next with the eyes of the investigator you now are, blame removed and sight clear.

By half past three the brightness has thinned at the edges. The tongue feels coated. A thirst comes that water does not quite clear. The shoulders that lifted at three have settled again. The mind that felt sharp for ten minutes starts to bargain for the rest of the day. By four the brightness has gone. The head feels coated. The tongue feels thick and calls for water and calls for more sweet at once. The same line on the screen swims again. The shoulders drop two inches this time. The hand that was satisfied at three stirs at half past three, then pulls at four. You know that empty, twitchy, slightly shaky, need-something-sweet-now feeling by heart.

So you reach again. A second dose, smaller in hope and larger in hurry. It does less and costs more. The second buzz is shorter, the second fog deeper. By five you are counting minutes to leaving, not from work done but from fuel gone. You walk out with a coated mouth, a restless stomach, and a head full of dose-talk about what will close the evening.

I have lived that desk a thousand afternoons. I tell you flat what it is.

If the bar gave energy, why did the hour after need another bar? If the drawer made work possible, why did work stop an hour later? If the fix filled the gap, why were you emptier after it than before?

Answer honestly and there is only one honest answer. The dose did not power the afternoon. The afternoon powered the dose-talk, and the dose borrowed the morning from you.

Stay at that desk a little longer, because the detail matters more than the outline. At quarter to three the slump arrives first as a mood. You sigh without knowing you sighed. You stretch. You blame the meeting, the weather, the poor sleep, the age on your shoulders. You tell yourself you are not a person who snacks at the desk, you are a person who needs to concentrate, and concentration needs help. The drawer opens an inch. You close it. You open it again. The talk in the head gets louder while the work gets thinner. "Just one small thing, then back to it. I have been good all morning. One quick thing will sharpen me."

We said that because we believed the sweet would sharpen. We read the heaviness as proof the body was out of fuel and the sweet as fuel poured in.

Look at what the ten minutes actually held. The eyes widened. The breath quickened. The hands moved faster. Not strength added. Noise quieted. The low that had been building since the morning dose was muted for minutes, and the muting was mistaken for power. You did not rise. You returned toward where you had started the day, touched it for ten minutes, then sank again.

That is why the lift has the shape you described so well yourself: short term lift then fast drop. You said it exactly. "I would have something quick and sweet but now know that will only give a short term lift which will inevitably be followed by a fast drop." I am not putting words in your mouth. Those are your words from your afternoons. The lift was short because it was borrowed. The drop was fast because borrowing always comes due by evening.

Ask the desk three plain questions and let the desk answer. When did plain lunch ever send you hunting bread twenty minutes later with shaking hands? When did a glass of water ever demand another glass in twenty minutes to keep you upright? When did soup eaten hungry ever leave you foggier than before it? You know the answers because your body gave them daily while the mind was busy blaming itself.

The fact is, no fixed dose ever carried an afternoon. The afternoon carried the dose-talk, and the dose-talk carried you to the drawer.

Follow the same desk through a whole week and the pattern tightens. Monday three o'clock: one bar, ten good minutes, fog by four, second reach at half past four, coated evening. Tuesday three o'clock: the drawer opens at twenty to three because Monday taught it to open earlier. Wednesday: a bottle at noon to hold off three, then a bar at three, then biscuits at four because neither held. Thursday: two draws before lunch because the morning felt flat from Wednesday night. Friday: sweet from ten onward, grazing, sipping, unwrapping, the desk littered, the work thin, the head loud. Each day the lift gets shorter and the sag gets longer, yet each day the mind says the lift is needed more. If the helper helped, would need grow as help grows? If the fuel fuelled, would the engine run thinner the more fuel it takes?

We thought we were topping up. We were digging the hollow deeper and calling the digging a repair.

There is a second witness at that desk besides you. The colleague who takes no sweet at three. Watch her without envy and without scorn, as an investigator watches. She sags a little at three. Every human sags a little at three. She drinks water. She stands. She opens the window. She eats nothing, or she eats an apple slowly, or she waits for dinner. Her sag passes in twenty minutes without a second act. She does not climb bright for ten minutes and crash for two hours. She dips and returns. Compare that curve to yours on the sweet day. Yours spikes higher for ten minutes and sinks lower for two hours. Hers moves an inch. Yours moves a foot. Which body looks fuelled and which looks whipped?

"I need sugar to function," you say. "Without sugary somethings to get me through, work stops."

I hear you. We all said it at that desk with the drawer half open. Now test it against your own week. Did work stop before the dose or after it wore off? Did the fog arrive on an empty body that had eaten nothing since morning, or on a body that had taken sweet at ten, at twelve, at three? Did the clearest mornings of your life follow heavy sweet evenings or plain evenings? Did the sharpest hours you can remember come after a bar or after a night and a breakfast that left the tongue clean?

It was you that did the work while the dose took the bow. Your legs carried you to the meeting. Your mind solved the problem before the wrapper opened. Your breath steadied you before the sip.

### TIGHT SWEET SHOES

Let me put a picture on your feet so you can never unsee it.

Imagine a man who wears shoes a size too small from eight in the morning. He pinches his toes into them. He laces them tight. He walks the corridors wincing. He sits at meetings with his feet throbbing under the table. He tells no one. He eats lunch standing so no one sees him shift his weight. He takes the stairs slowly because each step bites. By five his feet burn. At six he kicks them off in the hall and groans. "Ah," he says, eyes closed. "What a comfort. What shoes."

Would you envy his groan? Would you ask where he buys such marvellous shoes? Would you believe shoes give comfort?

You would tell him the comfort came from stopping pain he had paid all day to feel. You would tell him to wear shoes that fit and keep the evening comfort without the daytime pinch. You would not call him weak for aching. You would call the shoes wrong.

We did the same with BAD SUGAR, hour by hour.

Morning dose pinches. It spikes the system high and quick, then leaves the low behind it. Midday dose pinches tighter to lift the morning dip. Afternoon dose pinches tightest to lift the midday dip. Each removal feels sweet because each wearing hurt. The short term lift then fast drop you described so well is not character. It is footwear.

Take the tight shoes off for good and the evening groan goes with them. Keep them and you must keep paying for the groan you call a treat.

Ask it of the shoes and ask it of the sweet in the same breath. Did the shoes give comfort, or lend comfort they had stolen? Did the afternoon bar hand you power, or hand back for ten minutes the steadiness the morning bar had taken? Did you rise above the clear-headed person who never dosed, or climb for minutes toward that person and then sink below?

The fact is, you never rose above. No dose ever put in you what was not already yours by body-right. It quieted for minutes a noise it had planted hours before, then planted the next noise. The true source of the ten-minute brightness was you — your blood, your breath, your nerves doing their quiet work — with the credit stolen mid-flight by a sweet coat.

That is the whole fuel story seen without blame. Not a weak will. A tight shoe mistaken for a foot.

Walk that picture through a full day so it sticks. Eight in the morning: coffee with three sugars and a pastry on the run. The spike feels like waking. Nine thirty: the edge wears off, the head dips, the hands get light. Ten thirty: biscuit with tea to lift the dip. Eleven thirty: dip again, deeper, plus thirst. Twelve thirty: sandwich plus sweet drink to get through. Two: heavy eyelids though the work is light. Three: the desk hour we just lived. Four thirty: second drawer hour. Six: kitchen raid before dinner because dinner feels too far. Eight: dessert to close. Ten: hunt while the house sleeps. Each lift is the shoe taken off for ten minutes. Each dip is the shoe laced back on. By night the feet are raw and the mind says shoes are the only comfort it knows.

Now picture the same day in shoes that fit. Breakfast eaten hungry, plain and full. Mid-morning clear. Lunch eaten hungry, plain and full. Three o'clock dip, small and human, passing with water and air. Dinner eaten hungry, plain and full. Evening quiet. No groan at six because no pinch from eight. The comfort that remains is not dramatic. It does not make the eyes close. It simply stays. That staying comfort is what your body gives when no dose borrows against it.

Which day needed will and which day needed truth? Which day fought the feet and which day changed the shoes?

If the shoe comforted, why did the foot ache more each week? If the sweet powered, why did the afternoon end earlier each month? If removal proved value, why did value need daily renewal with no end?

Keep that picture by the door where you leave your shoes at night. In the morning the tight pair sits waiting, polished by habit, promising the evening groan you have learned to love. The fitted pair sits beside it, plain, unpolished, saying nothing. No one watching would call the tight pair a gift. No one watching the desk would call the drawer a helper once the whole day is seen. The eye loves the groan only while the pinch is hidden. Show pinch and groan together in daylight and the trick ends.

### THE SHORT LIFT AND THE LONG SAG

Follow one dose through the body the way you followed it through the hour, and the shape holds.

You eat the sweet. It arrives fast because it was built to arrive fast. Bliss-point engineers shape it so — crunch, melt, salt against sweet — to hit quick and leave quick. The mouth wakes. The wanting wakes with it. Each hit re-fires the wanting for the next, small beside hard drugs but ample to turn the loop. Not pleasure added. Wanting renewed.

For some, the surge calls a hard answer from the body and the hour after sags as the surge is cleared — not a clinical low, only the aftermath felt as fog and tug. For most afternoons no needle would find anything dramatic. The head still clouds. The temper still shortens. The "I-need-to-eat-something-RIGHT-NOW feeling" still climbs at four though dinner sits only two hours off. Symptoms without a dramatic number. A sag without a diagnosis. If you carry diabetes or take medicine for blood sugar, your clinician stays in charge while you read.

We mistook that sag for need of another lift. We read the signal back to front.

Hunger is slow, broad, patient. It grows over hours. It welcomes plain food. It closes when fed. The afternoon tug is narrow, sudden, picky. It wants one taste only. It scorns bread and soup. It nags till fed sweet, quiets for minutes, then nags louder. That is not the body asking for fuel. That is the last dose asking for company.

See the difference once and you cannot unsee it.

Real fuel satisfies and holds the mind steady for hours. The sweet dose holds the mind on itself for minutes. Real fuel leaves you clear. The sweet dose leaves you coated and hunting. Put them side by side in your own day and judge by what each does for you, not by what fear says about harm.

Has a plain meal ever left you pacing the kitchen an hour later for the same plain meal again? Has water ever called you back shaking for more water to stay upright? Has fruit eaten hungry ever fogged you worse than hunger did? Your body answered those questions daily while the mind kept blaming tiredness.

The lift is short because it was never built to carry. The sag is long because the lift was borrowed against it.

Learn the two hungers by their manners and you will never confuse them again. Broad hunger arrives without drama. The stomach empties. The mind turns to food without naming a brand. Bread sounds good. Soup sounds good. Fruit sounds good. Eat, and the signal closes for hours. The mind forgets food and returns to life. Narrow tug arrives with drama. The mouth waters for one thing. The mind names the packet, the shelf, the drawer. Bread sounds dull. Soup sounds wrong. Fruit sounds like a joke. Feed it sweet, and it quiets for minutes, then returns louder, pickier, more urgent. That return is the giveaway. Real need, met, goes quiet. False need, met, comes back stronger.

We called both by one name and so misread both. We said "I am hungry" at four when dinner was two hours off and the body had been fed sweet all day. We were not hungry. We were dipping from the last spike. The stomach was full without being fed. The tongue was coated. The head was loud. That loudness was not the body calling for fuel. It was the last hit calling for the next.

Test it the way you tested the desk. On a plain day, wait through the four o'clock tug with water and air and see what it does. It peaks, it nags, it passes in under an hour, and dinner tastes brighter for the wait. On a sweet day, feed the four o'clock tug with sweet and see what it does. It quiets for ten minutes, it returns at five, it drags you to the kitchen at six, it hunts at ten. Which response looks like fuel met and which looks like a loan renewed?

If sweet fuelled, why does fed sweet call for more sweet within the hour? If plain food is dull, why does plain food, eaten hungry, close the signal for hours? If the tug proves need, why does need, met with sweet, grow louder rather than quieter?

Notice too how quickly the tongue learns to lie. The first square of chocolate in the morning still tastes of something. The third square at three tastes of hurry. The fifth at four tastes of coating and thirst and a faint nausea under the sweet. If this were fuel, would more taste less? If this were enjoyment, would enjoyment thin as the day thickens? The thinning is the body telling the truth while the mind repeats the old sentence. Listen to the tongue, not the sentence. The tongue tires. The body keeps score.

### EMPTY CALORIES THAT FEED NOTHING

There is a plainer truth under the lift, and it needs saying without frills.

BAD SUGAR is empty calories that nourish nothing. It carries no building material the body can use to keep you upright. No lasting fuel the muscles can draw on for hours. No quiet satisfaction the stomach can read as done. It arrives as a rush, it leaves as a sag, and in between it does no work of feeding at all.

I mean that exactly as I say it. A plain meal feeds. Bread, potatoes, vegetables, fruit, a full plate eaten hungry — those give the body matter to work with. They fill, they hold, they clear the head for hours. A sweet hit does none of that. It coats the tongue, it spikes the hour, it wakes the wanting, and it leaves the body still unfed beneath the noise. The stomach stays unclosed. The head stays loud. The limbs stay flat. You can take in hundreds of sweet calories by three and still feel unfuelled at four, because unfuelled is what you are. Calories counted, feeding not done.

We believed the sweet was concentrated power. Concentrated is true. Power is false. It is concentrated speed of arrival, not concentrated worth. It hits fast because it was engineered to hit fast, and speed was mistaken for strength. A spark is fast. A log is slow. Which heats the house through the night?

Your body already owned the steadiness the sweet claimed to lend. The blood knew how to hold. The breath knew how to steady. The nerves knew how to fire and rest. Plain food supports that work. Sweet noise interrupts it. Each hit borrows ten minutes of brightness against two hours of flatness, then calls the flatness proof you need another hit. Round and round. The roller coaster you named so well. "I want to get off the roller coaster," you said. You were right to want off. The coaster never travelled. It circled the drawer.

Look at the eater who lives without daily hits. Not a saint. Not a dieter. An ordinary eater who eats when hungry and stops when satisfied. Her energy does not spike and crash. It rolls. Morning clear, midday steady, three o'clock dip small and passing, evening calm. She does not think of fuel at three because fuel is not in question. Her body feeds from meals and returns to baseline between them. That baseline is yours too when the noise stops. You were born with it. No packet installed it. No packet can improve it.

If BAD SUGAR fed, why are its steadiest users the emptiest at four? If it gave power, why does power fade fastest where doses fall thickest? If it nourished, why does the nourished mouth hunt before dinner is cold?

There is a test you can run without changing anything yet. Eat a plain lunch hungry tomorrow — full, unhurried, till satisfied — and note the three o'clock hour. Note the size of the dip. Note how long it lasts. Note what it asks for. The next day eat the same lunch plus a sweet drink and a dessert that promises lift, and note the same hour. Note the ten bright minutes. Note the coated tongue. Note the four o'clock pull. Note the second reach. Your own two days will teach more than any paragraph. One day fed. One day whipped. Both lived by the same body, with the same sleep, the same work, the same weather. Only the dose differs. Watch who does the carrying.

We thought sweet was fuel because the low it ended felt like lack of fuel. Lack was real. Fuel was misnamed. The lack was the last dose wearing off. The ending of lack was the next dose muting it for minutes. No new power entered. Old steadiness was touched and lost. That is the back-to-front error that held us all: we credited the dose with giving what the dose had taken, and blamed the body for missing what the dose had stolen.

Ask what feeding ever looked like in your own life before the loop ran the show. A bowl of soup after cold air that warmed the hands and cleared the head till evening. Bread torn hungry that quieted the stomach for hours without a second thought. An apple eaten slow on a walk that left the mouth clean. No coating. No thirst that water could not clear. No hunt twenty minutes later. That is feeding. Hold the sweet afternoon beside it and judge by doing, not by promise. Which one fed and which one borrowed?

### WHAT THE LIFT COSTS WHILE IT PRETENDS TO GIVE

There is a heavier account behind the afternoon, and I will put it plain because you deserve plain.

The fact is, a heavy daily load of BAD SUGAR batters the heart and vessels over years. The liver is forced to carry what plain meals should have handled. Pressure is pushed high. A low fire of irritation stays lit in the tissues. Hunger signals are drowned till full cannot be heard. The lift you pay for by day is collected by night as broken sleep, by year as weight creeping round the middle, by decade as risk to heart and stroke.

I am talking about what heavy daily use does across crowds over years, not what yesterday's bar did to you. I tell you this to show the lift was never free of charge, and I do not want you acting from fright. Fear never freed anyone. Sight does. See the charge and the lift loses its glow without needing dread to push you.

Look at the state it leaves as a lived day, not as a warning poster.

Flat in the limbs by evening, though you sat most of the day. Foggy in the head by late afternoon, though the work was not hard. Never satisfied in the stomach, though the mouth has been busy since ten. Meals half enjoyed because the tongue is coated. Evenings half remembered because the sofa hunt fills them. Mornings begun already tired because the night was restless and sweet-broken. A day that leaves you flat, foggy and never satisfied — that is the permanent weather of the sweet loop, not the person you are.

You called that weather tiredness and blamed age, work, weather, character. We all did. We said the roller coaster was life. We said "I want to get off the roller coaster" while believing the coaster was carrying us somewhere.

It carries nowhere. It circles the desk, the drawer, the kitchen, the bin. Round and round. Lift, sag, reach. Lift, sag, reach. The ticket gets dearer and the ride gets duller and the ground never comes closer.

Count the cost the way you counted the minutes at the desk. Money slipped through the till for packets, bars, bottles. Time slipped through the head for dose-talk — when to have, how much is allowed, how to hide the wrapper, how to start again Monday. Attention slipped through the afternoon for fog. Sleep slipped through the night for restlessness and thirst. Teeth paid. Mood paid. The belt paid. Not in one dramatic bill, but in small daily deductions that never stopped while the lift kept promising it was helping.

If the sweet gave, why are its steadiest users the flattest people in the room? If it fuelled, why does the fuelled afternoon end earliest? If it satisfied, why does the satisfied mouth hunt before dinner is cold?

There is plenty it does to the body over years. There is nothing it does for your energy that your body did not already own.

Stand in the kitchen at dusk and see the weather from outside. The work bag by the door. The plates from dinner still warm. The light too bright after the soft living room. The cupboard half empty though the shop was only yesterday. The bin filling with bright wrappers though the mouth feels still unmet. The head full of plans to be good tomorrow though tomorrow has failed a hundred times. That kitchen is not proof you are broken. That kitchen is proof the lift charges by the hour and pays nothing it promises.

Take that kitchen and the desk and lay them end to end. Morning pinch, noon pinch, three o'clock pinch, dusk reckoning. One day looks small. Multiply it for the rest of your working life and watch the small deductions turn vast. Thousands of bars. Thousands of fogged afternoons. Thousands of evenings half remembered on the sofa. That is what the loan costs when it is renewed daily. And for what? Ten bright minutes that never rose above where you started. Once seen as a lifetime, the bargain insults sense. No helper charges that rent. Only a trap does.

### THE WANT CAN END WHOLE

"But the want will come back," you say. "Even if the lift is false, the tug is real. I have felt it shake my afternoon. If I walk out, I will spend life holding the drawer shut with both hands till one tired day I pull it open."

I understand that fear because we all stood at that drawer with both hands on it.

Hear me clear. The tug you feel is not a life sentence to be held shut. It is a short echo that thins when unfed. When daily sweet hits stop, most feel a dull, flat grumble for a few days — short temper, dull head, that tug in the afternoon and evening — then it passes. Days-long, small-scale, gone. Plain meals satisfy through it. Mornings clear through it. Work continues through it. No count kept. No stairs climbed.

Why did it feel endless before? Because two pains were added and both blamed on tissue. The first was the tiny echo, brief and light. The second was the big panic of loss: my treat gone, my fuel gone, how will afternoons work, how will evenings close. That second pain was never physical. That second pain was belief mourning an illusion. Keep the belief that BAD SUGAR carries you and every afternoon becomes a trial of holding out. Remove the belief and there is nothing large left to hold.

You do not face years of white-knuckle afternoons. You face a few flat days walked on full meals, with hunger and satisfaction doing their quiet work underneath. The body rights itself quickly when the mind stops ordering it to want. An eater who sees the lift for what it was forgets doses for weeks without effort, because the mind is no longer posting a guard on a treasure.

This is why dread is the wrong uniform for this walk. Dread belongs to the method that kept you trapped, the method that told you afternoons would be grey and evenings would be empty and you must be hard to survive them. That method lied. The afternoon was grey because of the dose, not without it. The evening was restless because of the hunt, not without it. Take the tight shoes off and the feet do not need courage to stand. They need only ground.

You came to this chapter forgiven and investigating. Stay investigating for one hour more. Watch the next lift as a detective watches, not as a mourner watches. Note the ten minutes. Note the fog. Note the second reach. Note who did the work while the dose took the bow. Your legs carried you to the meeting. Your mind solved the problem before the wrapper opened. Your breath steadied you before the sip. It was you that carried the afternoon, not the dose.

BAD SUGAR takes the energy it claims to lend.

Think of tomorrow at three without changing anything yet. The dip will come, small and human. The drawer will call, loud and trained. You will know both for what they are. One is the body moving through a day. The other is yesterday's spike asking for company. Let them sit side by side in daylight and see which one needs answering. The dip passes with water, air, movement, minutes. The call passes too when seen, because seen-through calling has no weight behind it.

If the want were permanent, why does it thin in days for most who stop daily hits? If holding shut were needed, why do eaters who see clear forget doses without effort for weeks? If the tug proved need, why does need, left unfed on full meals, go quiet rather than grow?

You are not facing a daily holding action with no end. You are facing an ending that stays ended once the fuel belief ends. The want ends whole. No guard posted. No drawer held. No count. The afternoon returns to what it always was before the first con: hours to work through, a small dip to walk through, dinner to meet hungry.

That is why this chapter closes with a command about mood, not about sweets. Mood decides what the eyes can see. Dread narrows the eyes to loss. Elation opens them to sight. You have lost nothing to mourn. You have seen a lift for a loan. Begin the rest of this reading the way you would begin walking home in shoes that fit — head up, pace steady, glad the pinch is understood.

5. BEGIN WITH ELATION, NOT DREAD
You are escaping, not losing.
