### THE LAST REASON YOU THINK YOU HAVE

We have taken the ladder rung by rung.

We took the lift. We saw it was not a lift. We took the reward. We saw it was a push that called itself love. You are steadier for it. Keep that steadiness. We need it now.

Because now we come to the reason that feels most innocent. Most obvious. Most beyond argument.

You say it warmly. Defiantly. Even tenderly. I will ventriloquize it exactly as you say it, in your dialect, so you feel known before you feel freed.

"But I love the taste."

"I just crave sweets and when i eat them, i feel like i cant stop."

Do you hear how final that sounds? Do you hear the shrug inside it? As if all the demolition in the last two chapters was clever, but this one cannot be touched because the tongue cannot lie?

I understand. We all said it. I said it. For years I would have sworn on my life that I loved the taste of BAD SUGAR itself. Not the energy. Not the reward. The taste. Plain. Simple. Irreducible. Take everything else away, I would have said, and I would still want that taste on my tongue.

And I want to be warm to you here before I am ruthless to the trap. There is nothing wrong with loving taste. Taste is one of the great joys of being a human animal. Your body is built to delight in flavour. That is good. That is Nature's Guide speaking, not the Sugar Story. I do not moralize sweetness. I do not tell you food should be dull. I want you to enjoy more taste, not less.

So let me ask you, with total friendliness, the question that undoes the whole thing.

Do you love the taste? Or do you love the taste of the moment BAD SUGAR was sneaking a ride on?

The fact is, this belief collapses the moment we isolate the variable. And you have already done the experiment without knowing you did it.

Do you trust your tongue enough to test it?

Of course you do. You have nothing to lose and everything to gain by looking.

### THE TEST YOU HAVE ALREADY DONE ON YOURSELF

Let us isolate the variable. That is the engine of every demolition in this book. We take the cherished scene, we strip BAD SUGAR out, and we see what pleasure remains. The pleasure that remains was never its to claim.

Start where you live. The morning coffee. The afternoon tea. The grapefruit at breakfast. The porridge. The plain yogurt.

Ask yourself, plainly:

Do you take coffee black and unsweetened because you love its taste? Or do you add BAD SUGAR precisely because, without it, you do not like the taste?

There is only one honest answer.

You add sugar to hide the taste. You bury the bitter under sweet. You mask it. You coat it. If you loved the taste of sugar itself, why would you need the coffee at all? Why not spoon it dry from the bag? Why does nobody sit with a bowl of white granules and call it delicious?

Do you see the self-incriminating evidence? Your own behaviour disproves your stated reason. The thing you claim to love for its taste is the thing you use to make other tastes tolerable.

Try the next variable.

Take fruit. A ripe peach. A mango. Berries still warm from the shop. Does that need BAD SUGAR to be delicious? Does it need anything? Or does it taste exactly like itself, complete, clean, finished?

Now take confectionery. Take the biscuit that is mostly flour and fat and sugar, engineered to spike and crave. Eat it slowly. Really taste it. Not the idea of it. The substance on the tongue after ten seconds. After thirty. Is it a complex flavour you admire the way you admire peach? Or is it a single, loud, sweet hit that blots out everything else and leaves you wanting water?

Ask yourself:

If BAD SUGAR has a beautiful taste, why does it need chocolate, caramel, vanilla, salt, peanut butter, crunch, chew, colour and a cartoon tiger to be wanted?

And if BAD SUGAR has a beautiful taste, why does more of it not taste more beautiful — why does the fourth biscuit not taste better than the first?

There is only one honest answer to both.

It was never about a genuine pleasure, treat or lift of flavour. It was about a hit that sneaks a ride on flavours you already loved, or that hides flavours you did not.

Short. Plain. True.

You hid the taste. You did not love it.

### WHY YOU ADD SUGAR TO HIDE THE TASTE

Let me give you an original image so you feel this in your body, not just your head.

Imagine you have a beautiful wooden table. The grain is warm. The colour is alive. Someone tells you the table is not beautiful enough. They hand you a can of thick, sweet, white gloss paint. They say, paint it. You paint it. Now it is shiny. It is loud. It is sticky to touch. Can you still see the wood?

No. You buried it.

That is what BAD SUGAR does to taste.

Good food has taste already. Real bread. Nuts. Fruit. A stew. Cheese. Vegetables picked and cooked simply. They have subtlety, bitterness, sourness, sweetness in balance, texture, aroma. BAD SUGAR is the gloss paint. You pour it over the table and then you say you love the wood because you love the gloss.

Do you love the wood? Or do you love that you cannot see it anymore?

Think of the foods you claim you could not enjoy without BAD SUGAR.

Coffee without sugar is honest. It is bitter, aromatic, adult. Many people who never sweeten it love it precisely because it is not sweet. You sweetened it because you had been taught that bitter is wrong.

Grapefruit without sugar is bright, sharp, alive. You sugared it because sharpness had been taught as a flaw, not a note.

Porridge without sugar is nutty, warm, clean. You sugared it because plain had been taught as punishment, not as baseline.

Do you see who taught you?

We did not decide, as clear-eyed adults, that food is not food unless it is sweet. We were shown. Early. Repeatedly. At every birthday, every till, every advert that showed happiness pouring as chocolate sauce. We learned that sweetness equals goodness, and then we pasted it over every flavour until we forgot what the flavour was.

Remember the House-Party Gatecrasher — that loud sugared guest claiming your party's warmth was his?

Now ask yourself, trap question to trap question, until only one honest answer remains:

If you love the taste of BAD SUGAR, why do you only love it when it rides on something you already love — chocolate, cake, ice cream, biscuit — and never by itself, cold, by the spoonful?

And if you love the taste of BAD SUGAR, why do you add it to hide tastes you claim to love, instead of tasting those foods as nature packaged them?

There is only one honest answer.

You do not love the taste of BAD SUGAR. You love the flavour of the occasion. BAD SUGAR only smeared itself across it and sent you the bill.

Here is the killer pair. Read it slowly.

You did not sweeten food because you loved food. You sweetened food because you were taught not to trust it.

It's the other way around.

And because it is the other way around, there is no sacrifice in escaping. Your body makes its own steady glucose from protein and glycerol, quietly, around the clock, without any need for refined sugar imports. You are not depriving yourself of fuel when you stop BAD SUGAR. You are returning to a clean baseline that was already feeding you. That is not my opinion. That is how your body is built. Nature's Guide is sufficient. The gloss was never nutrition. It was whitewash.

### WANTING IS NOT LIKING

Now we must answer the voice that says, "That all sounds clever, but I still crave it. The craving proves the pleasure. I just crave sweets and when i eat them i feel like i cant stop. If I did not love the taste, why would I want it so badly?"

I want to answer this with total honesty, with no hedging about the core reframe and no overclaiming about science. I will tell you what is known, what is not, and what it means for you.

The fact is, each bite of BAD SUGAR can trigger your own reward circuitry. A nibble fires dopamine in the nucleus accumbens and re-primes wanting. That is a real, measured response. It explains why one bite becomes the box. It explains why "once I start I can't stop" feels mechanically true in that moment. The bite restarts the wanting.

But here is the distinction that frees you. And it is critical.

Dopamine is not pleasure. Wanting is not liking.

Your brain can fire wanting for something you do not even like while you are eating it. That gap is not speculation. It is the lived shape you already know. You reach. You eat. You enjoy less than you expected. You still reach again. Need rises while joy falls. The packet calls before the body asks.

Do you know that shape?

Of course you do. We all do. That is the signature of the Sugar Trap.

Now, I will keep the boundary you are owed. Much of the detailed dopamine work comes from animals, and the magnitude is smaller than with drugs of abuse. Human evidence is debated. A serious Cambridge perspective argues that bingeing reflects the schedule of intermittent access — the restriction-binge loop itself — more than a single food chemical, and that "sugar is addictive" remains an open question, not a consensus. Both camps agree the wanting/liking gap is real. Both camps agree intermittent access manufactures loss of control.

So I do not need the addiction label to prove the trap. And you do not need it to be free.

Whether you call it addiction or habit or brainwashing, the result is the same: the bite re-triggers wanting without proving pleasure. The craving proves the trap, not the joy.

Let me show you with cold logic.

If craving proved genuine pleasure, would you crave more the less you enjoy it?

If craving proved love of taste, would the smell from the kitchen trigger you before the taste touches your tongue?

There is only one honest answer to both.

You are not chasing taste. You are chasing relief from a wanting that the last hit created. That is not a genuine pleasure, treat or lift. That is a faint, empty tug that you were taught to read as love.

Ask yourself, flatly:

When you finished the box after that first sliver, did you think, that was more delicious with each bite?

Or did you think, why did I keep going when it stopped being delicious after the second one?

There is only one honest answer.

You kept going because wanting outran liking. The trap made you want what you no longer liked. That is not taste. That is a con.

Short. Under eight words. Feel it.

Wanting rose. Liking fell.

Cue fired. Tongue did not.

### THE SMELL THAT NON-USERS DON'T CRAVE

Now isolate the final variable. The one that seems to prove the tongue beyond all doubt.

The smell.

The bakery on the cold morning. The biscuit tin opening at 3pm. The chocolate wrapper crackled on the sofa. You smell it and your mouth waters. You think, my body knows what it loves. My tongue knows.

Does it? Or have you been taught to read that smell as love?

The fact is, non-users do not crave the smell at all. That is the hands-on proof that perception can be flatly wrong. Ask a person who has not fed the Sugar Story for years — not through willpower, but because they see it clearly — to stand with you at the bakery door. You inhale and feel a tug. They inhale and smell... warm. Sweet. Heavy. Perhaps cloying. Perhaps nothing much at all. No tug. No need-to-eat-something-RIGHT-NOW. No story.

Same air. Same molecules. Two completely different experiences.

So where was the pleasure?

Was it in the sugar molecules? Or was it in the Story that your brain had been fed about what those molecules mean?

There is only one honest answer.

If BAD SUGAR had an inherently irresistible taste, everyone would crave the smell equally, the way everyone pulls a hand from a flame. But they do not. Children who have not been trained on sugared cereal do not beg for black coffee. Adults who never sweeten their tea do not feel deprived without it. People who eat fruit as nature packaged it do not feel they are missing flavour. Only the trained crave the cue. That is brainwashing, not taste.

Do you remember the fear we named in the last chapter — that without BAD SUGAR you would lose sweetness itself? I want to relieve you now, gently, with what your body already knows.

You will not lose sweetness. You will taste more.

When you stop painting the table, the grain comes back. Fruit that seemed boring begins to taste bright again, because your palate is no longer blotted with loud gloss. Water tastes clean. A peach tastes like a peach. You do not need to add sweetness to love taste. You need to stop smothering it.

Ask yourself, future trap question, and answer honestly inside:

When you imagine a free life, do you imagine a life with less flavour?

Or do you imagine food tasting exactly like itself again, steady, clean, enjoyed without the spike and the tired, wired and crashing that followed?

There is only one honest answer.

You imagine more flavour. Not less. Because there is more flavour on nature's side of the line than on the trap's.

Short. True. Held.

Smell proved the Story. Not the taste.

Air was same. Story was not.

### IT WAS SNEAKING A RIDE ON FLAVOURS YOU ALREADY LOVED

Let me bring us to the landing. Let me be warm to you and harsh to the trap, and final for this belief.

We entered saying: I love the taste. I just crave sweets and when i eat them i feel like i cant stop.

We have dissected the sensory test you had already performed without noticing.

We saw that you add BAD SUGAR to hide the taste, not to reveal it. We saw that you never eat it alone, by the spoon, for its own beauty, but only when it rides on chocolate, cake, biscuit, ice cream — flavours you already loved. We saw that your body needs no sugar import to be steady, that the gloss was never fuel. We saw that wanting can climb while liking falls, that the bite re-primes wanting via your own circuitry without proving pleasure — and that the science is honest about its limits, so we do not need a label to see the con. We saw that non-users do not crave the smell at all, which proves the tug was trained, not inherent.

So finish the reassignment.

If BAD SUGAR gave you a genuine pleasure, treat or lift of taste, would it need to hide behind every other taste to be loved?

If BAD SUGAR loved you through taste, would that love leave you tired, wired and crashing after?

If BAD SUGAR were about taste, would one bite not be enough to admire, instead of a box you do not even remember tasting after the second piece?

There is only one honest answer. You know it.

You never loved the taste of BAD SUGAR. You loved the flavours it gatecrashed. You loved the peach. You loved the coffee's ritual. You loved the cinema, the laugh, the pause. BAD SUGAR smeared loud sweetness across them and claimed the credit, the way gloss claims the wood.

It's the other way around.

You do not love sweets because they taste good. You think they taste good because you have been told you love them.

Do you feel the release in that?

You have nothing to lose. The flavour remains.

Think of the next week. You walk past the aisle. A wrapper crackles. The old Story fires, "taste, treat, you love this." You do not argue. You do not suppress. You see it plainly now. You think: ah, that is wanting, not liking. That is cue, not tongue. That is the Story, not me. And you feel relief, not deprivation, because the thing you thought you loved never had a taste worth missing.

Here is the liberating inversion for this chapter. Say it inside and feel freedom, not sacrifice:

You do not need BAD SUGAR to taste sweetness. You need to stop BAD SUGAR to taste anything at all.

It's the other way around.

Let me land the peak verdict in the simplest line this book can give you for this lie.

THE TASTE WAS NOT THE SUGAR. THE SUGAR HID THE TASTE.

Short. Short again.

You loved the occasion. Not the gloss.

And because the occasion stays, the whole leg of a genuine pleasure, treat or lift that wore the taste costume falls with it. We do not take flavour from you. We return flavour to its owners — the food, the fruit, your tongue, your moment. The hit was sneaking a ride. You have caught it.

Carry that lightness into the next chapter. The next lie says it calms you and it is sociable — that sugar comforts stress and glues people together. We will meet that one where it feels most kind, and you will see it causes the very tension it claims to soothe.
