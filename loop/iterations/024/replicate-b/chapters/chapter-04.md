Chapter 4
THE LIFT THAT DROPS YOU

**IN THIS CHAPTER**
- Fluorescent afternoon and heavy eyes at the desk
- The drawer biscuit and the bright ten minutes
- The long slide to a foggy evening

*BAD SUGAR never gives you energy — it takes it from you and calls the crash a lift.*

### THREE O'CLOCK AT THE DESK

We all know that hour.

The light is flat and fluorescent. Your eyes go heavy. Your head goes thick. The work in front of you blurs at the edges. And the drawer slides open almost by itself.

“I just need sugary somethings to get me through.”

“I need something RIGHT NOW or I cannot think.”

I know those sentences because I lived them. We have all lived them. The biscuit. The bar. The bright can hissing open. Taken not for joy, but as fuel. A shot of sweet to keep the afternoon alive.

And for ten bright minutes it seems to work. The fog thins. The hands move faster. You breathe out and think, there, that helped.

Watch what happens next. That is where the truth lives.

By half past three the brightness thins. By four the heaviness is back, a little deeper than before, and your hand is back in the drawer staring at wrappers. By five you are snappy and dull at once. By evening you are flat on the sofa, foggy and heavy, promising to be good tomorrow.

Those who no longer do it tell the same story about that afternoon top-up. It gave a short lift which was inevitably followed by a fast drop. Not once in a while. Every time. The lift and the drop belong together.

Did the dose fail to work at four o’clock? No. It worked exactly as it always works.

### DON’T TRUST THE TEN MINUTES

Let me ask you plainly, as one afternoon user to another.

When did a fix ever leave you brighter an hour later than a free non-user feels all the time? If that biscuit truly gave you energy, why did you need another one by four? If it truly cleared your head, why the fog and the flatness by evening?

You cannot answer that and still defend the lift. The only honest answer concedes it.

The fact is... the same surge that lifts you can drag you down after. The bright ten minutes are the surge at work, and the surge carries its own after-dip with it. This is not a medical label and I place no diagnosis on you — if you live with diabetes or take medicines that touch blood sugar, keep your clinician in charge of that side.

Your everyday afternoon slump is simpler and closer than any label. We take a dose. We rise a fraction out of the hole the last dose dug. We sink again, a little lower. Then we mistake the next rise for help.

That drawer biscuit was never a genuine pleasure, treat or lift.

Do you see the trick? We judge by the last fix. We remember the ten bright minutes and forget the three dull hours that paid for them. Judge by the whole day and the con falls apart.

Think of loving the second you loosen tight shoes and forgetting you chose to wear them tight all day. The relief feels real. The gift is not.

By evening the price is written all over you. Heavy limbs. Dull head. No spark for the people you love. That is not your character and it is not your age and it is not a hard day. That is an afternoon of doses collecting their debt, leaving you tired, run-down and foggy — not because life tired you, but because the lift dropped you.

It never lifted you — it dropped you.

5. NEVER JUDGE BY THE LAST FIX.
The lift lies; watch the whole day.

**SUMMARY**
- The bright minutes after a sweet fix are the start of a drop.
- Hunger for another dose by late afternoon proves the last one took energy.
- The evening fog belongs to the doses, not to weakness or the day.
- The whole curve tells the truth that the last ten minutes hide.
