Chapter 12
ORDINARY PEOPLE WALK OUT

**IN THIS CHAPTER**
- A desk drawer at half past three, remembered from the other side
- A night kitchen with a biscuit box on the lap
- A plain morning kitchen, hungry and unhurried
- One tired sweet-lover telling how the door opened

*People exactly like you have already walked out, finding it simple and joyful, and there is nothing special about them that you lack.*

### THE QUIET OBJECTION

You have come a long way with me.

You stood in that sunny garden and measured the tables with your own fingers. You saw the cake take credit for sunshine it never made. Something in you loosened. And still, underneath, a small voice whispers the last defence. I know it because I said it myself for years.

"All right for others. Not for me."

"You do not know how I am in the evening. You do not know how many Mondays I have ruined by Wednesday. I am the relapser. I am the one who finishes the packet once it is open. I am the tired one."

I hear you, and I am not going to scold you for thinking it. That is not weakness talking. That is discouragement, and discouragement is honest. You have been fooled so many times by promises that left you heavier that you protect yourself by expecting nothing.

So let me do what argument cannot do. Let me introduce you to someone.

Not a saint. Not a guru. Not a person with a perfect childhood and a perfect kitchen and endless free time. A grazer and topper-upper and midnight picker, just like us. If you see yourself in her, you will have to ask a different question — not whether freedom is real, but whether you will keep pretending it is reserved for other people.

### I WAS YOU

I am going to tell you plainly how it was with me, because I was you.

For seventeen years I lived on sweet fixes. I do not mean I liked something sweet after dinner. I mean I arranged my day around it. Coffee with three sugars at eight. A soft drink at eleven, drunk without thinking while I worked. At half past three at my desk under the buzzing light, my eyes went heavy and my head went thick, and I reached into the drawer for biscuits — always three, never one, eaten standing half the time, crumbs on the keyboard. I told myself it got me through.

Evenings were worse. I cooked a proper meal, ate it, felt full, and at twenty past eleven I was in the kitchen in my socks, quiet so no one would hear, cutting "just a sliver" of cake or opening "just a handful" of biscuits and taking the box to the sofa. I never finished the sliver. I finished the box. Then I sat with that sour, buzzing, slightly sick feeling and promised tomorrow would be different. Tomorrow was the same kitchen, the same socks, the same promise.

I tried every Monday system you have tried. No sweets in the house — so I bought them "for the kids" and ate them in the car. Only at weekends — so Saturday became a two-day fog. Only dark chocolate, only honey, only fruit juice, only small bars. Each rule made the next fix brighter in my head and larger in my hands. Each failure made me more certain the fault was in me.

I was not a careless eater in other ways. I loved real meals. I loved cooking. But hunger frightened me. I had forgotten what it felt like to be truly hungry and then truly satisfied. I topped up all day so I never arrived at a meal with an honest appetite, then blamed my appetite for being broken.

You know the roller coaster I lived on. Sweet to lift the morning. Sweet to push through the afternoon. Sweet to pay myself for surviving the day. Sweet to soften the night. I need something RIGHT NOW — that was my sentence, ten times a day, said with my hand already in the packet.

What finally changed me was not a shock and not a lecture. A friend who had walked out lent me this way of seeing, and I read it the way you are reading now — still using, still sceptical, still half-hoping it would fail quickly so I could go back to not trying.

I remember the night I understood the lift. I had eaten a big sticky bun at four o'clock because my concentration had gone. For twenty minutes I felt brighter. By five I was duller than before the bun, thirsty, snappy, reaching again. I watched the whole curve that day instead of judging by the first twenty minutes. The bun had not carried me. It had borrowed brightness and charged me fog for it.

I remember the cinema evening that undid the reward story for me. I had told myself for years that chocolate was how I loved a film, how I rested after being good all week. That night I went with my sister, laughed till my face hurt, ate a proper meal before, and left the chocolate in my bag untouched without deciding to. I had a wonderful night. Walking home I thought, who loved me tonight — my sister and the story, or the bar in my bag? The bar did nothing and the night lost nothing. It was the other way around. I had loved the bar because of the night.

The physical side surprised me most, because I had feared it most. I had expected headaches for weeks, shaking hands, misery. What came was smaller and stranger. On the second afternoon without my drawer biscuits there was a faint flatness, a little restlessness in my hands around half past three. Not pain. Not hunger. Just a sense that something usually happened now and was not happening. I drank water, ate lunch properly hungry, went back to work. In an hour it had passed. On the third evening came my wobble, and I will not hide it from you.

We had friends round. Everyone stayed late. Cake came out with coffee, beautiful and glossy, and for a minute the old voice spoke loudly, "Just a treat. Once is fine. You have been good." My mouth watered. My hand half-moved. I felt left out and silly and tired all at once. I did not argue with the voice or push it away. I looked at it. I asked, what has that slice ever done for me but start the clock again? I remembered the socks in the kitchen at night, the fog at five, the drawer crumbs. I had one coffee, black, and talked, and laughed, and ten minutes later the voice had gone quiet, almost embarrassed to have spoken. I walked home clear-headed, and I lay in bed marvelling that I felt neither deprived nor heroic — just uninterested, the way you feel about a film that does not interest you.

After that, stability came quickly and quietly. The mornings changed first. I woke hungry — truly hungry, stomach awake — and ate eggs and fruit slowly till I was satisfied, and the food tasted brighter than I remembered. That heavy three o'clock slump at the desk under the buzzing light, the one that used to send my hand to the drawer, simply stopped arriving. I waited for it the first week, braced for it the second, then forgot to wait. My afternoons felt even, my evenings felt calm, my kitchen at night went silent. No counting days. No white-knuckling past shop windows. I walked down the bright aisle in the supermarket and felt what you feel when you see a crowd queuing for something you no longer want — a flicker of pity, then nothing, then on with enjoying your life.

I tell you numbers so you know I am not painting mist. Seventeen years caught. Three biscuits at the desk most workdays. Two sweet drinks a day. Eleven o'clock kitchen visits four or five nights a week. Three weeks of noticing old thoughts grow fainter. Then months of ordinary living where BAD SUGAR crossed my mind less and less, and each time it did, the thought brought relief that I no longer do it. No drama. No replacement system. No void to fill. Just meals when hungry, stopping when satisfied, and a head that no longer bargains.

Was I stronger than you? Ask yourself honestly.

Did I love sweet fixes less than you, so it was easier for me to leave them? You know I loved them the way you do — for fuel, for comfort, for celebration, for love.
Did I have less stress, fewer children, kinder evenings, so temptation never touched me? I had deadlines and bills and tired nights and people I adore and argue with, like you.
Or did I simply see, at last, that the fixes never did anything for me, and find there was nothing left to miss?

There is only one honest answer. And it leaves you nowhere to hide.

If a relapsing, box-finishing, drawer-hiding grazer like me walked out and stayed out, finding ordinary days brighter, what makes you the exception?

### WHAT MY STORY MEANS FOR YOU

I am not telling you this to impress you. I am telling you so you cannot keep the story of you as the one it never works for.

We all fell in the same way, hiding packets and blaming ourselves. We all told ourselves the same lines about getting through and deserving one and managing to be good. We all feared life would be duller without something sweet. And some of us — tired, binging, relapsing, ordinary us — have walked out and found the dullness was in the trap, never in freedom.

You do not need a stronger character. You need a clearer eye.

Look at your own evidence. Have you ever had a joyful meal, a laughing evening, a bright morning with no dose in sight and felt full? Of course you have. Have you ever had the dose without the joy — alone, rushed, foggy — and felt empty after? Of course you have. The joy travels with life, never with the packet. My story only repeats what your life already told you.

So when that voice says, "Easy for her," answer it with what you now know. She was not different. The trap was the same. The seeing was the difference. And seeing is available to you right now.

You are not the one it fails for. You are the one it was written for.

**SUMMARY**
- Easy escape is not reserved for strong or special people.
- One ordinary grazer lived your exact loop for years and walked out.
- The drawer fixes and night boxes gave nothing and charged fog for the illusion.
- A brief wobble on the third evening faded in minutes and never returned as a command.
- That heavy afternoon slump at the desk stopped arriving once the loop stopped.
- If freedom held for the relapsing and the tired, it holds for you.
