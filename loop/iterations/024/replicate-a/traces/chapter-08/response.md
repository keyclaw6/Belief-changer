Chapter 8
THE OPEN DOOR

**IN THIS CHAPTER**
A stone cell with the iron door standing open.
Hesitating feet at the bright threshold.
Two ropes pulling opposite ways, held by the same hands.

*Both fears come from the same trap, so letting go of fear is letting go of the trap.*

The Feet That Will Not Move

We have all known that moment in dreams. A prison cell, grey and cold, the smell of old blankets. And the door — impossibly — standing open. Daylight falling across the stone in a clean bar. No guard. No chain. You can walk out.

And the feet will not move.

I have stood with so many good, sensible, strong-minded people at exactly that doorway with BAD SUGAR. They can see the cell clearly now. The daily grazing. The drawer fix at four o’clock. The evening picking with the packet on the lap. They have seen the tiny dip for what it is and the loud story for what it is. And still they hover.

It makes complete sense that you feel torn. It would be strange if you did not. You have lived in that cell a long time. The light outside looks wonderful, and it also looks wide and unknown. Of course the heart beats faster at the threshold.

Let me stay beside you here, without a flicker of judgment, until the feet understand what the eyes already know.

There is nothing wrong with you for feeling fear. Fear at an open door does not mean you love the cell. It means you have been locked in so long that freedom feels unfamiliar.

Afraid You Will Fail

First rope. Listen to it in your own voice:

“What if I fail again, like every January? What if I want it tomorrow and I cave and feel more ashamed than before?”

“I’ve managed to be good this week. Why risk spoiling it by claiming I am free?”

We know that voice well. We have all tried rules and Mondays and being good. We have all stood in the night kitchen after a perfect day and watched a permitted sliver turn into the empty box. We carry those memories in our bodies.

So the mind says: do not promise what you cannot deliver. Stay where you are. At least here you know the shape of the disappointment.

I understand that tiredness. You are not weak for feeling it. You are a person who has been brave many times with the wrong map and been blamed for getting lost.

But look honestly at what that fear is really saying.

If BAD SUGAR were a genuine friend, why would the thought of keeping it for life frighten you? And if freedom were a loss, why would the thought of failing to get free frighten you more?

Ask yourself:

When you picture yourself still hunting for a hit every afternoon for the rest of your life, do you feel comforted or chilled?

Has a lifetime of fixes ever once left you feeling steady and complete, or has it always left you reaching again?

You know the answer. That cold feeling when you picture the years stretching ahead, packet after packet, foggy evening after foggy evening — that is not a warning against escape. That is the cell telling you what staying costs.

The fear of failing is not proof you will fail. It is proof you can already see the price of staying.

Afraid It Will Work

Second rope. It pulls the other way, quieter, almost embarrassed:

“But what will mornings be without something sweet? Will life be duller if it works?”

“just a treat. once a week is fine. Surely a sunny morning with people I love needs a beautiful cake to make it complete?”

We have all whispered that. We were taught early that sweet means love, that pudding means celebration, that comfort comes in bright wrapping. To imagine a birthday, a cinema evening, a slow café morning with no dose feels, for a second, like imagining the colour taken out.

I hear the tenderness in that fear. You are not greedy. You are afraid of losing warmth.

So let us test it gently, not with argument but with your own life.

Think of the sweetest moment you can remember — unhurried, loved company, time to spare. Was the warmth in the sugar on your tongue, or in the sun on your face, the laughter, the freedom to linger?

If the dose made the moment, why did the moment always need another dose an hour later to keep it going?

If life without a fix is so grey, why do you feel greyest in the hour after the fix?

There is only one honest answer. The occasion supplied every drop of pleasure. The dose was only ever sneaking a ride on the sun and the company and the leisure. Take the dose away and the sun still warms. Leave the dose in and the sun gets eaten while you are busy unwrapping.

The fear of succeeding is not proof life will be duller. It is proof you were conned into crediting the wrong thing for the joy.

Both Ropes In The Same Hands

Now stand back and look at your hands.

One rope says: you will fail, so do not move. The other rope says: you will succeed into dullness, so do not move. One shames you for the past. One robs you of the future. Both arrive at the same order: stay in the cell.

Do two enemies pull you toward the same cell? Or does one jailer hold both ropes?

This is the tug-of-war of fear.

Feel it as lived experience for a moment. Left hand straining with “I can’t do it.” Right hand straining with “I don’t want a life without it.” Shoulders aching. Breath shallow. And behind you, in the shadows of the cell, the same quiet hands holding both ends, letting you exhaust yourself so you never look round.

That shouting voice is the Big Sweet Con.

It fed the tiny dip with a huge story for years, and now it feeds your honesty with fear. It tells the failing story to keep you hopeless and the succeeding story to keep you wistful, and either way you stay put. The dip is already fading on its own while you get on with enjoying your life. The ropes are not made of need. They are made of talk.

See whose hands hold them and the ache leaves your arms.

You are not torn between two truths. You are held by one lie pulling both ways. Let the ropes fall and there is no war left, only an open door and daylight.

There is no decision left to agonise over. There is only a trick seen through.

8. NEVER DOUBT THE DECISION.
Doubt is the Trap talking, not you.

From this moment, when the old talk starts — “take one to prove you are free,” “have a small one because you have been good,” “you need something RIGHT NOW” — you do not argue and you do not obey. You smile because you recognise the jailer’s voice trying on your voice. You let the thought pass like a cloud while you stay clear and bright and free for the rest of your life.

You do not need fear to push you out. I do not want you leaving because the cell frightens you. I want you leaving because you see there was never anything in the cell you wanted. The relief is already here, marvellous and quiet: no more counting, no more hiding wrappers, no more bargaining with slivers. Just ordinary days, bright mornings, food enjoyed to satisfaction, evenings that belong to you.

Walk out now, not braced but rejoicing. The door was never locked. The war was never yours.

**SUMMARY**
- Hovering at the open door does not mean you love the cell or lack strength.
- Dreading another failure points to the cost of staying, not the danger of leaving.
- Picturing life as duller without sweet credits the dose for joy the moment supplied.
- Both fears pull toward staying because both are held by the same trap.
- Seeing one holder behind both ropes ends the pull and leaves daylight.
