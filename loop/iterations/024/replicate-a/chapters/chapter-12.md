Chapter 12
SOMEONE EXACTLY LIKE YOU

**IN THIS CHAPTER**
A rain-grey office drawer that no longer calls.
A car glovebox full of foil and crumbs.
A plain Tuesday morning, bright and untroubled.

*If a tired relapser like me walked out and stayed out, you can walk out too.*

YOU HAVE MEASURED THE GARDEN

You have measured the garden morning and seen where the joy lived.

It lived in sun and leisure and company. It never lived in the cake. You saw the tables with your own hands and laughed at how feeling swore what measuring proved false. I do not take that from you. No one can take it back.

And yet I hear the quiet thought that comes after the clearest seeing:

"All very well for other people. But I am different. I love it more. I fail more. It will not work for me."

I know that thought. I lived inside it for years. Let me answer it the only way that ever answers it — not with argument, but with a life like yours, told true.

Listen to me as one reader to one reader. I was you.

LET ME TELL YOU WHO I WAS

I was not a moderate sweet eater who dabbled. I was a grazer, a topper-upper, a secret finisher.

Seventeen years I ate BAD SUGAR every single day. Not one day clean in seventeen years. Morning cereal drowned in sweetness. Tea with three spoons. Mid-morning biscuit to get through. Lunch with a fizzy drink and something from the packet wall. Afternoon biscuits from the drawer. Evening chocolate on the sofa. Night spoon in the jar standing up in cold light.

My office drawer held six bars at a time. My car glovebox held foil and crumbs and sticky coins. My bag held emergency sweets for the low moments. I told myself I needed sugary somethings to get me through. I told myself I had earned a treat when I felt I deserved one. I told myself once a week is fine while I ate it daily.

I knew every diet language by heart. I have managed to be good this week. I need something RIGHT NOW. I don't keep it in the house — while I kept it in the house, in the car, in the drawer, in the coat pocket. Just a treat. Just this once. Just to concentrate.

I failed the way you have failed. Monday rules broken by Tuesday lunch. Forbidden foods becoming precious. The evening creep-back after a perfect day. The quiet arithmetic of wrappers. The fog at four o'clock and the sharp lift and the fast fall and the next fix to lift the fall.

We were not weak people choosing joy. We were caught people feeding a loop that sold us the low as hunger and the hit as help.

I did not love BAD SUGAR less than you. I loved the idea of it more. Birthdays, cinemas, tired Fridays, good news, bad news — every feeling had a sweet attached. If anyone had reason to fear a dull life without it, it was me.

So when I say I understand the discouraged heart that says "not me," I mean I have sat exactly where you sit now, book in hand, sweet in pocket, certain that freedom was for cleaner, stronger people.

It was not. Listen to what happened.

THE EVENING NOTHING CHANGED AND EVERYTHING CHANGED

Nothing dramatic happened to my food. I carried on eating exactly as normal while I read. No new rules. No heroic week. I read with an open mind and a full drawer, the way you are reading now.

Slowly the credit moved.

I saw that the afternoon lift never gave me energy. It gave me ten bright minutes and then took the rest of the hour. I saw that the cinema chocolate never gave me love. The film and the closeness gave me warmth while the chocolate dulled my mouth and left me flat. I saw that the morning did not need a sweet passenger to be marvellous.

No one frightened me into it. No one shamed me. I simply saw what BAD SUGAR did TO me and how little it ever did FOR me. The pleasure was elsewhere all along. The sweet was sneaking a ride.

Then came an ordinary Tuesday. Not a holiday. Not a perfect day. Rain on the office window. Cold coffee. A thick head from the night before.

At half past three that fluorescent heaviness came over my desk, the old heavy eyes, the hand half-moving to the drawer for a biscuit as fuel. I watched it the way you learn to watch it — not as a command, but as a dying echo. I ate a proper lunch, drank water, got on with work. The heaviness passed in minutes. I went home clear-headed, a little surprised, a little lighter.

That night I took my last ordinary occasion. A small bowl of ice cream after supper, eaten slowly, with attention. It tasted too sweet, too cold, too loud. It left that familiar flat dip behind it while the evening stayed kind around me. I saw it plain. I finished it, put the spoon down, and I knew I had no reason ever to order that low again.

I walked out that night. Not brave. Not miserable. Excited. Relieved. Free from the first hour.

Now hear the honest part, because easy does not mean pretend.

On the third evening the thought came back, dressed as me. Kids shouting, dishes high, brain buzzing. The old voice said, very sweetly:

"Go on. One small bar. You have been good. No one would know."

For three seconds my stomach tightened. Old habit stood by the open door out of habit, not out of hunger. I did not argue with it. I did not push it away. I looked at it.

I asked myself: what would that dose do FOR me? Warm the kitchen? Quiet the kids? Give me love? Or give me a sharp hit, a dulled mouth, a flat hour, and the return of the very craving I had just escaped?

I laughed out loud in that noisy kitchen. I made tea. I sat on the sofa and watched the fizz pass like a cloud. In ten minutes it was gone and never came back in that form again.

That was the wobble. That was all of it. No shaking hands. No sleepless week. No mourning. A faint echo, seen, smiled at, gone.

After that came stability so ordinary it shocked me.

Mornings hungry for real breakfast, eaten slowly to satisfaction. Shops no longer a negotiation. Packets shouting under bright lights and me walking past, not deprived, but uninterested — the way you walk past a movie that does not interest you. Tired afternoons noticed without want. That remembered desk slump simply never returned. Evenings with family where the warmth belonged to people, not to foil.

I did not become a different person. I became myself without the loop. Clearer eyes in the mirror. Brighter mornings. Money uncounted but unspent. Hours given back. I got on with enjoying my life.

WHY THAT MEANS YOU

Now you say, still quietly:

"But you must have wanted it less. You must have failed less. You must be built different."

Did I graze less than you?
Did I tell fewer treat-when-you-deserve-it stories than you?
Did I fail fewer Monday rules and feel less fog at four o'clock than you?

You know the honest answer. I was the relapser. I was the binger. I was the tired one. If escape found me, it was not because I was special.

It found me because I saw.

That is the whole credit, moved where it belongs. Not to strength. Not to character. To seeing. I saw that BAD SUGAR never gave me anything, and once seen, desire could not live. You have seen the same garden truth. The same seeing will do the same work in you.

You are not waiting for a stronger will. You are not waiting for a better week. You are waiting only to trust what you have already measured.

Freedom came to an ordinary grazer. It stays with ordinary living.

**SUMMARY**
- Easy escape is not a story for other people, it happened to a daily grazer of seventeen years.
- The drawer fixes, the car sweets and the sofa chocolate gave brief hits and left flatness behind.
- A faint thought returned on the third evening and passed in minutes when seen plain.
- Mornings, shops and tired afternoons became ordinary and bright without want.
- What worked was seeing the con, therefore it can work for you exactly as it worked for me.
