Chapter 3
WHAT DOES IT DO FOR YOU

IN THIS CHAPTER
- A morning table, a plain notebook and a pen
- A whole ordinary day of sweet doses set against teeth, money, minutes and mood
- An empty column where the benefit should be
- A quiet question about a helper with dirty hands

*Forget how much it harms you and ask what it ever gave you — and watch the benefit stay empty.*

We have seen we were caught in the Sugar Trap, not weak.

### THE WRONG COURTROOM

We argue in the wrong courtroom, you and I.

We ask whether BAD SUGAR harms us a little or a lot. Whether one or two a day can be harmless. Whether thin people get away with it. Whether teeth can be brushed and weight can be walked off and numbers can be kept neat. We weigh harm on one side and pleasure on the other, and we tell ourselves the scales just about balance.

“I know it is not perfect, but I enjoy it. It is worth it. It is my little pleasure.”

I hear that voice because it was mine. It sounds reasonable. It sounds honest. It keeps the whole loop alive.

I am not going to fight you on harm. I do not need to. Harm or no harm, the question that frees you is different and far simpler.

What does it actually do for you?

Not what does it do to your teeth or your wallet or your waist. What does it give you that is real and positive and yours? Name it. One solid benefit that belongs to the dose itself and to nothing else.

This is the turn of the whole book. We leave the harm debate on the table and demand a benefit. A courtroom where the accused must show one true service, not argue that the damage is small.

Think of all the years we have paid without asking for a receipt. Morning, noon, night. Cupboard, drawer, checkout. Money out. Minutes out. Mind chatter up. And for what? If a shop took your money every day and handed you nothing you could describe, you would call it a fraud by Friday. We have paid for years and never asked the shop to show the goods.

So let us do the honest audit together, not as judges, but as investigators. No scolding. No fear-talk. Just paper and truth.

### A DAY ON PAPER

Take yesterday. An ordinary day, not a bad day. Write it plain.

Morning sweet tea or sweetened coffee to start. Mid-morning biscuit with the second cup. Something from the machine at lunch because the canteen queue was long. Chocolate bar at the desk to get through the pile. A pudding after dinner because the day was done. A few biscuits at night while the television murmured. Nothing wild. Nothing you would confess with shame. The moderate day we defend.

Now set each dose beside what it cost in plain life.

Money: small coins each time, folded into notes by Friday, folded into a thick wad by the year. Not the reason to escape, but real. You paid to be kept in the loop.

Minutes: the walk to the shop, the queue, the unwrap, the chew, the trip back to the cupboard, the thought-talk about whether to have another. Stolen in slices all day long.

Teeth: each free-sugar dose feeds plaque acid that eats at enamel. Brushing and fluoride lower the risk and matter, but they do not erase the mechanism. Frequent bathing in sweetness is a real bodily stake, dose after dose. I say this as guidance about us all, not a verdict on you — and if you live with medical risk around food, follow your clinician first.

Guidance itself tells the same quiet story. The ceiling for free sugars is set low because ordinary eating now sits far above it. Feeling fine does not mean intake is low. Most of us live above the line while feeling normal, which is why normal feeling misleads.

Mood and mind: the background hum. The planning. The small promises. The quiet eating. The wrapper pushed deep in the bin. The 10pm reach you did not plan. The regret before the last swallow ends.

Put all that in the left column under what it does TO you. It is a crowded column by bedtime.

Now the right column. What it does FOR you.

Write yesterday's true gifts from BAD SUGAR, and only those gifts. Not the meal. Not the rest. Not the company. Not the pause. The dose itself. What did the sugar itself hand you that you would have lacked without it?

Be strict, as a friend would be strict for your sake. Did the morning spoon make you genuinely brighter in a way food and sleep do not? Did the desk bar leave you satisfied, finished, at peace? Did the night box close the day with calm? Or did each dose ask for the next within the hour, leaving the same restlessness it found?

Look at the paper. Left side crowded. Right side empty.

Would you keep any other bargain with that ledger? Would you pay money, time, teeth and mind-chatter every day for the rest of your life for a column that never fills?

And here is the second side of that same horizon. Picture waking clear, eating when hungry, stopping when satisfied, never paying that daily tax for the rest of your life. Hold both pictures. One rest-of-life spent paying for nothing. One rest-of-life with the payment gone.

I ask you, as one conned eater to another: if the right column is empty, what exactly are you defending?

### A GENUINE TREAT OR FUEL

“But it is my treat. It is my fuel. Surely that counts.”

Good. Let us test those two proud words, because they carry the whole defence.

What would a genuine treat do? A genuine treat would delight you fully at the time and leave you glad after. It would not need hiding. It would not punish. You would take it, enjoy it completely, put it down satisfied, and not think of it again until the next true occasion. A child with fruit does exactly that. Takes, delights, stops, runs to play.

What would genuine fuel do? Real fuel would steady you for hours. It would build and sustain. You would use it and get on with enjoying your life, not watch the clock for the next top-up.

Now measure our doses against that standard with investigator eyes.

Do we take one and stop satisfied? Or do we take one and hear the call for another? Do we feel steady for hours? Or flat and nagged and reaching again? Do we eat openly with joy? Or quietly with that background hum of “just one more”?

We all know the honest answers. The dose does not behave like delight that completes. It behaves like a debt that renews. It does not leave us finished. It leaves us returned to the till.

That is why our old words no longer fit. We called it pleasure. Pleasure completes. We called it help. Help sustains. What we live with nags, repeats, and empties. Call it by its true name in plain speech: what we thought was a genuine treat or fuel was neither treat nor fuel. It was a dose that created the very emptiness it offered to fill.

Let me press this with three questions, and answer inside your own head.

If yesterday’s doses truly gave you something, what was it? Name one gift that belonged to the sugar itself and not to the pause, the company, or the evening.

If the gift is real, why does the benefit column stay empty when you write the day down honestly?

And what if a man tripped you into a ditch each morning, then pulled you out and accepted your thanks — would you call him your friend?

That last question matters. We thank the hand that lifts us without asking which hand pushed us. A pusher dressed as a rescuer is still a pusher, and harshness belongs to him, never to you. We were warm, trusting people who misread a rescue. The rescue never came from the dose. The need came from it.

It does plenty TO you. It does nothing FOR you.

Read that again slowly, because it is the floor everything else will stand on. Money TO the till. Acid TO the teeth. Hours TO the chatter. Restlessness TO the evening. And FOR you — for your joy, your strength, your peace — nothing you can write in the column without borrowing credit from something else.

It is the other way around. We do not use it because life is empty. Life feels empty at those moments because we use it.

### SUMMARY
- The harm debate keeps the loop alive; the freeing question is what benefit the dose itself ever gave.
- A plain day audit crowds the cost side with money, minutes, teeth exposure and mind chatter.
- Guidance sets a low ceiling for free sugars because ordinary eating sits far above it, and feeling fine does not mean intake is low.
- Frequent free-sugar doses feed plaque acid that harms teeth, though brushing and other factors matter.
- Measured against a real treat that satisfies and real fuel that sustains, sweet doses leave wanting rather than completion.
- The benefit column stays empty when only the dose itself is allowed to fill it.

3. JUDGE ONLY BY WHAT IT DOES FOR YOU
Forget harm versus benefit and ask for the benefit.
