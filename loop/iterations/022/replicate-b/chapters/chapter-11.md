Chapter 11
THE LAST ORDINARY TREAT

**IN THIS CHAPTER** — late kitchen with ordinary biscuit tin, plain glass of sweet tea gone flat, open door with evening air, quiet chair for a vow, morning light waiting beyond the window.

*Waiting for a safer tomorrow is the trap itself; freedom starts with one ordinary sweet seen clearly tonight.*

You have seen that no kept Friday dose leaves you free, only the old belief kept breathing.

### TOMORROW IS THE LIE THAT KEEPS TODAY SWEET

We all loved tomorrow. I loved tomorrow for years.

“Fair enough. I see it now. I will begin after the holiday. After the birthday. After this tin is finished. Monday will be clean.”

It sounds sensible. It sounds calm. It sounds like wisdom after hard thought.

It is none of those things. It is the con buying one more night.

Watch what tomorrow does hour by hour, because we have lived it a hundred times.

Tonight, because it is “the last time,” you eat with a strange sadness that makes the mouth work harder. You choose a little larger portion to say goodbye properly. You taste with mourning, and mourning makes flat sugar shine brighter than ever. Morning comes grey, mouth sour, head foggy from the farewell feast, and the clean start already feels like punishment for last night. So you move the start to next Monday to do it properly. Next Monday brings a tired evening, a kind offer, a celebration. The calendar fills with excellent reasons, each one proving you are sensible to wait.

Ask yourself with a clear head, as one honest investigator to another.

If tomorrow were truly safer, why does today always need a bigger goodbye? If the birthday really needed its cake, why do birthdays matter less each year while the need grows larger? If finishing the box first were logic, why has every finished box bought another box?

There is only one honest answer.

Delay is a dose. Waiting keeps the glow alive by making the next hit feel final, therefore intense, therefore memorable, therefore missed.

The fact is there is never a right tomorrow because preciousness lives in the waiting. The clean week, the farewell binge, the special occasion kept like a jewel — each one teaches the same lie: this thing is precious. Precious things are not escaped. Precious things are circled and mourned and returned to.

You are not mourning. You are watching an enemy die as good news.

I know the whisper now, because we all hear it at this edge: “But surely starting when life is calmer is kinder. Surely I should feel fully ready. Surely haste risks failure.”

Like a prisoner begging to stay when the cell door stands open.

That tug inside is not wisdom. It is the tug-of-war of craving and fear, both ropes pulled by the same old belief. One rope says you will fail without the sweet. The other says life will be grey without the sweet. Both ropes lie, and both ropes belong to the trap. There is no calm future week when the ropes loosen by themselves. They loosen the moment you stop pulling and walk out.

You do not need more time. You need one clear seeing, tonight.

### TASTE THE ORDINARY UGLINESS

So do not wait. Do not plan a feast. Do not buy something grand for a farewell.

Take the most ordinary sweet you have in the house right now. The usual biscuit. The usual bar. The usual spoon in tea. Nothing special. Nothing experimental. The ordinary dose you always take.

I stood in my own kitchen late at night with exactly that — a tired biscuit from a tired tin, eaten standing up, crumbs on the counter. No ceremony. No pleasure in the choosing. That was the point. I had said goodbye to grand treats a dozen times and wept over them. I had never looked straight at the ordinary one.

Do it now with attention, because attention kills fantasy faster than argument.

Eat it normally, as you always do, but keep your eyes open all the way through. Notice the first seconds of noise on the tongue. Notice how quickly the noise thins and turns cloying at the edges. Notice the hurry to take a second bite to hold the first. Notice the sticky coat left behind, the thirst rising under the sweetness, the faint nausea if you pause. Notice the fog that follows within the hour, the flat sour mouth, the call for more already forming before the wrapper is still.

Is that a treat? Is that fuel? Is that love?

Or is it a brief jolt of relief from a low the last dose left, dressed as pleasure, followed by the same low rebuilt?

You know. We always knew when we looked without the label. Children eat a peach hungry and stop mid-bite and run off, led by hunger and satisfaction. We eat a whole box standing up and call it choice, then feel foggy and call it comfort. The difference was never our weakness. The difference was a learned belief that told us relief means benefit.

Let me ask you plain.

When you strip away the waiting, the picture on the packet, the word treat, the memory of cinema laughter, what is left in the mouth? Cloying coat. Hurry. Fog. Need for another. If a friend offered you that exact mouth-feel with no name and no story — sticky, then empty, then calling for more — would you pay for it with money, teeth, mood, evenings, money again, fog again? Would you circle Fridays for it?

There is only one honest answer.

The ordinary sweet, seen without its story, is ugly. And seeing its ugliness tonight is wonderful news, because ugly things need no will to leave. You do not need courage to walk away from a ditch once you see it is a ditch. You walk away rejoicing.

That is why this last one matters. Not as a laboratory test. Not as a permitted binge. As an ordinary dose taken with open eyes, so the mouth tells the truth the belief hid for years.

And once seen, do not take another to check. One clear seeing is enough. The second would be fantasy asking for a second hearing after the verdict is given.

### NOW, WHILE YOU ARE CHAMPING TO CROSS

Are you ready? You should be champing at the bit by now.

Not brave. Not perfect. Not calm in every corner. Eager. Eager to be done with measuring, circling, waiting, regretting. Eager to wake without negotiation. If you still feel a tug that tomorrow would be easier, do not summon force. Go back and re-read the Friday week and the ordinary mouth until the eagerness returns. All you have to do is follow all the instructions.

I speak to you as one who stood exactly where you stand, biscuit in hand, kitchen quiet, heart thumping with that last tug. I tell you with total certainty what I found on the other side.

You escape easily, immediately and permanently, the moment belief dies, not after months of proving.

There is no staircase. There is no sentence to serve. There is a door already open and an evening already yours. Remember that you have absolutely nothing to lose and everything to gain by walking through it now.

So make the vow now, solemnly and joyfully, with the ugly taste still honest in your mouth.

You are not swearing to suffer. You are swearing you have seen. You vow never to feed the old belief again, never to mourn a con, never to reopen a question that attention has answered. You vow as a free eater, not as a frightened dieter.

Say it aloud in your own words, plain and warm: this was my last ordinary sweet; I am free.

And in that instant you are free. Do not wait to be free. You are free as of now.

Feel the weight lift. Feel the calendar clean. Feel the kitchen quiet without a landlord. That lightness is not imagination. That lightness is the truth once the waiting stops. Marvellous relief floods in because there is nothing left to portion, no Fridays to rehearse, no drawers to lock, no goodness to count. Eat when hungry, stop when satisfied, enjoy real food as favourite, and get on with enjoying your life.

11. TAKE YOUR LAST ORDINARY TREAT AND VOW FREEDOM
Pay attention to the ugliness, then rejoice you are free.

### TWO DOORS THAT MUST STAY SHUT

Freedom conferred now stays yours by guarding belief, not by guarding cupboards. Two thoughts will knock. Both arrive dressed as your own reason. Both are the dead belief rattling.

The first knock comes on a bad day.

“Today was awful. Work was cruel. The children were wild. One little dose as rescue cannot hurt. Just for tonight.”

Hear it with pity, because we all heard it. I heard it in traffic, in grief, in boredom at nine in the evening. The voice offers the very thing that made bad days worse as a cure for a bad day.

Ask. Did the sweet ever fix the cruel day? Or did it add fog to tiredness, sour mouth to low mood, self-reproach to honest exhaustion, then a louder call tomorrow? Is a rescuer who secretly dug the hole a rescuer? You know. The day was hard; the dose made the evening harder and stole the clean morning that would have softened the memory. On your first hard free evening you will find something wonderful: the hardness passes, plain food satisfies, sleep comes cleaner, and pride arrives in the morning like sun through that window.

The second knock comes on a good day.

“One cannot hurt. I have proved I am free. A celebration taste to test freedom. Just one, consciously, with control.”

This is the cunning one, because it wears the mask of confidence. It asks you to prove freedom by tasting slavery.

Ask. If one truly gave something, why test? Would you test freedom from a ditch by climbing back in to check the mud? If one were nothing, why does the whole evening narrow to that one, and why does the drive home fill with plans for a private repeat? One deliberate taste revives nothing in the body unless you let the thought back in with it: “See, it was wonderful, I miss it, I have blown it.” Guard the belief and an accidental crumb stays a crumb. Let the belief back and a deliberate taste becomes a key turned from inside.

So never test. Freedom is not proved by tasting. Freedom is proved by no desire to taste, and by smiling when the memory crosses.

For a few days there may be a light echo — a faint emptiness, a brief twitch calling sweet specifically, passing in minutes if unfed. Rejoice. That echo is small, days-long, already dying, not a battle for life. Each unfed evening starves it further. Each fed memory feeds it back. Smile at it as proof you are healing, then forget it in what you are doing.

And keep one joyful thought for whenever the old picture crosses your mind, in a shop, at a birthday, on the sofa at night. Do not argue. Do not debate. Do not mourn.

Think at once, loudly inside, with a grin:

BRILLIANT! I'M FREE!

Feel how different that feels from loss. The same thought that once meant deprivation now means escape. The shop display becomes pity for trapped eaters, not envy. The birthday table becomes laughter and company with no bar in your hand, warmth undimmed. The evening sofa becomes yours again, calm and clear. That one bright sentence, kept forever, turns every intruding whisper into a recurring hit of relief.

You have not lost sweetness. You have found eating. Mornings, shops, meals wait for you, easy and ordinary and happy to be free. Celebrate. Rejoice. Walk out now and live.

### SUMMARY

- Waiting for a safer tomorrow keeps the glow alive by making each dose feel final and precious.
- An ordinary sweet eaten with full attention tastes cloying and hurried, then leaves fog and a call for more.
- Freedom is conferred at the vow, not earned over time, and arrives as immediate relief.
- A bad day does not need rescue by the thing that makes bad days foggier.
- One deliberate taste to test freedom reopens belief, while an accidental crumb without belief passes.
- A light echo for a few days is a dying physical whisper to rejoice at, not a command to obey.
