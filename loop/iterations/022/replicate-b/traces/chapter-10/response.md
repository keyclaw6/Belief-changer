Chapter 10
NO SAFE SWEET

**IN THIS CHAPTER** — Friday kitchen with shining allowance box, cliff edge with short-fall promise, empty house with locked drawer, calendar page with circled tomorrow, car seatbelt pulled across a quiet chest.

*Inside BAD SUGAR there is no safe one, only the belief kept alive.*

You have watched the cinema evening stay warm and laughing with no bar in your hand.

And now the mind that saw it tries one last bargain.

“Very well. The cinema bar gave nothing. The birthday cake gave nothing. But surely a small Friday sweet, kept under control, harms no one. Surely I can keep the house clean and allow one civilised dose. Surely I can wean down and start properly tomorrow.”

I hear you because we all said it. I said it. Every trapped eater says it at this exact point, with relief in the voice, as if negotiation were wisdom.

It is not wisdom. It is the last whisper of a dying con trying to keep one cord plugged in.

### THE FRIDAY THAT GLOWS TOO BRIGHT

Let us live your Friday rule hour by hour, as we lived it, and watch what allowance really does.

Monday. You decree: no BAD SUGAR in the house, one clean week, then Friday night, one dessert, eaten like a normal person. The week feels virtuous already. Tuesday you think of Friday twice. Wednesday you picture the pudding on the menu, the glossy top, the spoon going in. Thursday you are already tasting it at your desk at three o’clock. You tell a friend, lightly, “I’ve managed to be good this week, so Friday is earned.” The friend nods. The glow grows.

Friday comes. You buy the chosen dose with unusual care. You carry it home like treasure. You wait till the plates are cleared. You lift the first spoonful while everyone watches, or while no one watches and the quiet makes it feel more your own. For ninety seconds there is brightness on the tongue. Then the old hurry. A second spoon to hold the brightness. A third because the brightness thins. Then regret before the swallow ends, and the box empty though you swore one small bowl.

Ask, as one honest investigator to another.

If Friday were truly a small pleasure freely chosen, why did four days of thinking about it feel like waiting for pay? Why did the spoon need chasing within minutes? Why did Saturday morning wake with a louder call than any Monday before the rule began? And why did Thursday suddenly deserve a little starter, and Saturday deserve a little finisher, till the clean week held only on paper?

There is only one honest answer.

Allowance did not contain the want. Allowance fed the want and made it precious.

We lived that creep every time. What begins as Friday becomes Friday plus Thursday Eve because Thursday is nearly Friday. Then Saturday because the box is open and waste is a pity. Then Monday to repair the weekend fog with a quick lift. The line moves because the line is drawn inside the very thing that erases lines.

The fact is moderation makes BAD SUGAR more valuable, not less. Forbidden fruit tastes brighter. Spaced doses hit harder because the low between them grows deeper. Each permitted hit re-teaches the mouth that relief lives in a wrapper, and the lesson lands stronger for the wait.

That Friday glow is the Sweet Con talking.

It tells you the glow proves the dose is special. It is the other way around. The rule made the glow. Take the same dessert eaten unplanned, unwaited, on a dull Tuesday among three other doses, and where is the magic? Gone. Flat sugar, cloying coat, fog to follow. The magic was never in the recipe. The magic was deprivation plus anticipation plus a label that said treat, working on a mouth already taught to call relief pleasure.

Does a free person need a calendar to enjoy food? Does a child ask which day joy is allowed? Who is in charge when a grown adult circles a date to be permitted a biscuit?

You know. We were never in charge under a rule. We were tenants asking the landlord for an hour of light.

### THE CLIFF WITH THE SHORT FALL

Picture it clean, because absurdity seen plain frees faster than argument.

A friend walks you to a cliff on a bright morning. Sea below, gulls, wind in your coat. He points to the edge and says, cheerful and reasonable, “Jump. Only fall a few metres. Enjoy the fall, then stop in mid-air. Everyone sensible falls a little on Fridays.”

You stare at him. “Fall a little? From a cliff, fall is fall. Gravity does not do Fridays.”

He shrugs. “You lack balance. Others manage a short fall. Just one neat drop.”

Would you jump? Would you admire his moderation? Would you blame your fear on weakness?

Of course not. You would laugh him off the cliff-path and walk home free.

Yet that is exactly what “just one on Fridays” asks of you inside BAD SUGAR. Jump into the Sugar Trap but land softly. Feed the low a little but do not create the low. Light the call but do not hear it afterwards. Keep the belief that one dose gives something, but do not act on the belief you just kept.

If there were benefit in doing it often, you would not be reading a book to escape it. If there is no benefit in doing it often, what possible point is there in doing it occasionally? A trap that gives nothing on Tuesday gives nothing on Friday. A poison in sweet coat does not become food by calendar.

“But just one tiny taste cannot restart anything. It is only a crumb.”

Ventriloquise it fully, because the voice is cunning. “I don’t keep it in the house, so I am safe. I only taste at parties. I only finish the children’s plates. Surely that is control.”

Let me ask you plain.

If you do not keep it in the house because you know one box becomes an empty box, is that control or is that confession? If the house must be locked like a safe, who is the prisoner? If one taste at a party were truly nothing, why does the whole party narrow to the table where the tastes sit, and why does the drive home fill with plans for a private repeat? If finishing small plates proves mastery, why do small plates get finished with a hurry no plain meal ever caused, standing by the bin, uncounted?

There is only one honest answer.

The house rule proves the trap is alive. The locked drawer proves the prisoner still serves. Control that needs locks, dates, promises, and secrecy is not freedom wearing a neat coat. It is slavery with a rota.

It does plenty TO you. It does nothing FOR you.

I state that flat because you have audited it yourself across a hundred evenings. Brief noise on the tongue, then the low calling for more, then fog, then self-reproach, then the next plan to repair fog with the thing that made fog. No Friday ever broke that chain. Friday only gilded one link and charged you a week of waiting for the shine.

### TOMORROW NEVER TASTES SWEET

Then comes the softest bargain of all, spoken kindly, almost reasonably.

“I accept it all. I will quit tomorrow. After the birthday. After the holiday. After this box is finished. A clean start on Monday.”

We all loved tomorrow. I loved tomorrow for years. Tomorrow lets you feel wise without changing anything today. Tomorrow keeps the belief warm overnight.

Follow tomorrow hour by hour and see who it serves.

Tonight you eat with a strange sadness, half mourning, half gloating, because it is “the last time.” The dose tastes more precious for being last. You eat more, not less, to say goodbye. Morning comes grey, mouth sour, energy low from the farewell feast. Tomorrow feels like punishment already, so you postpone to next Monday to “do it properly.” Next Monday brings a work dinner, a tired evening, a kind offer. The calendar fills with excellent reasons, each one proving you are sensible to wait.

Ask yourself with a clear head.

If tomorrow were safer, why does today always need a larger goodbye? If the birthday cake truly mattered, why do birthdays matter less each year while the need grows larger? If finishing the box first were logic, why has every finished box bought another box? Is tomorrow a plan, or is tomorrow a dose wearing a diary?

There is only one honest answer.

Delay is the dose. Waiting keeps the glow alive by making the next hit feel final, therefore intense, therefore memorable, therefore missed.

The fact is there is never a right tomorrow because the trap lives in the waiting. The clean week, the farewell binge, the special occasion kept like a jewel — each one teaches the same lie: this thing is precious. Precious things are not escaped. Precious things are mourned, circled, returned to.

You are not mourning. You are seeing an enemy die as good news.

I know the whisper now: “This totality talk sounds extreme. Surely a little of anything is fine. Surely you are turning food into fear. Surely weaning is kinder.”

Hear that voice with pity, because it wears the mask of common sense and speaks for the con.

Weaning asks you to keep the belief alive while pretending to loosen it. Small daily hits keep the low topped up just enough to feel normal, and the gaps between them fill with fantasy about the next hit. You do not drink less poison kindly. You drink it attentively, gratefully,Preciously, till the small becomes the large again because the mouth was never told the truth, only told to wait.

And is totality extreme when the thing gives nothing? Is it extreme to take zero sips from a ditch you have climbed out of? Is it extreme to refuse to jump a little from a cliff? Extremity would be spending the rest of your days measuring crumbs, locking drawers, circling Fridays, counting goodness, fearing birthdays — that is extreme. Refusing the whole con in one clean seeing is not extreme. It is relief.

One exception keeps you inside the Sugar Trap.

Read that twice and feel the weight lift, not fall. It means you do not need a hundred rules. You need one clear seeing. No measuring. No bargaining. No Fridays to rehearse. The entire exhausting arithmetic collapses because there is nothing to portion. You do not portion a con once seen. You leave it.

### THE SEATBELT, NOT THE STEERING WHEEL

“But what if a crumb slips in by accident? A sauce sweetened, a biscuit pressed on me, a bite taken before I read the label? Does one slip mean I am hooked again for life?”

No. And understanding this frees you from panic forever.

Think of the belt across your chest when you drive. It sits quiet, unnoticed, doing nothing journey after journey. It exists for an accidental jolt no careful driver plans. It does not mean you drive into walls to test it. It does not mean a jolt proves you cannot drive. It means the body is sturdier than fear, while the mind must never steer into the wall on purpose.

Your body works the same way with BAD SUGAR. An accidental taste, eaten without belief, without glow, without plan, passes like a jolt against the belt. The body copes. The stomach settles. The day goes on. What revives the trap is never the crumb. What revives the trap is the thought you allow back in with the crumb: “See, it tasted wonderful, I miss it, I have blown it, I may as well keep going.” Guard the belief and the crumb stays a crumb. Let the belief back and the crumb becomes a key turned in a cell you had left.

So do not panic over accidents, and never plan them. Do not test freedom with deliberate tastes. A driver does not prove his belt by aiming at lorries. Freedom is not proved by tasting. Freedom is proved by no desire to taste, and by smiling when the thought crosses because you know what it was.

That eight o’clock whine is the Nibbler dying.

Feel the smallness of it now that Friday has lost its glamour. A little twitch, a little emptiness calling sweet specifically, never satisfied by plain food, passing in minutes if unfed. We once read that twitch as order, as hunger, as need-something-now. It was never command. It was echo. Each unfed evening starves it further. Each fed Friday feeds it back to life and calls the feeding proof.

And the schedule itself was always part of the snare. Weeks of clean days broken by bright binges, then shame, then clean days again — that yo-yo is not your character. That yo-yo is what spaced hits do. Allow, deny, crave, binge, regret, allow again. Break the spacing by refusing the allowance and the yo-yo loses its string. Hunger returns to slow build, plain food satisfies, stopping feels natural because there is no forbidden glow left to chase.

You are not helpless and you were never broken. The one-bite rush to the bottom of the box proved only that belief was still alive, not that you are condemned to obey it for life, and if food change could carry medical risk for you, your clinician leads and this book is belief only. Once belief dies, the bite loses its order. A bell can ring in an empty hall without anyone marching.

Let me leave you at the edge of freedom with the arithmetic done.

No cut-down. Cut-down makes precious.
No special sweet. Special keeps belief.
No tomorrow. Tomorrow gilds today.
No house-rule pride. Locks confess.
No weaning tastes. Tastes rehearse.

There is one stable state inside a con that gives nothing: none, gladly, for good. Not grimly. Not bravely. Gladly, because glad is the truth once the glow is seen as rule-made, not dose-made.

Stop rehearsing the fall. Walk away from the cliff. Keep the belt for jolts, never for steering, and get on with enjoying your evenings with the calendar clean.

10. NEVER KEEP A SPECIAL SWEET
One exception keeps the whole Trap alive.

### SUMMARY

- A Friday allowance makes each dose glow brighter by waiting, not by taste.
- One permitted taste carries the binge because the belief is still alive, not because you are broken.
- Clean weeks broken by bright binges form a yo-yo where spacing drives craving.
- Tomorrow planning gilds today’s dose and keeps preciousness alive through farewell eating.
- A locked house and a circled date confess the trap rather than prove control.
- An accidental crumb passes like a jolt against a belt, while a deliberate exception steers back into the trap.
