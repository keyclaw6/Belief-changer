Chapter 5
REWARD

Nine o'clock finds you in the kitchen with the fridge door open and its light laid across the lino. The house has gone quiet after the long day. The sink holds the last plates. Your shoulders ache from hours of holding yourself upright at the desk and the table and the wheel. Your eyes feel dry when you blink. You believe the day asks for a closing kindness, and kindness matters to you, because you have carried much without complaint.
You believe a small sweet delivers that kindness, and the belief arrives dressed as memory, because memory lends every old answer a glow.

IN THIS CHAPTER: a bakery doorway, a payday kitchen, a nine o'clock light and a family table.

*The people paid you and the moment held you; BAD SUGAR only rode along.*

The Bakery Doorway

You remember the bell over the bakery door, and you believe the memory proves sweetness made childhood warm, because warmth lives in the picture. Hold the picture with me and look at all it holds. Your mother holds your hand at the counter. The glass case stands low enough for a child to press a nose near. Rows of iced buns sit in paper cups. A tray of smile-face cookies looks back with currant eyes and a piped grin. The air smells of warm dough and sugar dust and coffee from the back. Your mother nods toward the tray and says choose one, and her face softens as she watches you choose.
You believe the softness came from the cookie, and gratitude lives in you, because you love her still. Look again with tender eyes. The softness came from her hand around yours and the unhurried ten minutes out of a hard week and the pride on her face when you grinned back with crumbs on your chin. The cookie sat in the middle of all that like a small bright button on a large warm coat. You believe the button made the coat warm, and children read so, because bright things catch the eye. The coat made the coat warm.
We all stood at such counters, and you believe your case holds a special tenderness that argument must not touch, because tenderness deserves honour. I honour it fully. I tell you the tenderness stays untouched while the button takes its true size. Your mother bought love with time and attention and walking you there in the rain and lifting you to see. The shop added flour and icing. Flour and icing never lifted a child to see. Hands lifted. Eyes watched. Time slowed. Those were the givers.
You tell me, in your own true words, "The sweetness of chocolate can hint at love," and I take the sentence with care, because love talk deserves care. You believe hint proves kinship, and poetry persuades, because love lends weight to whatever stands near it. Notice what stood near. Love stood near on those afternoons in the shape of a woman who knew your name and your tooth gap and which cookie made you laugh. The sweet stood near because she placed it there as her messenger. Messengers carry notes. They do not write them. Take the messenger from the memory in thought and leave all else, the same hand, the same bell, the same ten unhurried minutes, the same walk home with the bag swinging, and the afternoon keeps its glow. Put the messenger back and remove the hand, the bell, the minutes, the walk, leaving only a paper bag eaten alone under cold light, and the glow goes out. The glow lived in the hand and the hour. The sweet only rode along.
You believe years of birthdays prove the sweet earned its place, and loyalty lives in you, because you keep what kept you. Count what truly kept you across those years. The kitchen chair pulled close. The story read twice. The lap that held you after the scraped knee. The laugh across the table when the icing slid sideways. The sweet attended those scenes the way a hat attends a wedding, present in the photographs, absent from the vows. We confuse attendance with authorship when the photographer points the lens at the hat. The heart knows the difference when asked plainly.
You believe removing the sweet removes the child from the picture, and fear gathers there, because no one wishes to lose a beloved morning. Hear this plainly and kindly. The child stays. The mother stays. The bell stays. The hand stays. Only the button changes size. That is no loss to mourn. That is a coat seen true at last.

The Payday Parcel

Friday evening smells of chips and rain on coats, and you believe the tired body asks for a sweet wage on top of wages, because the week took much. See the kitchen with me as the bags land on the table. Pay has landed in the account. The week of early alarms and late buses and held tempers closes behind you. Someone tips a small bag of payday candy onto the side plate, unwrapped ends twisted bright, and says we earned this. The kettle boils. The television murmurs from the corner. Feet rise onto the low stool.
You tell me, in your own true words, "Sugar is the reward for a hard earned paycheck," and "I deserve a little treat now and then," and "celebrating, rewarding myself, going to the cinema, etc with chocolate," and I hear pride and tiredness braided together, because both are honest. You believe the braid needs a sweet tie to hold, and reward talk feels just, because earned things taste best. Ask where the earned taste truly sits. The pay sits in the account. The rest sits in the chair. The pride sits in work done and bills met and children fed and a hard manager faced without flinching. The candy sits in bright twists and does no work at all. It pays nothing. It crowns nothing. It borrows the shine of the pay packet and calls the shine its own.
You believe the crown makes the king, and ceremony dazzles, because crowns catch light. Watch crowning with cool eyes. A paper crown placed on honest work shines for a photograph and wilts by morning. The work stands without it. The week was hard and you met it. That meeting is the pay. The mouthful adds noise, then calls for another mouthful, then leaves the tongue coated and the eyes heavier and the evening thinner. True pay leaves the worker richer. This pay leaves the worker reaching.
Think of the holiday tin tied to long trips and grandmother gifts, and you believe distance proves the sweet carried love across miles, because miles test love. See the tin with me on the shelf, round and deep with its painted lid, opened only when visitors came or letters arrived from across the sea. The tin held toffees wrapped in wax and a paper lace doily underneath, and grandmother measured them out two at a time with ceremony, counting into small palms while stories ran about ships and snow and cousins you had not met. Your mouth remembers sweetness. Your heart remembers being counted in.
You believe counting in came from the tin, and the mistake is easy, because the tin held the counting. The tin held paper and wax. Grandmother held the counting. England lived in her voice and her hands and the stamps on the parcel and the smell of her coat from the cold platform, not in the toffee. The toffee attended the reunion the way music attends a dance, heard alongside, never the partner. Remove the toffee in thought and keep the voice, the hands, the parcel string, the story about the snow, and the room keeps its ache of joy. Remove the voice and keep only the toffee eaten alone at a counter, and the ache goes out. The ache was love. The toffee only rode along.
We learned pay talk early, and you believe early lessons must hold truth, because early hands fed us. Early hands fed us love with what the shops offered that year. Shops offered bright twists because bright twists moved quickly and kept without cold. Mothers and grandmothers reached for what stood near when they wished to say you matter and the week is done. The message was theirs. The wrapper was the shop's. Confusing the two credits the shop for the mother's heart. The mother's heart needs no wrapper to stand.
You believe a gray life waits where payday candy leaves the table, and the picture saddens, because gray tables frighten generous people. Look at the table after the bright twists leave it in thought. Pay still landed. Feet still rest. Kettle still steams. The programme still murmurs. Talk still runs about the manager and the bus and the neighbour's dog. Laughter still comes when it comes. Music still plays from the small speaker while plates soak. Sunlight still lays its last bar across the sill in summer months. Mouth still tastes supper eaten to satisfaction, salt and butter and bread, and water drunk cold. What left was noise and coating and the call for more noise. What stayed was the evening. The evening was the reward all along.

The Nine O'Clock Kitchen

Stay at nine o'clock with the fridge light on your face, because you believe this hour proves BAD SUGAR soothes, and soothing feels precious on hard days. The day replays behind your eyes while you scan the shelves. The sharp email. The traffic. The child crying over homework. The news with its loud faces. Your jaw holds the day's clench. Your stomach holds a small hollow ache that is not quite hunger and not quite tiredness. The chocolate bar waits in the door shelf where you placed it that morning against this very hour.
You tell me "I need something sweet," and "a pick-me-up," and "to get me through," and I hear no weakness in the phrases. I hear a hard-working adult asking for comfort in the only language the years taught. You believe the language proves the cure, and the mistake is natural, because fluent phrases feel true. The phrase came from years of tins and adverts and kind voices saying sweet helps, screens showing bright wrappers beside bright faces after hard days. The hollow ache is small and wordless. The sentence about chocolate is long and well-dressed. Small aches pass with breath and rest and warm drink and lamplight. Well-dressed sentences can be unlearned.
You believe the first squares soften the clench, and softening feels real, because shoulders drop a fraction as you chew. Watch the softening with investigator eyes. Shoulders drop because the day paused, because you sat at last, because the house quieted, because breath slowed, because no one asks anything of you while you chew. Rest softened. Quiet softened. Sitting softened. The sweet attended the softening the way a loud guest attends a lullaby, present in the room, absent from the soothing. Give the same ten minutes without the bar in thought, same chair, same lamplight, same warm drink held in both hands, same breath slowed on purpose, and the clench loosens the same. The bar added a loud ten minutes on top of loosening, then a coated tongue, then a wish for another row.
I have stood in that exact light with the door open and the cold on my ankles, eating past the point of tasting, telling myself the day deserved it. You believe such nights show a special hunger that only foil answers, and the story flatters the bar, because special hungers need special answers. There was nothing special in my mouth after the third square except sweetness grown dull and a jaw working from habit. The day still sat in my shoulders. The email still sat unsent. The traffic still sat in memory. The sweet soothed nothing. It covered the hour with noise while the hour asked for rest.
You believe stress needs sweetness the way cold needs blankets, and blankets prove the picture, because cold truly asks for covering. Test the picture with your own evenings as witness. Blankets warm and leave warmth behind. Sweet loudness warms nothing and leaves the hollow louder. After blankets the body rests. After the bar the hand returns to the shelf. After blankets sleep comes clean. After the bar sleep comes heavy with dreams that toss. After blankets morning thanks you. After the bar morning finds foil in the bin and a vow to be good that wilts by lunch. Blankets answer cold. The bar answers a schooled sentence about cold.
The tiny physical craving at nine o'clock is small and brief, a thin tug like a moth at a window, gone with a glass of water and a change of room. You believe the tug roars, and roaring frightens, because the brainwashing voice lends the tug a megaphone. Hear the split plainly. The tug is wordless and passes like cloud shadow. The voice says you earned it and the day demands it and one row harms nothing and tomorrow starts clean. The tug never learned those sentences. Schooling learned them. Once you hear schooling as schooling, the shelf loses its pull. The hand can close the door on a moth. It was the megaphone that made the moth look like weather.
You believe the office week proves the same, the drawer at three, the vending clatter at four, the Friday box passed hand to hand with jokes about survival, and survival talk bonds teams, because shared strain asks for shared rites. See the rite with clear eyes. The bond came from faces easing at last, from jokes, from the hour when no manager watches, from sunlight through blinds, from music leaking from one desk radio. The box stood where bond should have been and took bond's photograph. Teams bond over water and air and laughter just as fast when the box stays shut. The box never told a joke. People told jokes. The box only rode along.

The Family Table

Glance toward the celebration table, because you believe this scene proves the sweet holds love itself, and love must not be argued down. I argue nothing down. I keep every kindness intact. See the birthday dinner with dishes cleared and candles still warm in the air. Someone carries the rich cake with its glaze catching light. Voices lift. A joke runs about being no fun for refusing. A dish of mousse waits beside with its spoon standing. You lift a spoonful to avoid standing out while eyes watch.
You believe refusal would refuse the people, and loyalty lives in you, because you love these faces. Keep the faces fully. The love sits in the cook who rose early, in the hands that iced and carried, in the singing off-key and loud, in the shoulder squeezed as you sat, in the story told twice because grandfather loves telling it. The cake sits among all that like one bright plate in a long parade. Parades delight because crowds gather and music plays and children wave. No one thanks the plate for the parade.
You believe the mousse crowns the meal, and crowning language flatters hosts, because hosts deserve praise. Praise the host fully. Praise the roast and the gravy and the table laid with care and the hours of shopping and chopping and washing. Those were the gifts. The sweet close attended the gifts and borrowed their gratitude. Eat the plain meal to satisfaction in thought and sit in the talk and the lamplight and the full belly and the unhurried hour, and the evening stays complete. The completeness came from sufficiency and company. The wrap only rode along.
You tell me "Dessert is the best part of every meal," and "chocolate makes me feel like I am in heaven," and I hear heaven talk with respect, because heaven means most precious. Ask heaven with plain questions. If the dish truly carried heaven, why does heaven need refilling before the plates reach the sink? If the best part truly crowned, why does the crowned evening often end with heaviness and a vow about tomorrow? True best parts leave the heart quiet. This best part leaves the hand listening for more. Listening proves wanting, not heaven.
I speak of heaven carefully so no tenderness breaks. Your people stay beloved. The room stays warm. The song stays sung. Only the sweet changes size from author to attendant. Attendants deserve no scorn. They deserve true placement. Let the cake be cake among loved faces, not love among cakes. The distinction keeps all love and removes all con.

The Tight Shoes

You believe comfort talk must hold some truth, because ache asks for aid, and aid deserves a name. I give you a picture to test all comfort talk against, because pictures prove faster than definitions.
Think of tight shoes worn from morning to night, narrow at the toe, pinching with each stair. You believe they look smart, so you keep them through the dull hours, adjusting the lace at lunch, pressing the heel at three, counting the minutes to home. At the door you bend and pull them off and stand in socks on the cool floor. A sigh leaves you head to foot. Blood returns to the toes. The floor feels kind under the arch. You believe removal gave pleasure, and sighing proves it, because sighs tell truth.
Ask plainly what removal gave. It gave the end of pinch it caused all day. It gave nothing the barefoot morning lacked before the shoes went on. It poured no joy in. It stopped a pain it charged admission to relieve. No one calls tight shoes a comfort. No one pays for the privilege of pinching to earn the sigh. Yet we praise the sigh as though shoes healed. The sigh marks debt cleared, not gift given.
So it is with the comfort dose. Morning opens with night behind and mouth clean. Midday adds its small frictions, noise, hunger, hurry. The schooled hand answers each friction with a sweet hit that lifts loud for minutes and drops flat after. Evening finds nerves humming from a day of lifts and drops. The night bar arrives like shoe removal, a brief loosening as loudness covers tiredness, shoulders dropping while chewing. You believe loosening proves cure, and sighing persuades, because tired bodies love sighs. The loosening proves pause and sitting and quiet, not cure. The hum underneath was charged all day by the same loud answers now praised for muting it. Take the day's loud answers away in thought and the hum thins. Keep them and praise the mute, and the hum returns louder within the hour, asking for more muting.
You tell me the sigh must count for something, and sighs move you, because you are kind to your own tiredness. Count it true. Count it as relief from pinch, not as gift. True comfort leaves the wearer quieter for hours, like water to thirst, like sleep to tired eyes, like sitting to aching feet. This comfort leaves the eater louder within the hour, listening for the drawer, planning tomorrow's wage of foil. Quiet marks true answers. Return tickets mark borrowed ones. The sweet always holds a return ticket.
What you took through all these years for help at low hours was never a genuine pleasure, comfort or lift. It was pinch then sigh, dose then debt, noise then call for noise. Once seen, the comfort claim cannot stand beside the clock. The shoes come off for good when the pinch is seen as pinch. No one mourns tight shoes. Feet remember floors.

Wanting Without Liking

Watch the packet after the second square, because you believe wanting proves liking, and pursuit flatters the pursued. Here pursuit proves the opposite.
You open the bar for taste after supper eaten to satisfaction, meat and bread and vegetables and water, hunger gone by every honest signal. The first square tastes sweet on the tongue. Sweet taste can please the mouth for a moment. I claim no otherwise. Mouths know sweet from bitter from birth. Pleasing the tongue for a moment is small and simple. The claim under trial is larger: that the bar pays and comforts and lifts the whole person for the evening. Mouth moments cannot carry whole evenings. Meals carry evenings. People carry evenings. Rest carries evenings.
By the fourth square the tongue reports less and the hand reports more. By the sixth the tongue reports little and the hand moves fastest. You believe stronger want must mean deeper joy, because we chase gold and love with just such heat. Ask your own mouth as witness. Did the sixth square please more than the first? Did the tenth taste better than the second? Did midnight find you happier than the first bite promised? There is one plain answer that fits all three, and you already feel it. Wanting grew as liking thinned. The hand kept moving after pleasure stopped reporting. That extra reaching is the signature of the loop, not the voice of joy.
The fact is wanting and liking parted company long ago in this habit. Liking lives in the mouth for a minute and tells truth about taste. Wanting lives in the learned pull toward the shelf and tells truth about schooling, not about joy. Schooling ties hard hours to foil across a thousand evenings until the hour itself seems to call. The call sounds like need because it borrows need's tone. Need ends when met. This call returns after meeting, a little louder, a little earlier the next day. Ending marks true answers. Returning marks the loop feeding itself.
Let me put plain questions, and you answer inwardly with the candour you already showed me. If the bar truly paid you for the hard day, why does the paid evening ask for another payment before sleep? If the dish truly comforted the ache, why does the comforted ache knock again at ten and again at midnight? If the sweet truly held love, why does it leave you counting foil in the bin while love sleeps undisturbed in the next room? There is one plain answer that fits all three, and you feel its weight without any scolding from me. The occasion paid you. The sweet rode along.
You believe your evenings differ because your strain runs deeper, and pride lives in you, because you carry heavy loads well. I honour the loads and the carrying. Nurses and drivers and teachers and clerks all bring me the same nine o'clock story, different kitchens, same light, same door shelf stocked against the hour. You believe shared ache proves shared cure, and nature talk rests minds, because nature cannot be helped. Shared timing here proves shared schooling. The shelf stands in every kitchen at the same hour, and hands trained by the same years reach at the same minute. Take the stocked shelf from the kitchen in thought and leave all else, the same hard day, the same quiet house, the same aching shoulders, and the same minute grows quieter in memory. Hands still ache. Eyes still blink. But no bell rings from the door shelf, because no bell stands there.
That is the sugar trap. It dresses a learned want in the clothes of love and pay and comfort, sets it where tired hands reach, and calls the reaching proof of worth. Strip the clothes and the want stands thin, a small tug that passes with breath and rest and warm drink and talk. Dress the hour in its true givers, people, rest, music and sunlight saved from the day, and the tug finds no hook. Hooks need schooled sentences to hold. Unschooled hours hold no hooks.

The Occasion Paid You

Gather the rooms together, because you believe scattered doubts change nothing and gathered demands change everything, and resolve suits you. The bakery doorway gave you a hand and an hour, not a button. The payday kitchen gave you pay and rest and pride, not twists. The nine o'clock light gave you quiet and sitting and breath, not a bar. The family table gave you faces and songs and sufficiency, not glaze. Each scene claimed BAD SUGAR authored what the scene carried. Each scene, read plainly, shows the scene carried and the sweet attended.
You believe at least one scene must belong to the sweet alone, and caution lives in you, because clever men oversell. Keep caution and aim it straight. Name the scene where no people, no rest, no music, no sunlight, no pride, no hunger met and left satisfied attended, where foil alone made the hour. Midnight foil alone leaves coating and tossing and bin foil. Drawer foil alone leaves counting and hiding and heavier Fridays. Vending foil alone leaves sticky fingers on a keyboard and eyes duller within the hour. Solitary foil never throws a party. It only texts its friends and fills the house with guests who eat past enjoyment. Company, rest and meals throw parties. Foil only attends and claims the photographs.
You believe memory will always read the old way, and permanence saddens, because long readings feel fixed. Readings change when credit changes address. Each time the bakery bell rings in memory, let the hand hold the glow. Each time payday lands, let rest hold the crown. Each time nine o'clock pales the hall, let lamplight hold the soothing. Each time candles warm the table, let faces hold the love. The sweet keeps its true size, a loud minute on the tongue, pleasant while it lasts and gone when it goes, never the author of evenings. Small pleasures need no mourning when large ones stand revealed. Large ones stood there all along, waiting for their names back.
Stop asking what the sweet gives the moment and ask what the moment gave without asking, and the whole reward turns its coat without any strain from you. People paid. Rest paid. Music paid. Sunlight saved from a walked street paid. Hunger met and left behind paid. The sweet rode along and called itself driver. Drivers take hands off the wheel and the cart keeps rolling. Passengers who called themselves drivers get left at the kerb when seen.

SUMMARY
- I now believe reward lived in people and rest and I see the sweet only rode along.
- I now believe comfort was pinch then sigh and I read the loosening as debt cleared, not gift.
- I now believe wanting outlived liking and I know the extra reaching marks the loop, not joy.
