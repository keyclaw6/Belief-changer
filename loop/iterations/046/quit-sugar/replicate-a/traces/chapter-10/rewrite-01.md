Chapter 10
MADE TO WANT

The white coats taste before you do. Small white cups in a row, numbers on the base, spoons rinsed between sips, a quiet room with no music and no hunger in it. A man at the head of the table watches faces, not mouths. A little more. A little less. A little more again until the face softens at the middle cup and hardens at the last. He writes the number down. That number will ride in lorries to your street. It will sit on the shelf where your hand passes at three o'clock. It will sit on the birthday table where love sits. You believe the wanting starts inside you because you feel it inside you, and feeling inside looks like proof of origin.

IN THIS CHAPTER: a tasting room with white cups, an aisle that never ends, small print that hides, old papers kept from daylight, what the load costs the body, a birthday table where love sits, and three weeks lived plain.

*Your sweet tooth was built for you long before you felt it as yours.*

THE ROOM WITH WHITE CUPS

We all picture craving as something private, a small voice from the belly asking for sweet, because private feels like nature and nature feels like truth. Walk with me into the room where that private voice was rehearsed before you ever heard it. Men and women in plain clothes sip and spit and mark papers. They are not looking for the sweetest cup. The sweetest cup loses every time. The face pulls back from it. The mouth says too much. The middle cup wins, the one where sweetness sits high but no single flavour shouts loud enough to tell the brain to stop. You believe middle must mean mild because middle sounds like moderation. Middle here means maximum eaten before the stop signal fires.

Ask your own mouth as witness, and answer plainly without scolding yourself. Why does one biscuit call for the second before the first is swallowed, while a ripe peach closes with its skin and its stone and leaves the hand quiet. Why does chocolate confectionery vanish by the handful while the same mouth tires quickly of honey straight from the spoon. Why does the cinema box empty in the dark without a single remembered bite. There is one plain answer that fits all three without any blame on you. The winning formula was built to avoid the distinct overriding flavour that tells the brain to stop, so the hand keeps moving while the tongue keeps waiting for a finish that was removed on purpose.

I stood in no such room myself, and you believe that must distance me from it because distance should keep hands clean. It kept me nowhere clean. I ate the number written in that room for years and called the number my taste. My taste never chose the number. The number chose my taste by arriving first in every shop and every tin and every drawer, arriving early in childhood when the bakery smiled and arriving hourly in adult life when the afternoon thinned. We lived this together, you and I, reaching for what we called liking while the cup that won in that quiet room won again in our kitchens. The liking never led. The formula led and the mouth followed and called following choosing.

The fact is desire here was manufactured by formula, not discovered in the eater.

Sit with that sentence a moment in the body, because the body already knows it. Think of the sweet you call your favourite, the one you would name if a friend asked. You believe favourite proves inwardness, because favourites live in people and people hold them dear. Ask where favourite met you first. It met you on a high shelf at eye level, in a bright wrapper that hissed and shone, beside twenty cousins wearing different colours over the same middle sweetness. It met you at school and at work and at birthdays, offered by kind hands, praised on screens, priced to move. Favourite never grew from inside like a tooth. It was placed from outside, repeated until repetition felt like nature. That is the sugar trap.

THE MELT THAT NEVER WARNS

Hold a piece of chocolate confectionery on the tongue with me and do not chew, and you believe melting proves lightness, because light things melt and heavy things sit. Watch melting from the inside and lightness wears a mask. The piece thins to nothing in seconds, leaves almost no chew to remember, no fibre to pause on, no bulk to tell the mouth where the meal stands. The brain counts little because there was little to count, while the dose has already passed. You believe vanished must mean nearly nothing, because vanished looks like absolved. Vanished here means uncounted, never unfelt. The hollow returns louder for the vanishing, and the hand answers louder.

Ask the packet years as witness, and answer inwardly with the candour you already showed me. If melt meant light, why did light evenings end heaviest, with wrappers hidden for morning and coating on the tongue and the three o'clock pictures brighter the next day. If the food truly read as nearly calorie-free, why did the hand never read itself as done. If pleasure drove the eating, why did eating continue past enjoyment into duty, bite after bite with duller taste and quicker hand. There is one plain answer that fits all three. The food was built to be hard to stop and to trick fullness, fast-melting combinations rarely found in nature that trigger the reward signal hard and disrupt the quiet that tells a plain meal it has closed. Wanting outlived liking because the build kept want fed while liking thinned.

You tell me, "I have a sweet tooth," and I hear how physical it sounds, because teeth sound like body and body sounds like fate. Teeth chew. What calls here is not teeth. It is the whisper turned shout, natural rewards whispering while engineered fast hits shout into the same ear at higher dose and faster rate. A whole orange eaten at the table closes with chew and pause. The same sweet engineered to melt closes nothing. It opens. The paid actor looks like the hero in that opening hour, arriving like relief, bringing you back daily for another take while wearing the clothes of comfort. The haze filter sits over all of it, tasting life through comparison with the last bar so plain food reads dull beside remembered shout.

The fact is the melt was designed to leave no witness except want.

THE AISLE THAT NEVER ENDS

Walk the supermarket slowly with me at seven in the evening, that slow aisle walk we all know, and you believe the shelf proves choice, because many colours look like many minds and many minds should mean freedom. Count with me without hurry and choice thins. Biscuits, cakes, pastries, ice cream, chocolate confectionery, sugary drinks, sweetened cereals, flavoured yoghurts, among others, row after row wearing different names over the same middle sweetness. About two in three packaged foods on those shelves carry the hyperpalatable build, sweet with fat or salt in combinations the orchard never grew. You believe ubiquity proves popularity, because many shelves should mean many wants met. Ubiquity here proves placement, not popularity. The want was stocked before it was felt.

Lift a plain-looking packet with me and read the small print without blinking, and you believe careful shopping must work, because labels look like facts and facts should guide hands. Labels here hide while they show. Added sugars ride under many names, syrups and crystals and concentrates taking turns at the top so no single word looks large. They ride inside biscuits and chocolate and flavoured yoghurts and cereals and fizzy drinks, a wide family, never one shelf. For years the showing of added beside total stayed new and partial, so the careful eye counted one name while three others walked past. Counting never failed for lack of care. It failed because the fuel was never where the eye was taught to look.

You tell me, "the industry only meets my natural tooth," and I take the sentence gently, because it arrives wearing the clothes of sense and every screen repeats it. If the tooth were natural, why did it need armies of tasters and meetings and bright wrappers and eye-level shelves and cartoon tigers and holiday tins to wake it. If the meeting only followed want, why did want rise exactly where spend rose, street by street, year by year, mouth by mouth. If the shelf only answered, why did the answer arrive before the question in every childhood, bakery first, catechism after. There is one plain answer without any scolding. The tooth was schooled. The school built the room with white cups, stocked the aisle that never ends, printed the maze in small print, and called schooling appetite.

The fact is the shelf taught the belly what to ask for.

WHAT WAS KEPT FROM DAYLIGHT

Open the old drawer with me, the one with papers yellow at the edges, and you believe confusion proves science is slow, because slow looks honest and honest should be trusted. Read the dates with me and slowness wears a mask. In 1965 a review that discounted the links between sucrose and heart trouble was funded by the very trade that sold sucrose, and the funding stayed off the page while the doubt stayed on it. Years later a project that fed sucrose and watched triglycerides climb and saw signals worth chasing was terminated without publishing, the papers filed away while the shelves stayed full. When teeth rotted, the strategy papers talked of everything except eating less of the stuff, deflection printed as concern. You believe you chose with full knowledge, because adults should choose informed and informed should mean free. Full knowledge never reached your hands. Confusion was profitable while clarity would have cost.

I tell you this not to plant bitterness in you, because bitterness still looks backward and I want you looking out. I tell you this to lift the weight you carried for years off your shoulders and set it where it belongs. You were not weak in a fair market. You were a lone mouth against tasters and printers and funders, conned with warmth due to you as a person, never defective. Pity the years you blamed character for what was design, and never envy the design. Anger belongs at the design, never at you and never at the kind hands that offered cake. The hands loved you. The formula used the hands the way it used the shelves, as carriage.

Ask what changes when the lights come up in that drawer, and hear it plainly. Nothing in your mouth changes. Everything in your mind changes. The same packet that looked like your friend now looks like a brief hired to keep you picturing between doses. The same ad that looked like celebration now looks like instruction. The same doubt that sounded like your own caution now sounds like a sentence written for you in 1965 and repeated until it felt like thought. You do not need to fight the drawer. You only need to see it once. Seen traps lose their teaching power, because teaching needs trust and trust needs darkness.

The fact is you were kept in the dark by design, and daylight ends the lesson.

WHAT THE LOAD COSTS

Stand with me a moment at the body truth, stated plainly at full strength, because soft words here would cheat you. Higher added sugars ride with higher heart and stroke trouble across large populations followed for years. Overload converts to fat, raises pressure, keeps chronic inflammation lit. Liquid sugar bypasses the appetite control that chew and bulk engage, landing fast and calling fast. The same treat that feeds the loop feeds acid against teeth, free sugars to plaque acid to decay, and decay of that kind remains the most common noncommunicable condition across the world. Lift one treated bottle and do its plain arithmetic with me, because numbers here are daylight. One twelve-ounce cola holds thirty-nine grams, almost ten teaspoons in one hand, nearly spending in minutes the whole daily budget the world's health guides sketch for a full day.

Hear me now with the same plainness, and let your shoulders drop as you hear it. I do not want you to change from fear, and fear never walked anyone out of here. I tell you the load so the lift stands revealed as deferred load, not free energy, and the method takes care of the seeing while the seeing takes care of the walking. You have nothing to lose by reading on, and a whole life to gain. The body you live in already knows how to run its steady supply without spoon-by-spoon imports, manufacturing what it needs from plain food while holding its line. It never asked for a fast glass to think clearly. The glass only taught the three o'clock hollow to arrive on time.

You tell me, "but I feel fine," and I hear the relief underneath, because fine feels like pardon and pardon feels like permission to keep one. Fine today never priced the load carried for every day from now on while the loop repeats. The loop charges by repetition, dose by dose, year by year, pictures between doses wiring deeper while mouths call wiring liking. Ask the mornings as witness, waking heavy and foggy and irritable after loud evenings, crust in the eyes while the pillowy lure waits for afternoon to shine again. That heaviness never proved you broken. It proved the load landing where loads land. Seen plainly once, the sweet stops looking like a small joy with a small price. It looks like a loud bill for a quiet minute.

The fact is the lift was never free, and knowing the price sets you to escape, not to tremble.

THE TABLE WHERE LOVE SITS

Come with me now to the strongest room, the birthday dinner, because we will not sneak past it. Warm light, plates close, laughter over the day, a rich cake carried in with candles leaning and everyone singing a little off key. The host holds the first slice toward you with love in the eyes and a grin about being no fun for refusing. The table goes quiet for half a second. You take the slice. You take the mousse when it comes because the spoon is already in every hand and finishing looks like belonging. You believe refusing would refuse the people, because love holds the plate and plates look like love. Love here holds nothing but itself. The sweet only rode in on its carriage.

Watch that hour piece by piece with me, and keep tenderness for every face round the table. The glow you remember never came from icing. It came from people gathered and paused, from work stopped and music low, from being seen and seeing, from sun through blinds and rest from screens and talk that runs past eight. The sweet billed the hour for its riding and sent the bill afterward, a loud hit after unfed hours, a hollow by evening, pictures the next day. You would have glowed the same with an empty hand and a full plate of real food, because glow came from gathering, never from glaze. It never was a genuine pleasure, comfort or lift. It was people, rest, music and light, with a rider clinging behind.

You tell me, "but that cake is my most beloved," and I hold the sentence with both hands, because beloved sounds like identity and identity should not be stripped. Ask beloved where it lived across years, and answer without flinching. It lived at every birthday and holiday tin and office send-off where love gathered thickest, arriving exactly when the heart stood most open. Beloved never proved the cake kind. It proved the moments kind and the cake present. A song heard at every wedding sounds like love itself until you hear the song alone in a cold kitchen at midnight past enjoyment, coating on the tongue, finishing to avoid standing out while nobody watches. Played alone, without people and music and pause, the song thins to sugar and fat and middle sweetness with no stop signal. The love stays with the people. Only the rider gets demoted.

Pity the table still counting who took and who passed, and never envy it. The colleague lifting a second slice with a laugh about deserving one after a hard week is not indulging where you are missing. He is feeding the same hollow that will thin his afternoon light and call him past enjoyment by night, while you sit inside the same love with clearer eyes. Hold your cup with warmth when the joke lands. Meet the grin with a grin.

Stay in your seat while the plates begin to move, because you believe the dangerous minute comes after the joke, when hands have something to do and yours do not. Watch how little danger is in it once you see it plain. The host stacks two plates and reaches for yours. You hand it across with both hands and thank her for the meal, and you mean the meal, the chicken and the greens and the potatoes roasted hard at the edges, eaten with appetite among voices. You believe thanks will sound thin without praise for the cake riding beside it, because praise for the sweet used to be the toll paid for the rest. Notice how no toll is asked. She takes the plate with a nod and a smile that has nothing to prove, and turns to the sink with the stack rattling soft against her wrist.

Remain at the table while the cloth fills with crumbs and candle wax and the small wreckage every shared meal leaves. You believe staying will expose you, because in the old years staying meant watching others eat what you told yourself you could not have while pictures pulled hard behind your eyes. That was the school talking, not the evening. Pictures feed on belief, and belief has changed. You watch the knife laid across the empty cake stand and the icing smeared on the server, and you know those smears hold no orders for you. They are washing-up now, nothing more. Your cup is still half full. You lift it and drink. The talk has already moved on to who burned the gravy last Christmas and whether the train strike will hold through Friday.

When the second servings come you will see how ordinary passing can be. The host wipes her hands on the tea towel and lifts the stand again toward your end of the table. Have a little more, she says, there is plenty. The uncle beside you holds out his plate for a second corner and laughs about saving room since Tuesday. All eyes swing toward you for half a breath, kind eyes, curious eyes, not cruel ones. You believe this is the moment where belonging gets weighed, because the old voice taught you that love is measured in mouthfuls taken together. Answer with warmth and without a speech. No thank you, I had plenty, that chicken did me. I am happy with my tea. Say it the way you would refuse more potatoes when you have had plenty, with the same face, the same weight. You need no story about sugar and no apology for health, because a story would make the sweet important again and an apology would make your chair smaller. A plain thanks keeps your chair the same size as every other chair round the table.

See what happens in the breath after you pass. Nothing breaks. The stand moves on. The uncle takes his corner. The girl opposite takes the cherry from the top because someone has to, and everyone laughs. You believe the laugh leaves you outside, because outside is where the old method always put you, alone with rules while others shared. Stay inside the laugh and check. You laugh with them about the cherry, you ask if it was worth the fight, she rolls her eyes and says always, and the talk rolls forward without a gap where you used to be. No one leans away. No one marks your plate with a look. The man who made the joke about being no fun reaches across and refills your cup from the pot without thinking, the small unthinking kindness people show when you are one of them. You believe you must earn such gestures by eating what they eat. You earn them by being there, by listening, by answering, by staying.

Let the talk run past eight, because you believe the evening needs the sweet to hold it together past the meal, because dessert used to be the bridge between dinner and the rest of the night and without it the span would sag. Watch the span hold. The candles lean lower. Someone opens the window an inch because the room has grown warm. The host brings out the small cups nobody asked for and pours tea that tastes of smoke. The conversation loosens the way it always loosens when plates are gone and no one must be polite about chewing. Work stories come out. The neighbour tells the one about the parcel delivered to the wrong street and the dog that claimed it. You believe you will watch the clock and count minutes until you can leave with good grace, because counting minutes is what unfed hours taught you, waiting for the next picture to be answered. There is no next picture to answer. You do not watch the clock. You hear eight chime from the church down the road and are surprised, then you hear half past and do not move, because talk is still warm and your place in it is plain.

Take your part in it. You believe silence is the price of passing, because keeping your hands empty used to keep your mouth shut too, lest a word betray want. That bargain belonged to strain, not to this table. Ask the neighbour what happened to the parcel in the end. Tell the story about your train running forty minutes late with the guard apologising like it was rain. You know your voice sounds like yours, not like someone playing a part. Hands help here too. You gather the scattered spoons into a small pile so the host can lift them in one go. You move the empty glasses to the end where she can reach them. Small work, shared work, the kind people do when they belong and are not thinking about belonging. Nobody thanks you large. Nobody needs to. The work is its own place at the table.

When goodbyes begin you will learn what the old fear never let you learn. Coats come out of the bedroom piled high on the bed. Children are lifted half asleep onto shoulders. The host stands by the door with her cheek ready, tired and bright the way hosts look when a meal has gone well. You believe her cheek will cool toward you, because you gave back the slice she offered with love and love refused must turn cold. Walk up and test it. Thank her for having you, for the chicken, for getting everyone round. She holds your arm a second longer than custom and says I am glad you came, you must come Sunday, and means it. Her eyes hold no ledger of bites. You see love intact where the old voice promised a frost. The uncle claps your shoulder on the way out and says drive safe. The girl with the cherry waves with one hand while holding her father's hand with the other. You wave back. Belonging walks out of the door with you, not away from you.

Step into the night air with empty hands and notice what your hands do not do. You believe they will argue all the way home, because homeward used to be the corridor where pictures grew loudest, the kitchen waiting dark and still with the day's restraint behind you and the packet waiting to finish the evening. That corridor was built by gaps, and there is no gap tonight. You ate. You drank tea. You laughed past eight. The drive smells of rain on warm stone. The bus hums low. The walk home clicks under your shoes. You think of the cherry fight and smile again. You believe a smile after a birthday must be followed by a hollow, because sweetness used to bill every hour and send the account due at night. There is no account due. Your stomach sits settled under your coat. Your mouth tastes of tea and roast, not of coating. Your hands stay in your pockets, not from effort but from having nowhere they need to go.

Come through your own door and put the kettle on the way you always do. You believe the kitchen light will feel unkind tonight, because kitchen light at night used to mean the raid, the fridge glow, wrappers kept from morning, eating past enjoyment to finish the packet. Turn the light on and see. The room is only a room. The bread sits in its bag. The fruit sits in its bowl. The plates from breakfast sit washed on the rack. You pour water and wait for it to boil and feel no pull toward the cupboard where the old packets lived. You know this is not strain holding you up. It is the absence of orders. The smears on the cake server at the party held no orders. The tin on your shelf holds none. The evening did not wind you up, so there is nothing to unwind. Drink your tea standing by the window and look at the street lamps. Go to bed when you are sleepy.

Waking the next day tells you what the old calendar never told you. Mornings after celebration used to open heavy, with a thick head and a sour mouth and the small shame of hiding for morning, the foil crumpled, the box light. You believe one evening must still charge you the next day, because the body was said to keep score in craving and craving was said to be fate. Notice the scorecard blank. You wake and your mouth tastes only of morning. You make breakfast and eat it with appetite. The thought of last night arrives, and with it no bright pictures pulling toward a repeat, no three o'clock rehearsal starting early. You see the birthday table the way you see a film the day after, faces and jokes and the cherry fight, not a glowing slice turning slowly in the dark behind your eyes. The tin at work, the shop shelves, the drawer at home stay in their places, printed and stocked as before, but the shout in them has thinned to print. The middle sweetness that won in that white room wins no prize in you. The melt that left no witness leaves no instructions. The small print that hid three names for one dose is only small print.

Ask the afternoon as witness, because three o'clock was always the courtroom. The hour comes with its old props, the tired eyes, the long screen, the kettle clicking on in offices down the hall. You believe pictures must brighten here, because this was the hour the bell rang loudest and the hand answered quickest. Sit through it with your ordinary work and watch. The bell does not ring. Or it rings so faint from another street that you hear it as weather, not as orders. You drink water. You eat your lunch. You answer the small wanting signal the way you answer a doorbell in an empty house down the road, heard, not obeyed. You know the difference now between hunger that asks for food and pictures that ask for repetition, and pictures without belief are only pictures. By four the hour has passed the way hours pass when no dose lands loud after waiting and no hollow answers after. No hiding is planned for night. No pardon is drafted for morning. The day ends the way plain days end.

Kindness never lived in icing. It lived in staying, in listening, in laughing, in hands held round the same table. The people stay loved. Only the sweet steps down.

The fact is love never needed the slice, and the slice never was love.

THREE WEEKS LIVED PLAIN

I want to tell you plainly how the turn came for me, not as a timeline for you to follow but as one mouth lived through to the other side, because I tied every string you tied and cut none by strain.

I was forty-one, two children, a desk job with the drawer, a freezer with a foil square behind the peas dated for visitors. I ate BAD SUGAR the way the calendar taught, clean through good intentions and loud on freed days, eighty and more loud days a year without ever counting them until I counted backward. My afternoons thinned toward three with pictures brightening hourly. My nights finished past enjoyment with hiding for morning. My mornings woke heavy with coating and fog. I believed all of it proved a tooth in me, deep and natural, because private craving feels like nature.

The first days without the loop felt strange in the way any quiet house feels strange after a loud tenant leaves. I kept opening the fridge and scanning without hunger, standing in cold light looking for the bright thing that used to answer hours. I lifted an empty wrapper to my nose one evening without thinking and the smell pulled harder than any bite had for months, sweet and warm and immediate, and I watched my hand half rise before thought caught it. That is when I understood with my body what no sentence had given me. The pull fired before the bite, smell and wrapper and hour calling louder than eating ever did. Pictures feed the loop as surely as mouthfuls. I set the wrapper down and ate a plain supper to satisfaction and the call thinned within the hour like a bell heard from another street.

By the second week the scanning thinned. I ate plain meals unhurried, chew and pause telling the meal where it stood, and the hand forgot the kitchen for hours in a way I had not known since childhood. Fruit tasted sweeter, not as a sentence someone told me but as a morning fact, an apple cracking loud with juice running, the tongue finding finish where it once found thinness. The haze filter had lifted without my pushing it. The afternoons held on plain food in a way the calendar years never held, one hour following the next without the loud dip and the louder talk.

In the sixth week a tin came to work, the same celebration tin with the same bright lid, office send-off, paper plates, the joke about being no fun. I took a cookie with the others to sit inside the love, not outside it. I ate two bites standing with a cup in my hand. The first bite tasted bright and loud, the middle sweetness shouting where the apple had whispered. The second bite tasted duller, coating already forming, the hand already moving toward a third while the tongue asked for less. I set the half cookie on the plate and forgot it through talk and laughter, found it later cold and curled, and left it for the bin without triumph and without loss. That night there was no raid, no hiding, no heavy morning pen. There was a closed meal and sleep and a plain breakfast eaten with appetite. I had not proved strength. I had watched the most beloved fail to deliver once the shout stood beside the whisper and the filter stood removed.

I tell you this not as a promise of days but as proof of kind. The craving quieted because the belief feeding it quieted. The taste returned because comparison stopped shouting over it. The cookie left unfinished because liking never lived in it once want stopped driving. What was built in rooms with white cups and stocked aisles and small print and old papers could not survive daylight and steady living. Daylight showed the builder. Living showed the lie.

The fact is the beloved sweet, met again with clear senses, cannot pay what love pays.
