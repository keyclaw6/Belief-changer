Chapter 7
THE LOW THAT CALLS ITSELF A HIGH

**IN THIS CHAPTER**
A belt worn too tight all day, the late-night bite to the morning rattle, bright supermarket aisle of wrappers

*The lift was never a lift at all; it was relief from the low the last dose left behind.*

### The Day That Eats Itself

You know the ease of a real meal that finishes by itself. We have seen the afternoon flare leave us flatter, the evening reward belong to people not wrappers, and hunger quieten when food comes.

Now follow one ordinary day of the fix and watch what it really does.

Last night the box or the bar. This morning the mouth a little furry, the head a little thick, an empty, slightly edgy hunger that feels like it needs something sweet before breakfast is even thought of.

By eleven the hands are restless. By three the drawer opens to get me through. There is a bright ten minutes. Then the flatness again, heavier. By nine the whisper is back: “just a treat, I’ve been good this week.” One sliver becomes the row. I just crave sweets and when i eat them, i feel like i cant stop. Midnight promises, morning rattle, and round again.

Ask, and answer as the investigator you now are:

When the lift is real fuel, why does it need refuelling an hour later?
When the sweet fixes the low, why does the low always return a little deeper?
If that roller coaster is energy, why does a child who ate no fix run all afternoon without it?

There is only one honest answer. The dose does not fill the hole. It digs it.

That is the sugar trap working in plain sight: a low created, a low briefly covered, a low guaranteed again.

### The Belt

Wear a tight belt all day on purpose.

Cinched hard from morning. Ribs pinched. Breath shallow. Stomach squeezed. Bear it through meetings, through traffic, through supper. Then at midnight, in private, unbuckle it an inch.

Ah. Relief floods you. You sigh. You tell yourself loosening belts is one of life’s great pleasures.

Would you believe you? Would you wear the belt again tomorrow for the joy of loosening it?

We laugh because the trick is naked. Yet that is exactly what we do with BAD SUGAR.

Each dose tightens tomorrow’s belt. The blood rushes up, insulin rushes to meet it, the surge overshoots, and you sink below where you started — tired, flat and craving, not because life is low but because the last fix made it low. The next bite loosens what the last bite tightened and takes the thanks for the rescue.

It is the other way around.

The high is not a high. It is the sigh of the belt let out one notch by the same hand that buckled it in the morning.

Once seen, it cannot be unseen. No dose ever lifted you above the normal of a person who never started. It dragged you down and then let you climb halfway back, and called the climb a pleasure.

There is no pleasure in loosening a belt you never needed to wear.

### What The Body Goes Through

I want to tell you plainly what happens, and then tell you why there is nothing to fear in it.

When sugar arrives in bursts — a binge in the evening, a graze after restriction, a sweet to get through — animals given that on-and-off pattern do what we do. They binge. They chase. They show a brief rush in the wanting circuit, then a measured dip after, with the opposing signal rising as the rush falls. Given free and constant access, they do not show that pattern. It is the intermittence, the restrict-then-binge rhythm, that builds the escalation.

That rush re-arms wanting. Understand this clearly because it matters: wanting is not liking. The hit does not make life sweeter. It makes the next hit more wanted. That is why you can need the family size to get the same brief quiet and still say it did not even taste that good. Taste never drove it. The dip drove it.

The dip itself is real and small. After the binge there is a true low in that wanting circuit, a little emptiness that complains. In rats made to stop suddenly it can be precipitated and measured. The lab that mapped it most closely calls what it sees mild but well-defined. Mild. Well-defined. Already dying.

You must hear both words. Real enough to feel as that edgy rattle. Small enough to starve out in days, not weeks, not months. I am not calling it a human disease and I am not calling you ill. I am saying your body tells the truth: a small whine follows a big spike, and we were taught to misread the whine as need.

Scientists argue fiercely about the word to put on all this. Some say addictive, some say not, some say only binge under that yo-yo schedule counts. Let them argue. Both camps grant what you need: under that on-and-off pattern, bingeing happens, cues fire before hunger, and needing more while enjoying less fits exactly. The label is contested. The loop is not.

Your own body already manufactures the steady fuel it needs from ordinary food. No sweet completes eating. The days-long hump when the bursts stop — flat for two to five days for many, then clearing — is the Crumb complaining as its food stops, not your body failing. It passes. It is time-limited and small, and on the other side clean hunger and fullness speak again.

And now the hard part, said flat because you deserve it straight.

At population level, heavy added-sugar habits travel with a heavier load: hearts and vessels strained through liver overload and pressure and appetite bypassed, teeth rotted because free sugars feed the acid that pits enamel, and everyday intake running far above what global guidance suggests — keep free sugars below ten percent of energy, below five for extra benefit. These are not lectures about you. They are what the heavy habit is linked with out there in crowds and clinics.

I do not want you to stop from fear of that. Fear never freed anyone. I tell you so you see the lift was never free energy; it was deferred load dressed as energy. And the marvellous news is this: stop feeding the loop and the load stops growing, the mouth rests, the guidance takes care of itself without counting or fearing. You are escaping a con, not running from a sentence.

That small whine is the Crumb starving. The story that told you the whine means need was fed to you by the Confectioner. Keep that separation and you have the whole mechanism.

### The Aisle That Wants For You

Walk hungry — or not hungry at all — down the bright aisle of wrappers.

Lights clean and white. Row after row rustling. Smell pumped sweet from the bakery corner. Golds and reds shouting. Before your stomach has said a word, your hand is already half out. Your mouth waters. A picture plays: evening sofa, cinema row, birthday laughter, the bite that will settle the day.

That is not hunger. That is cue.

The wanting circuit learns places and packets and hours. Wrappers, smells, times of day can fire wanting without any bodily need, and with BAD SUGAR the learning is fast because the spike-dip teaches hard. This is why you can feel stuffed from supper and still be pulled to the cupboard at the advert break. The aisle fires first. The stomach never got a vote.

Like a friendly helper who tripped you in the dark and then loudly lifts you up and takes your thanks, the fix plays rescuer to a fall it arranged. Once you see the trip, you stop thanking the hand.

So meet the aisle thought for what it is: a thought. Not a command. Not hunger. Not love. A learned flare, already fading if you do not feed it. You do not have to fight it or answer it. You only have to see whose voice it is.

It is not yours. It never was.

### The Craving Is Not Proof

“I need something RIGHT NOW or I can’t settle.”

“I can’t stop once I start, so I must need it.”

“Don’t keep it in the house — that proves it’s stronger than me.”

I hear you, and I am warm to you. Of course craving feels like proof. That is what we were all taught to believe.

But ask:

If craving proved need, why does it vanish ten minutes into a real meal?
If one bite proves you cannot stop, why can a person who never started walk past the whole aisle without a flicker?
If the sweet were love or fuel, why does it leave you tired, flat and craving every single time?

Craving proves only that the last dose left a low and the old story misnamed the low. The low is real. The meaning we gave it was false.

That edgy emptiness is not your body crying for fuel. It is the dying whine of a tiny parasite that lived on bursts. That whine is the Crumb asking for its next feed. The grand tale about treat and reward and deserve-it and once a week is fine was never your wisdom. That story was fed to you by the Confectioner — by every advert, every table, every kind hand that offered love in a wrapper until you mistook the wrapper for love.

Be harsh with them, never with yourself. You were conned, not weak. You fell into the sugar trap as we all did, on false information, and you stayed because the schedule kept the whine alive and the story kept the meaning alive. Starve the one, correct the other, and both die together.

That is why cutting down never worked. Intermittence is the fuel. Little bursts keep the dip-and-chase alive and make each hit feel more precious. There is no healthy level of BAD SUGAR as a habit other than zero, though your body can ride out an accidental blip as a belt copes with a jolt. The mind cannot keep a deliberate exception without waking the story, so we keep no exceptions on purpose.

You have come far enough to see it, and you have absolutely nothing to lose and everything to gain by seeing it to the end. Keep reading with excitement. Do not change anything yet. Let the low show itself for what it is.

7. SEE THE LOW AS THE TRAP’S WORK
It creates what it relieves.

**SUMMARY**
- The bright lift is brief relief from the dip the previous dose produced.
- Tightening then loosening never gave anything; the dose tightens and loosens the same belt.
- Bursts teach wanting and fire cues before hunger; wanting is not liking or need.
- The after-low is real and small and passes in days; the body keeps itself fuelled from real meals.
- Heavy habits carry deferred load for hearts and teeth; seeing that ends the free-energy story.
- Craving is the echo of the last fix plus an old story, not proof of need.
