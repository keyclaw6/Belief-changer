Chapter 10
THE SWEETEST TABLE

IN THIS CHAPTER
A birthday table with candles low, a dark cinema row, a wrapper rustle in a bright aisle

*The sweetness was in the people and the moment — the sweet only sneaked a ride.*

The wiring story is closed. The cut-down bargaining is closed. The on-off schedule that taught the binge is seen for what it was.

What remains is the one table you still guard. I understand it. We all guarded it.

THE TABLE YOU STILL GUARD

Picture it fully with me. Not a secret binge. Not a drawer at low ebb. Something warm.

A birthday. Evening light. Candles low and wavering. Faces you love leaning in close. Laughter spilling over itself. Someone slides the cake toward you and everyone sings, off-key and happy. Or the cinema — lights down, arm touching arm in the dark, the shared bag passed hand to hand while the story opens on the screen.

And the thought arrives, tender and fierce:

“But that is genuinely sweet. Surely the shared dessert, the cinema chocolate, is love itself. Take that away and you take the warmth away.”

I hear you. Of course you guard that memory. That is why you are still reading.

Let us look at it as investigators, not as mourners. I will not spoil the memory. I will return it to its owners.

Run that birthday again slowly in your mind. Where exactly was the joy? Was it in the white crystals on your tongue? Or was it in her laugh when the wax ran onto the icing? In the press of shoulders around a small table? In being known and welcomed? In the taste of real food eaten together while everyone lingered because no one wanted the night to end?

Ask it plain.

If the dose made the night, why do the happiest nights live in memory as faces, not flavours? If the chocolate held the love, why does the love stay sharp years later while the taste has vanished beyond recall? If the sweet created closeness, why do you feel closest in the laughter before the first bite and the talk long after the plates are empty?

The fact is this: that night was never a genuine pleasure, treat or lift from BAD SUGAR. That is the sugar trap at its sweetest — letting the dose sit in the middle of love so love gets blamed on the dose.

Strip the dose out and replay it. Same room. Same people. Same candles. Same real food enjoyed hungry together. What is lost? Nothing that ever loved you. The warmth stays intact because the warmth was never in the packet.

The dose only sneaked a ride.

THE DARK ROW

Now the cinema. The lights go down. You feel the seat dip as someone dear settles beside you. The anticipation. The private joke whispered before the titles. The hand finding yours in the dark.

We learned to say we were celebrating, rewarding myself, going to the cinema with chocolate, as if the evening needed a sweet ticket to count. We believed the bag made the film funnier and the company closer.

Run it twice.

First as we lived it: reach, chew, reach again in the dark without hunger, half attention on the screen and half on the rustle, then the flat edgy want for more before the middle of the film. Do you call that attention? Do you call that company?

Then without the fix: eyes on the story, shoulder warm against shoulder, real hunger quiet and then satisfied by an ordinary meal after, walking home talking about the ending, alive to the night air. Which version loved the film more? Which version loved the person beside you more?

You know the answer because you have lived both halves without noticing. The sweet never deepened the moment. It divided it.

You may know that bright aisle where wrappers rustle and smell fires wanting before hunger. That tug in the chest is not proof the film needs chocolate. It is a thought to reframe, not a force to obey — a cue learned bite by bite, firing before the body asks for anything. See it for what it is and it loses its voice.

Did the people laugh less because you noticed them fully? Did the story shine less because you watched it clear? Or is it the other direction entirely — that the dose stole minutes of presence and then took thanks for the presence that remained?

The love stayed. The dose added nothing.

SEE IT PLAIN

Let me show you how easily feeling can lie, with your own eyes open.

Think of that birthday table again. Hold the picture steady: candles, faces, noise.

Now keep everything and lift the cake’s sweet centre out of the picture. Keep the meal, keep the laughter, keep the late talk. Notice what your heart does. Does the tenderness drain away? Or does it stand, whole and warm, without help?

Now do the reverse. Keep the dose and lift the people out. An empty room. A box of sweet on a bare table under hard light. Eat it alone standing up. Is there any birthday left in it? Any love? Any cinema magic?

You have just felt it in your own body. Take the people away and the sweet is dead. Take the sweet away and the people are still everything.

That is not a trick of words. That is perception corrected by your own attention. If feeling can be so wrong about where the joy lives, what else has it misread? The lift that was a dip. The reward that rented affection. The comfort that created the ache.

Trust what you just saw more than what you were taught to feel.

WHAT I FOUND AT MY TABLE

I want to tell you what happened at my own table, because I guarded it exactly as you do.

For near twenty years I was the man who brought the big bag to the cinema and ordered the extra slice “for the occasion.” My daughter’s eighth birthday — small flat, too many children, paper hats slipping — I remember standing by the cake feeling oddly proud of my sweet tooth, as if it proved I knew how to celebrate. I ate until my teeth ached and called the ache joy.

I believed certain nights would turn grey without a fix. Christmas. Birthdays. Friday films with my wife asleep on my shoulder. I feared I would sit apart from love, watching others glow while I nursed water and envy.

Then the belief broke, and I did nothing but see.

The next birthday came round — my son’s, eleven of them shouting in our kitchen, candles leaning, icing sliding sideways. I ate real food hungry with them, laughed until my sides hurt, cleared plates sticky with small fingers. Halfway through I caught myself waiting for the pang of loss. It never arrived. What arrived was ease. I was not watching joy. I was inside it.

The cinema followed. Same dark row. Same dear weight against my shoulder. No rustle to manage, no counting how much was left, no flat reach for more before the end. I walked out clear-headed into cold air, talking fast about the story, hungry in a clean way for supper. My wife said I seemed more with her. She was right. I was.

That was years ago now. Births and deaths since. Good winters and hard ones. The freedom never wobbled because it was never held up by effort. It was held up by seeing. That voice that once whispered “just this once, it’s love” showed itself plainly as the Confectioner, the old story feeding a dying whine — and I could pity it without obeying it.

I tell you this not to impress you but to free you. If joy lived in the dose, my brightest nights since would have dimmed. They have grown brighter. People, moment, real food — they were always enough. They are more than enough when nothing divides you from them.

There is nothing honest in missing what never loved you back, and everything stable in keeping what always did.

NO OCCASION HOLDS IT

See where this leaves us.

If there is no benefit in the daily fix — no fuel, no reward, no comfort that is not theft — what hidden benefit could gather once a year in candles and make poison into love? A con does not turn true on birthdays. A thief does not turn generous at Christmas.

This is why totality feels safe now. Eat whatever real food you like, whenever you are hungry, and you will never miss BAD SUGAR. On that abundance, what is there to beg from one small allowed dose? The table is already full. The night is already sweet with living.

You do not need to defend love by feeding it what dims it. You do not need to prove belonging by sharing what harms every sharer. Love the people. Keep the moment. Enjoy the meal. Let the dose go with the rest of the trick.

You are ready to leave the last hiding place and step clear.

10. REFUSE THE SPECIAL OCCASION
No benefit often means no point occasionally.

SUMMARY
- The birthday and cinema warmth belonged to people, moment and real food, never to the dose.
- Removing the dose leaves the night whole, while removing the people leaves the dose empty.
- Cues that fire before hunger are learned thoughts, not proof of need and not orders.
- Freedom at bright times is steady because seeing holds it, not effort.
- A year’s sweetest night cannot lend value to what has no value on ordinary days.
