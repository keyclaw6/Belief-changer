Chapter 9
NOWHERE LEFT TO HIDE

IN THIS CHAPTER
A sunlit cliff edge, a seatbelt on an ordinary road, a table cleared of tired excuses

*You are not built to need it — you were caught to want it, and what caught you can be seen and left.*

You have seen it now. The brightness after a dose was only the loosening of what the last dose had tightened. The Willpower Method had no finish line and could never have freed you. Both fears, of failing again and of a grey life without treats, belonged to the trap, not to you. Leave all that closed.

What is left is a handful of small voices. They sound like you. They are not you.

YOU ARE NOT WIRED — THIS WIRED YOU

“I’m wired this way. I have an addictive personality. It runs in my family. I just can’t control myself around sweet.”

I hear it kindly, because we all said it. It feels true in the moment the box empties in your hands. Let us look at it as investigators.

Did you arrive in the world demanding a fix at four in the afternoon? Did you need something RIGHT NOW before you ever learned that sweet would quiet it for twenty minutes? Do children raised without constant sweet crave in exactly your way, or did we learn the wanting together, bite by bite?

The fact is this: the traits we call proof of wiring are the rent paid for living in the trap.

Across large crowds, about fourteen in a hundred adults and about twelve in a hundred children answer yes often enough on food questionnaires to meet addiction-like scores for highly palatable food, much the same rate as for alcohol and tobacco — I speak of crowds here, not a verdict on you. Does that sound like a rare flaw in you? Or like a common con that catches ordinary people?

You are not uniquely weak. You were commonly caught.

Researchers still argue honestly about the word addiction for sugar. Teen trials that remove sweet drinks genuinely disagree with each other about which way the discomfort goes. What both sides grant is plain to anyone who has dieted: put sweet on an on-off schedule and bingeing follows. Restriction feeds escalation. The yo-yo is the form.

So turn it around. It is the other way around.

We did not binge because we were that type. We became that type because we binged, and we binged because the schedule taught us to. The roller coaster to get me through, the can’t stop once I start in the evening after been good this week — those are not character. Those are what intermittent fixes do to any appetite.

And let me give you the graceful truth: even if you did carry a hungry temperament, it would change nothing. A taught hunger ends when the teaching ends. Seeing the con returns sweetness to life itself. You do not need rewiring. You need escape.

THE QUICK OBJECTIONS, ANSWERED PLAIN

Now the rapid ones, in your own dialect. Hear each as it arrives, already labelled. That is the sugar trap talking, not thinking.

“But it’s just a treat. Treat when you feel you deserve one.”

Deserve what? Heaviness? The edgy flatness an hour later? Ask what good is there at all in the dose itself, apart from ending for minutes the edginess the last dose created. People, rest, laughter, pride in work done — those are the reward. The sweet only sneaked a ride on them. There is no reward in renting back for minutes what you already paid away.

“Once a week is fine. I’ll cut down.”

Come with me to a cliff edge at noon. Bright sun. Clean drop. A cheerful guide says, “Jump! Just fall a few metres. Be moderate about gravity.” Would you argue about technique? Would you ask for a safer day to jump?

You laugh because the offer is absurd. Cutting down on a con is the same absurdity. Moderation keeps the schedule alive, and the schedule is what drives the binge. The small allowed fix becomes precious, the precious fix fills the week with waiting, the waiting breaks on Friday night standing up in the kitchen. If there is no benefit in doing it often, there is no point in doing it occasionally either.

“Don’t keep it in the house and I’ll be fine.”

Whose house is the world? The aisle rustles. The drawer at work opens. A birthday passes. If freedom needed a locked cupboard, it would not be freedom. We do not need a smaller cage. We need out.

“I need something RIGHT NOW or I’ll crash.”

I know that edgy hunger well. A sweet surge sets insulin to work, and that same surge can leave a real after-dip — I speak of bodies in general here, not a sentence on you — yet most everyday slumps are felt without any measured low at all. The feeling is real. The reading of it is back to front.

Ask it plain. If sweet gave fuel, why are you flat again an hour later and reaching again? If the low proved need, why does the need return faster the more you feed it? There is only one honest pattern: the fix creates what it relieves. Your body makes its own steady glucose from ordinary food, so living without BAD SUGAR leaves no hole to fill.

“I can’t stop once I start.”

Exactly. Thank you for the evidence. One bite becomes the box because the first hit re-arms the wanting. Does that describe a free treat you enjoy? Or a triggered loop you suffer? You have just told me moderation cannot hold — and told me why it should never be asked to.

“I’ll stop tomorrow. Monday is better.”

If tomorrow is so fine, why not now? If sweet truly gave you something, why are you planning to lose it on Monday? Tomorrow is the trap’s favourite hiding place because tomorrow never arrives. The cost is not tomorrow’s box. It is every edgy afternoon between now and then, paid for something you already know gives nothing.

“What if I slip by accident?”

Think of a seatbelt there for an accidental jolt, never for driving erratically. Your body can cope with an occasional accidental blip. Your mind cannot keep a deliberate exception. The belt guards the accident. It never licenses the crash.

See them together and they vanish together. Each objection, heard plain, confesses the trap.

TOO EASY TO BE TRUE?

Last hiding place. The method itself.

“This sounds too easy. Real quitters suffer. My friend cut down by force. The internet says avoid all triggers and fight it daily.”

Of course they say that. Other quitters quit by suffering because they kept the belief and fought the wanting. They believed sweet was a genuine joy and then lived in denial of joy by force. No wonder they white-knuckle and count and warn you it will be hard. They never left the trap. They only decorated the cell.

Ask it plain. If suffering worked, would they still be suffering? If force freed, why are they still afraid of one bite? If their advice were true, why does it need your misery as proof?

Do you want to stop? Of course you do — that is why you picked up this book. Do you want to keep believing sweet loves you back after everything you have seen it do TO you and nothing FOR you? You know you do not. Will you let a voice that never escaped tell you escape is impossible?

There is nothing hard here once the belief is clear. The body echo, when regular doses end, is a few days of flat edginess at most, rising for two or three days and fading to nothing, already dying if unfed. Small. Passing. You walk through worse with a cold. The rest was never physical. It was story, and story ends when seen.

So hear conflicting advice for what it is: the old brainwashing trying to pull you back into the cell by pity and fear. Pity it. Do not obey it.

9. IGNORE ADVICE THAT CONFLICTS WITH THIS METHOD
Other quitters quit by suffering.

SUMMARY
- The hungry, out-of-control traits were rented from the on-off schedule, not built into character.
- Commonness across crowds proves an ordinary con, not a rare flaw.
- Cutting down keeps the schedule alive and makes the fix precious until it breaks.
- One bite carries the box because the hit re-arms the wanting, not because joy was found.
- Tomorrow never frees because the belief stays intact while days are paid away.
- Other sufferers describe the wrong method faithfully, and their struggle is no guide to escape.
