Chapter 7
THE LOW THAT PRETENDS TO BE A HIGH

IN THIS CHAPTER
A tight belt worn all day, a bright aisle of rustling wrappers, the full circle from last bite to edgy evening to next fix

*The high you felt was never a high at all — only the brief quieting of a low the last sweet created.*

You have felt real hunger quiet under real food. You have felt fullness arrive on its own. The afternoon lift stands dead — it did plenty TO you and nothing FOR you. The cinema pay stands dead — the warmth was people and moment, never the bar. Leave both closed. What is left is the last whisper that still sounds like need.

THE CIRCLE THAT CALLS ITSELF NEED

Follow one ordinary day with me, from the last sweet to the next.

Evening. A few biscuits after dinner, eaten half without tasting. Brief brightness. Then the television hour, a heaviness behind the eyes.

Morning. Mouth dry, belly not clean-hungry but unsettled. Coffee and something sweet to get started. Mid-morning lift, then the edge returns by eleven.

Afternoon. That drawer. The fix. Ten bright minutes. Then heavier flatness. Then the second reach you promised yourself you would not make.

Night. The cupboard, standing up. Regret instant.

Ask it plain.

Does the brightness arrive on a clean body — or only on an edgy one? Does a child fresh from play need that brightness to laugh — or do you need it only because the flatness came first? If the sweet truly lifted you, why are you always lower an hour after than you were an hour before?

There is only one honest pattern, and you have lived it a thousand times. Each dose lifts you a little, then drops you a little lower. The next craving is not hunger calling for fuel. It is the drop calling for relief.

That is the sugar trap in its purest form. It creates what it relieves.

THE BELT THAT HURTS TO RELIEVE

We all wore it.

Picture a man who wears a belt two holes too tight, all day, every day. At night he loosens it one hole and sighs with pleasure. “Ah, what relief. What comfort. I could not get through the evening without this loosening.”

Would you call the loosening a genuine comfort? Would you tell him he needs belt-loosening for energy? Would you let him fear life without the loosening?

Of course not. You would tell him to take the belt off.

We wore that belt around our appetite. Each sweet pulled it one hole tighter — a quick spike, then the sag, then an empty, slightly edgy hunger that feels like it needs something sweet. Each next sweet loosened it one hole and we called the loosening a lift, a treat, a rescue.

It was never a lift. It was a loosening of what the last sweet tightened.

Once you see it, you cannot unsee it. The low does not prove you need it. The low proves you had it. You were tired, flat and craving not because you lacked sugar, but because the last dose was wearing off.

It is the other way around.

THE AISLE THAT WANTS BEFORE YOU DO

Walk with me down the bright aisle.

Lights clean and white. Row upon row of colour. The rustle of packets. The smell of warm bakery pumped into cool air. Your belly is not hungry — you ate an hour ago — yet your mouth waters and your hand reaches.

Did your body ask? Did real hunger stir?

No. The wanting fired before hunger. The wrapper fired it. The smell fired it. The hour fired it. The place where you always buy fired it.

We built that. Or rather, it was built for us.

There are armies of clever people whose whole living is to make that rustle louder than your own belly. To find the exact crunch, the exact melt, the exact sweet-salt point that keeps the hand going back for one more. To put the big bag where your tired eyes fall at half past five. That engineered pull is not appetite. It is a cue, planted on purpose, firing a learned want.

And inside, a small voice answers the cue at once: “Need something RIGHT NOW.” “Just a treat.” “I can’t stop once I start.”

That voice is not you. That is the Crumb whining to be fed.

The Crumb is tiny. A trivial physical echo, a small dip after each binge that nags for a top-up and dies within days if unfed. Alone it could never hold you. What holds you is the story that feeds it — the Confectioner whispering that the whine means need, pleasure, reward, fuel. Starve the whine and pity the story, and both lose their power.

A friendly helper once tripped you in the dark, then loudly lifted you up and took your thanks. You would not thank him twice once you saw the foot. Do not thank the sweet for lifting the heaviness it put there.

WHAT THE BODY PAYS, PLAINLY SAID

I will give you the hard facts flat, because you deserve them clean, and then we will leave fear behind.

A habit of daily sweets loads the body it pretends to lift. Meal after meal of quick sugar batters the liver, nudges pressure and inflammation upward, slips past fullness so you eat more than hunger asked, and bathes the teeth in acid that rots them from the outside in — I speak of what heavy sweets do in bodies in general, not a sentence on you. Public guidance now says free sugars should sit below a small fraction of food, and most grazers live far above it.

That is not doom. That is arithmetic. The lift is borrowed, the load is kept.

And the low after is real. Each binge fires the wanting circuit hard — a brief hit that re-arms the next want — then leaves a small measured dip behind, wanting blunted and cues sharpened, so the family bag is needed where a square once did, and the sight of wrappers fires first. Laboratories can watch that rise and dip happen. They can watch intermittent treats drive bigger binges than steady plenty, which is why cutting down and being good all week only fattens the next blow-out.

Here is the honesty you are owed. Those laboratories watch animals, not you. Wanting is not liking — the circuit can shout “more” while the mouth enjoys less. And clever people honestly disagree on what to call it. Some say dependency, mild but well-defined and quickly over. Some say habit and learning, not that heavy word at all. Both sides grant what you have lived: under stop-start rules, the binge grows and the craving returns.

So take what is solid and leave what is argued. The grip is real. The grip is small. The grip passes. You do not need to fear it, and you do not need to change from fear. You change because there was never anything to keep.

If experts argue over the label, what does that tell you about the size of the physical part? If the whine were mighty, would they call it mild? Does a tiny, passing discomfort justify a lifetime of slavery rented as treat?

No. It justifies pity — pity for the trick, never blame for yourself.

THE MANUFACTURE OF DESIRE

Put the two halves together and the whole con stands naked.

From below, the schedule drives the binge. Forbid, crave, allow, binge, regret, forbid again. Each exception keeps the loop alive. Each “once a week is fine” keeps the Crumb alive to shout for twice a week.

From above, the story drives the schedule. The Confectioner taught us that sweetness lives in the sweet, that low means need, that edgy means fuel, that celebration means chocolate. So we misread the belt-tightening as hunger and the loosening as love.

From outside, the engineers drive both. Bliss-point crunch. Aisle light. Cinema smell. Packet rustle. They profit when you read a dip as a need.

Three ropes. One neck. And every rope is held by the same hands — not yours.

Does the craving prove you need it? Or does the craving prove the last dose is leaving? Does the aisle prove food is delicious? Or does the aisle prove wanting can be manufactured before hunger wakes? If sugar truly gave energy, why must you keep paying for energy you never keep?

You know because you felt real hunger yesterday — clean, calm, answered by food. This edgy gnaw is not that. This is the echo, fed by a story, triggered by a wrapper.

Keep looking, for you have absolutely nothing to lose and everything to gain by finishing this seeing.

7. SEE THE LOW AS THE TRAP’S WORK
It creates what it relieves.

SUMMARY
- Each sweet gives a brief brightness and then leaves a lower flatness that calls for the next sweet.
- The edgy urge is the wearing-off of the last dose, not the body asking for fuel.
- Cues of light, rustle and smell can fire wanting before any real hunger appears.
- The physical echo is small and passing; the story that calls it need keeps it alive.
- Heavy sweet habits load heart, body and teeth while pretending to lift; seeing the load removes the illusion, not joy.
- The low is made by the trap and sold back as a high; once seen, it cannot be mistaken for need again.
