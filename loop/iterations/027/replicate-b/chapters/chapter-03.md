Chapter 3
WHAT DOES IT DO FOR YOU

**IN THIS CHAPTER** — Morning wrappers on a cold table, the office drawer at three o'clock, the dentist's mirror and the shop till, a quiet page with two columns

*Forget how much it harms you and ask the harder question — name one true good it does you, and watch the page stay empty.*

### THE WRONG COURTROOM

You have already seen the first daylight.

Your hand moved before your thought in that late kitchen. The box grew light while you watched. That proved capture, never weakness. I hold you to that verdict because it is true, and because we need it for what comes now.

Most sweet lovers who admit the capture still keep one locked door. They say, very reasonably, very honestly:

"I know it rots teeth. I know it piles on weight. I know it costs money. But it helps me. It gives me something. Life would be greyer without it."

I hear that voice because it was my voice for years. I stood with crumbs on my shirt and said, yes, it harms me, but the benefit outweighs the harm. I balanced harm against pleasure like a careful accountant and always found a reason to keep paying. I told myself I was being balanced. I told myself I was being adult about it.

Let us leave that courtroom today.

Harm against benefit is the wrong trial. It goes nowhere. You can argue harm for years and never get free, because harm invites defence. Someone feels fine. Someone knows a thin sweet eater. Someone brushes well. Someone says, my grandmother ate cake daily and lived to ninety. Harm can be explained away. Harm makes you feel judged and lectured, and a judged person stops listening.

I am not here to judge you. I am warm toward you and harsh toward the fraud that caught you, never the other way around.

So I change the question. I put harm to one side, all of it, and I ask for the other side alone.

What does BAD SUGAR do FOR you?

Not what does it do TO you. We know plenty about that, and we will look at a little of it plainly in a moment. I ask now for the benefit. Name one real benefit, felt in your own body on your own ordinary day, that belongs to the dose and to nothing else.

You say, it treats me. You say, it fuels me. You say, it is a genuine treat or fuel in a grey day.

Good. Let us test those honest words against your own life. Your life is the evidence. Bring it.

If BAD SUGAR truly treated you, why did the treat leave you flat? If it truly fuelled you, why did the fuel leave you reaching again within the hour? If it made life sweeter, why did life feel less sweet after the packet was empty than before it was open?

Did you feel treated at the bottom of the box, or hurried and foggy with regret arriving before the swallow ended? Did you feel fuelled when the second reach came, or emptier than before the first bite? If the benefit was real, why did you need to repeat the benefit to feel normal again?

There is only one honest answer, and you already know it. The treat never treated. The fuel never fuelled.

We were all conned together, you and I, into calling doses by kind words. Treat. Reward. Fuel. Love in a wrapper. Those words were installed early, by kind hands and bright packets and singing adverts, long before we ever thought to question them. We believed sweetness was kindness and energy was in the packet. Brainwashed people are not weak. They are misled. Misled people do not need stronger character. They need daylight.

Think how strange that accountant in your head really is. He lists costs he can see — teeth, weight, money, time — and then writes one vague word on the other side to balance them all. Pleasure. Help. Treat. He never asks what that word weighed. He never asks when it arrived or how long it stayed. He accepts the packet's promise as payment. Would you accept that accounting from a builder? From a bank? You would laugh him out of the room. Yet with BAD SUGAR we all signed the book without checking the till.

I signed it for years. I wrote reward under Tuesday and comfort under Thursday and fuel under three o'clock, and I called the page balanced. I was not lying. I was misled. The words felt true because everyone I loved used them, because every celebration spoke them, because the shop glowed with them. Feeling true is not being true. A con lives on feeling true.

So close the harm book for today. Open the benefit book. One page. One question. What good, exactly, did you receive that you would miss if the trap had never touched you?

### A DAY ON PAPER

Take one ordinary day with me. Not a bad day. Not a binge day. A normal moderate day, the kind you call harmless.

Morning. Tea with two sugars, or a sweetened cereal that claims to be wholesome. The bowl tastes sweet and kind. Ten minutes later the mouth is dry and the mind has already moved on. Mid-morning, a biscuit with coffee because the drawer is there and everyone takes one. You do not even stand up to fetch it. The hand knows the way. Lunch, a sandwich and a fizzy drink, or a bun because you earned it after a hard morning. The drink is cold and bright. An hour later hunger arrives early and sharp, sharper than the work deserves.

Afternoon, the dip arrives and a bar from the machine answers it. Ten minutes of buzz in a grey meeting, then attention scattered, then the eyes heavy. Evening, dessert because the meal feels unfinished without it. A warm moment with the plate, spoons out, talk around the table. Then fullness without satisfaction. Night, a square of chocolate while you watch, then another square, then the row quietly gone while the programme plays on.

Write it down if you like, hour by hour, in two columns. Left column: the dose. Right column: the good it did.

Be exact, like an investigator who agreed to question what he thinks he knows about sweetness.

Nine o'clock dose: what good? Ten minutes of sweet taste, then mouth dry, then thought already moving to the next hour. Eleven o'clock dose: what good? A brief lift, then scattered attention, then hunger arriving early. Three o'clock dose: what good? Ten minutes of buzz, then fog, then a second reach at four. Evening dose: what good? A warm moment, then fullness without relief, then the dusk tug returning before bed.

Now add the other side, not to frighten you, but to see clearly. Mood: up then down then up then down, never steady, never calm. Teeth: acid bath after acid bath. Wallet: coins daily that run to notes weekly that run to a holiday lost yearly. Time: minutes queuing, unwrapping, chewing without tasting, smoothing packets, hiding wrappers, promising Monday. Sleep: late buzz then restless night, then the stale mouth at dawn. Morning: head dull, tongue coated, thought of sweet returning with coffee before the day has begun.

Look at your two columns.

The left column is full. Doses logged like trains arriving. The right column, the benefit column, is empty. Taste that fades before the swallow ends is not benefit. A lift that guarantees a low is not benefit. A pause that must be repeated hourly to feel normal is not benefit.

I speak as one who kept that same page. I wrote treat in the right column for years. I wrote fuel. I wrote reward for surviving Tuesday. I wrote comfort after stress. When I read the page honestly, none of those words described what my body felt. My body felt brief sweet, then flat, then want. My mind supplied the kind words. My body paid the bill.

Walk the day again slowly and watch the pattern you already know. When did you feel steady? Was it after a dose, or between doses when the noise went quiet for a little while? When did you feel clear? Was it with sweet on your tongue, or after a plain meal eaten hungry and left at enough? When did you feel proud? Was it while reaching, or in those rare hours when reaching never crossed your mind?

Ask yourself three plain questions over that page, and answer from your own day, not from my theory.

When the dose was in your mouth, did joy rise or did hurry rise? When the packet was empty, did you feel satisfied like the child with the peach who runs off to play, or restless and slightly sick and already thinking of more? If each dose gave something real, why did more doses leave you with less, never with more?

There is only one honest answer. The page proves it. Nothing was given. Much was taken.

That is your second verdict. Hold it beside the first. You were caught, never weak. And the catcher gave you nothing.

Keep that page on the table. We will need it. Every claim the trap makes from here must write its payment in that right column, in your handwriting, from your body. Vague words are no longer accepted. Treat must show itself. Fuel must show itself. Comfort must show itself. If it cannot show itself on an ordinary day, it does not exist.

### TEETH AND THE CEILING

Let me speak plainly about two hard facts, because you deserve daylight, not slogans.

First, teeth. Free sugars feed plaque acid and that acid decays teeth. That is mechanism, not moral. The guidance to limit free sugars exists because mouths across whole populations show the same pattern. Brushing and fluoride matter, and I do not dismiss them. They clean and protect. They do not change what the acid does when sweet hits bathe teeth five, six, seven times a day. A dentist sees it in the mirror before you feel it. Small pits. Soft edges. Fillings where smooth enamel stood.

Look at your own audit and count the baths. Morning spoon. Mid-morning biscuit. Midday drink. Afternoon bar. Evening dessert. Night chocolate. Six, seven baths. Each one brief. Each one forgotten. Each one leaving acid to work while you work. You do not feel it. You feel fine. Feeling fine misleads when harm warms slowly.

I say this flatly because it is true. I do not say it to frighten you into change, and I do not want you changing from fear. Fear never freed anyone. Fear makes the packet glow brighter as forbidden rescue. See the fact, then let fear go. We are here for freedom, not for fright. This is about the pattern across all of us, not a sentence on you.

Second, the ceiling. Public guidance sets a daily ceiling for free sugars, with a lower target for extra benefit, because ordinary intake sits far above both. The fact is, most of us live far above that ceiling while feeling fine and calling ourselves moderate. A can here, a bun there, a spoon in every tea — normal, kind, everywhere — and the total sails past guidance by noon.

I do not speak of poison in a single bite. I do not speak of one cake sealing a fate. I speak of load across days and years. The lift you feel is deferred load, never free energy. The body carries it, the liver carries it, the blood pressure carries it, the appetite control carries it, until carrying shows. Populations that carry it heavily show more heart and strain. That is population risk, not a verdict on you. You are a person reading a book, not a number in a table.

Do you see why feeling fine proves nothing? We all felt fine while the total climbed. We all called ourselves moderate while the drawer emptied weekly. We all believed normal meant safe because normal was everywhere. Normal was manufactured. Shelves glowed. Packets sang. Portions grew. The ceiling stayed where it was while intake walked past it. Feeling fine was the slow water warming. The frog never notices until the heat is habit.

Ask again, calmly, with the page before you.

If feeling fine meant intake was low, why did your wrappers fill the bin while you felt fine? If brushing undid the baths, why did the mirror still show pits in brushing mouths? If the lift was free, why did the afternoon always send you back to the drawer?

There is only one honest answer. Feeling fine misleads. The baths happened whether you felt them or not. The total climbed whether you counted it or not.

And now the question that ends the wrong trial and opens the right one.

Do you want to carry that load and pay that price for the rest of your life for something that never gave you anything?

Or do you want to walk clear and easy for the rest of your life, eating when hungry, stopping at satisfied, tasting real food with a clean mouth?

Both futures last the same length. Only one is freedom. Choose by benefit, not by fear. When fear whispers, let it pass. When benefit is asked for, let the empty column answer.

It does plenty TO you. It does nothing FOR you.

Read that verdict slowly. Let it stand in short sentences because truth is short. TO you: acid, load, cost, fog, repeat. FOR you: nothing you can name that survives your own day on paper. Once seen, it cannot be unseen. The accountant closes his book. There is nothing to weigh. The harm debate ends because the benefit side is empty.

### WHOSE HAND IS THAT

There is a picture I want you to hold for a moment, only as a question, because the full answer belongs deeper in our walk together.

What if a man pushed you into a ditch every morning, then came along at noon, held out his hand, pulled you out, and you thanked him with all your heart for the rescue?

Would you call him kind? Would you pay him daily for the service? Would you say you could not face afternoons without him?

Or would you see, in a flash, that the rescuer and the pusher were the same hands, and the gratitude belonged to daylight, never to him?

Ask that question over your three o'clock drawer. Who dug the low that the bar then lifts? Ask it over your evening sofa. Who sharpened the tug that the chocolate then quiets for ten minutes? Ask it over your morning tea. Who taught that tea without sweet tastes of nothing?

I do not ask you to answer fully tonight. I ask you to keep the question open. The empty benefit you logged is not accident. An empty benefit with a loud promise always has two halves: a tiny body echo misread as need, and a belief that reads that echo as gift and calls it treat and fuel. Name both halves now, briefly, and we will starve both by seeing them. The echo is small and passing. The belief is loud and learned. The belief feeds the echo. The echo seems to prove the belief. Round and round we went, you and I together, thanking the hand that pushed us.

Does a hungry child feel that narrow echo, or an open call that a meal answers? Does a free eater thank a packet for returning him to normal, or eat and stop and get on with enjoying his life? The hand you thank is the hand that pushed. Once seen, gratitude ends. Freedom begins.

### THE EMPTY COLUMN IS FREEDOM

So stand with me at your table and look at the page again.

You entered this chapter conceding harm but guarding benefit. You said, it may harm, but it helps. You called it a genuine treat or fuel because everyone you love used those words and because the echo in your body seemed to confirm them.

Look at the page your own day wrote. Mood yo-yo. Teeth acid. Wallet lighter. Time lost. Evening tug returning. Benefit column empty from morning to night.

Speak your last defence with me, out loud, so it loses privacy.

You say, but I enjoy the taste. Good. Taste is ten seconds on the tongue. Do ten seconds explain hours of want? You say, sugary somethings get me through. Through what? Through a low that arrived after the last sugary something? You say, I have managed to be good this week, so I deserve one. Does goodness need acid to prove it? Does love need a wrapper to speak? You say, celebrating with chocolate is different. Is the warmth in the chocolate, or in the laughter and the lights and the people you love?

Answer from your page, not from the packet's promise. Did taste satisfy or sharpen? Did getting through free you or chain the next hour to the same drawer? Did the deserved one close the day with relief or open the night with negotiation? When the room was warm, who made it warm — the dose in your hand, or the life around you?

There is only one honest answer. The benefit was never in the dose. The pleasure you remember was the moment, the pause, the company, the rest after work — life itself, with a dose sneaking a ride and taking the credit. Credit belongs to you and to your life, never to BAD SUGAR. It is the other way around from what you were taught.

I do not ask you to fight anything. I do not ask you to clear your cupboard or count or confess. I ask you to see. Seeing is enough at this hinge. A caught person who sees the empty column no longer begs the catcher for kindness. A misled eater who sees the kind words were installed no longer obeys them as his own reason. The tug narrows. Hunger widens. The tug hurries. Hunger waits. The tug never says enough. Hunger says enough with relief.

Keep living exactly as you do while we talk. Keep your meals. Keep your shops. Keep your table. We change the belief first. Behaviour follows on its own when belief dies, without strain and without loss.

For now, I lay the rule for this hinge, to keep your mind in the right courtroom while we walk. Follow it fully, as part of our contract.

3. JUDGE ONLY BY WHAT IT DOES FOR YOU
Forget harm versus benefit and ask for the benefit.

**SUMMARY**
- The harm debate keeps you defending, while the benefit question ends the trial because no real benefit survives your own day.
- A normal day logged hour by hour shows doses arriving like trains and the benefit column staying empty from morning to night.
- Sweet hits bathe teeth in acid through the day, and ordinary intake sails past guidance while feeling fine, which proves feeling fine misleads.
- The brief lift guarantees the next low, so gratitude toward the dose mistakes the pusher for the rescuer.
- The remembered glow belonged to the pause and the moment and your own life, never to the packet that rode along and took the credit.
- Once the benefit column is seen as empty, continuing has no gift to offer and stopping has no gift to lose.
