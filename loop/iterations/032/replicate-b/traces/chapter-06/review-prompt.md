Chapter 6
FOOD AS FAVOURITE AGAIN
*Hunger met with real food is joy itself, and you lose no joy when BAD SUGAR leaves the plate.*

THE GREY PLATE YOU FEAR

You have seen the lift for what it is. You watched the afternoon brighten for ten minutes and cloud for hours, and you know the brightness was relief, not power. That was hard sight, and you held it kindly.

Now a quieter fear steps forward. It does not shout about energy. It whispers about joy.

It says: very well, the lift is false, but food itself will turn grey. Meals will be duty. Evenings will be flat. A life without sweet hits will be a life of chewing joylessly while other people glow over dessert.

I hear that whisper because we all heard it. We lived in it for years. We called dessert love and sweet our favourite food, and we pictured a table without it as a table with the colour drained out.

Let me meet that fear with warmth, not with scorn. Of course you fear it. You were taught it. Every birthday, every cinema, every tired evening taught you that pleasure lives in the sweet coat and plain eating is the price you pay to stay alive. If that teaching were true, escape would feel like loss.

It is not true.

I promise you as a fellow eater who once believed it wholly: eating without BAD SUGAR is not greyer. It is brighter, warmer, funnier, more alive. Hunger returns as appetite. Satisfaction returns as arrival. Real food tastes the way food tastes when the mouth is clean and the body is calling.

You suspect a play on words here. I can hear you say it, word for word as I said it:

"You mean my favourite food is now cabbage? That is a trick. You are redefining pleasure and hoping I will not notice."

I am not. There is no catch. There is no word game. The pleasure is real, bodily, unmistakable, and it was yours before any packet taught you to doubt it.

Ask yourself plainly, as the forgiven investigator you became:

If sweet made meals joyful, why did meals grow joyless the more sweet you ate, with picking before dinner, grazing after dinner, and no meal ever feeling like arrival?

If dessert is love, why did the most loving tables of your life leave you stuffed and restless and half-absent, thinking of the tin while loved faces talked?

If plain food is grey, how did you eat plain food hungrily as a small eater and run off laughing, with no counting and no gloom?

You know. Of course you know. Joy never lived in the coat. Joy lived in hunger met.

HUNGER IS THE INVITATION

We have forgotten hunger. That sounds strange, but it is true.

For years we never let hunger arrive. We dosed before it could speak. Mid-morning tug, we dosed. Afternoon dip, we dosed. Evening restlessness, we dosed. Night mouth, we dosed. We called every tug hunger and fed every tug sweet, and then we wondered why we never felt the clean, keen, happy emptiness that makes food marvellous.

Real hunger is not pain. Real hunger is invitation.

It starts as lightness. A hollow ease in the belly. Attention sharpening toward smells. The mouth watering at bread crust, at broth, at an apple cut open. Thoughts of food arrive bright, not frantic. The body says: I am ready. Come and eat.

We all knew this as small eaters. We played hard, we grew empty, we ate fast and with delight, and we stopped when delight thinned. No lecture taught us. No clock taught us. The body led.

Then the wheel taught us a different signal. The head tug. The mouth itch. The edgy cannot-settle that arrives an hour after a dose and screams for sweet RIGHT NOW. We named that hunger. It never was hunger. Hunger builds slowly and is satisfied by food. That tug builds fast and is satisfied by nothing for long — not even by the sweet that summoned it.

Learn the difference once and you own it for life.

Hunger says: I could eat. The tug says: I must have something sweet.

Hunger waits for a meal and grows kindly. The tug cannot wait and grows nasty.

Hunger is satisfied by bread and soup and fruit and leaves you clear. The tug is fed by a fix and leaves you calling again within the hour.

Hunger belongs to you. The tug belongs to the trap that dug the ditch and sold the rope.

Feel hunger again and you will feel what I mean faster than any sentence can prove it. Skip nothing. Punish nothing. Eat when the body calls, not when the clock or the packet calls. Let the belly empty a little between meals. Walk a little. Work a little. Play a little. Let appetite gather the way dusk gathers — gradually, naturally, without force.

Then eat.

You will notice what the sweet eater never notices: the first mouthful of plain food when truly hungry tastes vivid beyond any coated memory. Bread tastes nutty and warm. Butter tastes clean. Potato tastes earthy and sweet in its own honest way. Greens taste bright. Fruit tastes like sun held in skin. Chew. Pause. Breathe. Taste again. The body says yes, yes, yes with every bite, and the mind is nowhere else — not in the cupboard, not in tomorrow, not in regret. Only here, in mouth and belly and company.

That is not duty. That is feast.

And satisfaction — real satisfaction — is just as kind.

It does not arrive as stuffed stupor. It arrives as ease. The edge leaves hunger. The brightness leaves taste. The belly feels settled, warm, grounded. Food loses its electric glow and becomes simply good, then enough. You put the fork down naturally. You breathe out. You want to talk, to laugh, to move, to get on with the hour. No war. No bargain. No promise to be good tomorrow. Only done, glad, free to live.

When did you last feel that? Not full to bursting. Not empty and grazing. Satisfied. Finished. At peace with the plate.

You were meant to feel it every day.

A MARKET MORNING EATEN HUNGRY

Let me take you somewhere ordinary, because freedom lives in ordinary places, not in theories.

Come with me on a Saturday morning. No plan, no rules, no lecture hanging over you. You slept well enough. You wake a little empty — pleasantly empty, the kind of empty that makes the day feel possible.

You walk to the market. The air is cool. Stalls piled high. Deep green leaves flecked with water. Tomatoes warm from the van, smelling of vine and sun. Peppers glossy as toys. Herbs that perfume your hands when you touch them. A baker's table with loaves cracked open, crust singing, crumb steaming faintly. A fruit man calling out, cutting a peach for tasting, juice running down his wrist.

You buy what draws you. Some vegetables for roasting. A loaf of fresh bread, still faintly warm. A little cheese, olives, eggs if you like them. Peaches, apples, berries — what the season offers. Nothing measured. Nothing forbidden. Nothing earned. Only food that looks alive and smells like itself.

You carry it home hungry. That matters. You do not pick on the way. You let hunger build while you chop, while the oven warms, while bread is torn and set on the table. The kitchen fills with smell — oil and garlic and roast edges, fresh crust, cut fruit. Your mouth waters. Your belly calls. Company drifts in, or quiet keeps you, both are fine.

Then you sit and eat.

Notice the first bite. Truly notice. Crust that cracks then yields. Soft inside, faintly sour, deeply savoury. Roast vegetables caramel at the edges, soft in the middle, salted just enough. Fruit after — cold peach, sharp berries, juice on the chin. Chew slowly. Talk between bites. Laugh. Reach again because appetite says reach, not because a packet says keep moving.

Notice the middle of the meal, where the sweet eater never looks. Taste is still good but calmer. Hunger is softening. You eat a little slower without deciding to eat slower. You drink water and it tastes wonderful. You feel warmth spreading, steadiness returning, attention clearing.

Notice the end, which is the whole lesson. There comes a moment when food stops glowing. The next bite would be pleasant but not needed. The belly feels settled. The mind turns outward — to the day, to people, to light through the window. You stop. Not from virtue. Not from fear. From arrival. The plate is not empty and you do not care. The body is satisfied and you can feel it.

Sit in that minute. That minute is the treasure the wheel stole.

No fog follows. No sticky mouth calls for a chaser. No edginess builds by three o'clock. Energy stays level, mood stays kind, hands stay quiet. An hour later you are still clear. Two hours later hunger has not screamed. When it returns, late, gently, you will welcome it again.

Tell me truly: was anything missing?

Was the meal grey? Was joy absent? Did you need a sweet coat to make bread marvellous, or did hunger make it marvellous?

Reverse the picture and see clean. Take the same morning dosed the old way. Sweet coffee on waking. Juice and biscuit by ten. Bread eaten while already half-full and half-foggy, tasted barely, followed by picking, followed by stupor, followed by the 3pm ceremony. Same market. Same loaf. No joy. Why? Not because bread changed. Because hunger never arrived and satisfaction never could.

It is the other way around.

FAVOURITE WITHOUT A TRICK

Now let me answer your suspicion head on, because I respect it.

You think I am playing with the word favourite. You think I mean: you will learn to like plain food the way prisoners learn to like walls — by necessity, not by delight.

I mean nothing of the sort.

I mean your favourite foods, eaten hungry, will delight you more without BAD SUGAR than they ever did with it.

Think of your own favourites stripped of coats. Roast chicken with crisp skin. Stew that has bubbled for hours. Fresh fish with lemon. Pasta with oil and garlic and green leaves. Ripe mango. Warm bread. Soup eaten tired and cold. Breakfast eaten after sleep and hunger. These are not consolation prizes. These are the foods human bodies were built to celebrate.

Why did they fade? Not because you outgrew them. Because the mouth grew numb and hunger grew confused.

When every day carries five or six sweet hits, taste buds learn to shout to be heard. Subtle tastes vanish under the blast. Bread tastes like cardboard after chocolate. Fruit tastes sour after cola. Soup tastes thin after cake. You conclude plain food is dull. The dullness is not in the food. It is in the coated mouth judging clean food by coated standards.

Clear the coat and taste returns within days. Not by effort. By absence. The tongue wakes. Water tastes sweet in the morning. An apple cracks loud and floods. Bread sings. You do not force appreciation. Appreciation forces itself on you.

And hunger seasons everything. The hungriest meal is the tastiest meal — every traveller knows it, every child knows it, every worker who ever ate lunch late knows it. We never needed to improve food. We needed to arrive hungry.

So no, this is not: learn to love grey. This is: arrive hungry and find colour waiting.

Hear the voices that will try to pull you back, because they sound like common sense until you test them:

"Fruit is sugar too, so nothing matters. You may as well eat sweets."

Does that survive honest questioning? If a peach and a bar were the same, why does the peach eaten hungry satisfy and quiet the hand, while the bar eaten full excites the hand and empties the box? Why does one leave clear attention and settled belly, the other fog and second reach? One is food with water, fibre, scent, chew, meal and company inside it. The other is a dose engineered to light want and lay down the dip. Your body knows the difference even when slogans pretend there is none.

Natural sugars inside whole fruit, vegetables and plain meals are not the target here. They belong to the menu of real eating. When this book says sugar, take it to mean BAD SUGAR — refined and added free sugars and junk snack-carbs eaten as hits. An apple is not a hit. A meal is not a fix. Hunger knows. Satisfaction knows.

Hear the second voice:

"I have managed to be good this week, so I deserve a sweet. Food is reward."

We will meet celebration fully later. For now notice only this: if sweet were reward, the rewarded weeks would feel best. Do they? Or do rewarded weeks end flat, foggy, promising Monday again? Reward that punishes is not reward. It is a loan dressed as a gift.

Real reward feels like arrival, not like debt. A meal eaten hungry after good work. Fruit shared laughing in sun. Bread torn at a table of friends. The body says thank you and means it. No after-shadow. No secret wrapper. No vow.

Ask the questions that close the grey-plate story:

If plain eating is grey, why do the clearest eaters you know — hungry workers, playing children, holiday walkers — glow over plain meals without a single packet in sight?

If sweet makes food joyful, why did your own joy in food shrink as sweet grew — with less tasting, more chasing, and no meal ever enough?

If favourite needs a coat, why does the coat please less with every bite while hunger pleases more with every return?

There is only one honest answer. Hunger is the sauce. Satisfaction is the feast. The coat only hid both.

Credit the true cook and the verdict lands by itself: it was never the dose that made eating wonderful. It was you, hungry, meeting real food, in life, with people. The dose only ever sneaked a ride on your appetite and claimed your delight as its work.

LOVE WAS THE MEAL, NOT THE COAT

You call dessert love. Many of us did. We say celebrating with chocolate, treat when you feel you deserve one, sugary somethings to get me through. The words are tender. The feeling behind them is tender. I will not mock tenderness.

I ask only that tenderness be credited truly.

Think of the evenings you call love. Not the packet — the room. Warm light. Tired bodies softening. Someone you love across the table or beside you on the sofa. Talk loosening. Laughter coming easier. Relief that the day is done. In that room a sweet appears and the moment glows. You conclude the glow came from the sweet.

Isolate the variable and test it honestly.

Was the warmth in the sugar, or in the pause, the company, the rest, the laughter, the being held without demand? Would the room have been cold without the packet? Have you never laughed till tears without sweet in your hand? Have you never felt deep love over plain soup when someone ill needed feeding, over bread shared on a walk, over fruit offered to a child? Of course you have. Love never needed a wrapper.

I remember my own kitchen years ago. Dinner cooked while picking at something sweet, love waiting at the table while I stood at the counter eating in secret haste. I told myself the picking was love of food. It was not love. It was tug, fed standing up, tasted barely, hiding the appetite that would have made dinner joyful. Dinner then half-enjoyed, company half-joined, evening half-lived. The sweet did not add love to the meal. It stole appetite from the meal and attention from the people.

See how the frame reverses:

It is not that sweet makes company sweet. Company makes sweet seem sweet, by lending its glow for minutes.

It is not that dessert makes the meal. Hunger makes the meal, and dessert laid on top of blunted hunger only fogs the arrival.

It is not that you love sweet food the way you love people. You love people and moments, and sweet rode along and claimed the credit.

Keep every tender thing. Keep birthdays. Keep cinemas. Keep tables loud with friends. Keep cooking for those you love. Keep being cooked for. This escape forbids no joy and shames no sweetness found in real food and real company. What leaves is only the dose that muted taste, confused hunger, and sold back your own love as its gift.

Imagine the same celebration without the hit, appetite intact. Lights low, film funny, room warm, laughter shaking shoulders, hand held or shoulder leaned on. shared fruit, shared bread, shared meal earlier, eaten hungry and finished satisfied. Attention on faces, not on wrappers. No rustle drowning dialogue. No sticky chase after. No regret before swallow ends. Morning after clear, tongue clean, memory whole. Is anything lost? Or is everything kept except fog?

You do not lose love when BAD SUGAR leaves. You get love back undistracted.

YOUR BODY ALREADY KNOWS HOW

Here is the plain bodily truth that makes all this possible, stated flat so fear cannot use it.

Your body makes its own steady glucose from plain meals, hour by hour, without spikes, without crashes, without begging. Bread, vegetables, fruit, protein, ordinary fats — eaten hungry and stopped at satisfied — supply what blood and brain need in the body's own timing. No dose required. No top-up ceremony required. The steadiness was yours before the first sweet taught you to doubt it, and it waits underneath the noise to return.

I speak of bodies in general, not a verdict on you — if you live with diabetes or take anything that affects blood sugar, are pregnant, or carry any medical risk around food, your clinician's word comes first and this book is belief change only.

Feel what that means. Eliminating BAD SUGAR is not deprivation of fuel. It is removal of noise. The machine does not stall without hits. It steadies without them. Hunger sharpens. Satisfaction deepens. Taste brightens. Sleep settles. Afternoons hold. Not by rules. By return.

You do not need to learn a new way to eat. You need to let the old way — the first way — breathe again.

Eat what you want, when you are hungry, stop when you are satisfied, and never punish yourself again.

Read that again slowly, because it is not a slogan. It is permission and description at once.

Eat what you want — real food that draws you, meals that satisfy, fruit that calls in season, bread warm, vegetables bright, what your household loves, what your appetite asks for when clean. No good-food sermon. No bad-food shame. No eating plan. No counting. No moral ledger.

When you are hungry — not when the head tugs an hour after a fix, not when the clock says graze, not when edginess says dose. When the belly lightens, the mouth waters, attention turns gladly to food.

Stop when you are satisfied — when glow softens into ease, when hunger quiets, when the next bite would be habit not appetite. Put the fork down. Breathe out. Get on with enjoying your life.

And never punish yourself again — no pay-back fasts, no guilty extras, no Monday sentences, no secret eating and public virtue. Punishment was the wheel's guard. Kindness is freedom's ground.

Do you feel how different this is from every diet you ever served? Diets said: eat less than hunger asks, stop before satisfaction arrives, punish lapses with tighter rules. That war taught the circuit to binge and taught the heart to hide. This says the reverse: trust the signals the wheel confused, and they will clarify faster than you dare hope.

Test it in the smallest way and watch.

Tomorrow, let yourself grow truly hungry for one meal. Not starved. Not faint. Only clearly ready. Then eat plain favourite food slowly, noticing. Then stop at satisfied, even with food left. Notice the hour after. Clear head. Settled belly. Quiet hands. Kind mood. That hour is not willpower. That hour is nature, visited once, promising to visit always.

We fell into the trap together, dosing before hunger could speak and calling the tug appetite. You will walk out of it by the same door we all walk: hunger, meal, satisfaction, life. No mighty battle. No grim vigil. Only signals trusted until they sing again.

So here is my sixth instruction:
TRUST HUNGER AND SATISFACTION
Eat when hungry, stop when satisfied, enjoy real food.

Carry that gladly into kitchens and markets and tables from today on. Not as a rule to obey with clenched teeth. As a kindness to inhabit with open mouth and easy belly. Let hunger invite. Let food delight. Let satisfaction finish. There is no grey plate waiting. There is only the eater you were before the coat, hungry again, satisfied again, marvelling how wonderful plain things taste when you meet them clean.

You entered fearing food joy was sweet and life without hits would be grey. You leave having sat hungry at a real table, tasted bread and fruit vivid, stopped at ease, and felt the hour after clear. With appetite returned, you can look without dread at the last whispers of the trap — and smile as they fade.
