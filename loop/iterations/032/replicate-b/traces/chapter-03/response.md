Chapter 3
WHAT DOES IT DO FOR YOU
*Forget how much it harms you — tell me one thing it truly does for you, and watch the glow disappear.*

YOU ARE CAUGHT. NOW WE ASK THE RIGHT QUESTION

You know now you were not weak. You were caught.

That changes everything about blame. It changes nothing yet about desire. You still feel the pull on a tired afternoon. You still hear the old story whisper that whatever the cost, there must be something in it for you, or why would a sensible adult keep reaching with a full cupboard and a full mind?

I honour that whisper. I do not brush it aside. You are a capable person. You do not stand in a bright kitchen at ten o'clock for nothing. If you believed there was zero in it for you, you would not go back a second night, let alone a thousand nights.

So we ask the honest question together. Not as judges. As investigators with the same evidence in our hands.

Most sweet eaters I meet want to argue about harm. I know the script by heart because I spoke it for years:

“Is it really that bad? My grandad ate cake and lived to eighty. I brush my teeth. I am not that bad. Others eat far more. I only have a little. I have cut down.”

I understand that argument from the inside. While we argue about harm, the trap stays safe. Harm can be haggled. Harm can be balanced. Harm can be postponed until tomorrow, until after the birthday, until after the holidays. Harm lets the old story survive with one sad sentence that sounds reasonable and keeps you hooked for years:

“All right, it may harm me a little — but the benefit outweighs it.”

Do you hear your own voice in that sentence? I hear mine.

Let me show you why that sentence is the lock on the cell, and why we will lay it down today and pick up a far more dangerous question.

Harm is the wrong battlefield. On harm, the con can always bargain. One thin friend who lives on biscuits. One headline that says sugar is fine. One good blood test. One week of being moderate. Doubt returns and the hand returns with it. Benefit is the battlefield the con cannot survive. If there is no benefit at all, what is there to balance? What is there to miss? What is there to pay for?

STOP WEIGHING HARM AGAINST BENEFIT

Let me be plain with you, as one escapee to another.

I am not asking whether BAD SUGAR does more harm than good. I am asking — what good is there at all?

Read that twice. Feel the shift in the floor beneath you.

Harm asks: how heavy is the price? Benefit asks: what did you ever buy?

You were taught to hold scales in your mind. On one side, teeth, weight, wallet, low mood, foggy afternoons, hours nibbled away. On the other side, pleasure, comfort, lift, reward, love. And you were taught the second side wins, just about, just enough to keep reaching.

What if the second side is empty?

Think of a fraud trial. The court does not ask whether the thief spent some of the stolen money on flowers for his mother. The court asks: did he have a right to take it in the first place? Kindness done with stolen goods does not make theft kind. We do not weigh. We demand the right.

Your body is the courtroom. BAD SUGAR stands accused. We will not weigh harm against charm. We will demand proof of charm.

You say: “But I enjoy it. That must count.”

Does it? What exactly do you enjoy? When does the enjoyment arrive? How long does it stay? What happens inside the same hour after it fades? Those are investigator questions, not insults. A true benefit survives them. An illusion dies under them.

I am not asking you to fear BAD SUGAR. I do not want you to change from fear. Fear never freed anyone for long. It only makes you clutch harder at the very thing you fear losing. I want you to see clearly, so freedom arrives as relief, not as dread.

And to see clearly, you need evidence from your own life, not from lectures. So let us audit one ordinary day together. No change in eating. No heroics. Only honest looking while you carry on exactly as normal.

THE DAY THAT TELLS THE TRUTH

Take yesterday. Not a bad day. Not a binge day that shames you. An ordinary, moderate, respectable day. The kind you would defend to a friend.

Morning. Tea with two sugars, or a sweetened cereal you call light. Mid-morning biscuit with coffee because work drags. You call that small. A few sweets from a shared bag because they were there. You call that nothing. Afternoon chocolate bar to get through the dip. You call that needed. Evening pudding because the meal feels unfinished without it. You call that normal. Night handful from the box while the house sleeps. You call that a slip, but a small one.

Write it down with me in two columns. Not harm versus benefit. TAKING versus GIVING.

In the TAKING column, be brutally honest for once. Time lost queueing, unwrapping, chewing, hiding wrappers, thinking about when the next one comes. Money leaked coin by coin, week by week, year by year — add it and feel how the small becomes huge across a lifetime of smalls. Mood swung up for minutes, down for hours. Teeth bathed again and again in sweetness through the day. Sleep made lighter. Hunger confused until every tug feels like emergency. Mind busy with when and how much and whether you were good.

That column fills fast. You know it does. You lived it.

Now the GIVING column. What did BAD SUGAR actually do FOR you yesterday?

Did the morning dose build a clean, steady energy you could still feel at noon? Or did it lift you briefly and leave you looking at the clock by eleven, already thinking about the next?

Did the mid-morning biscuit make the work joyful? Or did it pause the drag for ten minutes and return the drag with interest, with a fog on top?

Did the afternoon bar make you sharp and bright? Or did it make you buzzy, then flat, then reaching again by four, with a second reach close behind the first?

Did the evening pudding make the meal loving? Or was the love in the food, the company, the hunger met, the laughter at the table — with sweetness only riding along and claiming the credit?

Did the night handful give you peace? Or did it give you urgency while chewing and regret before swallowing ended, and a vow for tomorrow that tomorrow broke?

You know. Of course you know — you were there for every hour of it.

Ask the trap questions whose only honest answer sets you free:

If each dose truly helped, why did yesterday need so many? If the first dose gave, why did it create hunger for the second? If sweetness satisfied, why did satisfaction never arrive?

A meal satisfies and interest loosens. A real pleasure leaves you fuller and calmer. A true friend leaves you steadier than he found you. BAD SUGAR leaves you emptier and more driven after each hit. That is not the signature of a gift. That is the signature of a taking machine.

We all lived that audit. We logged the doses against mood and teeth and wallet and time — and the benefit column stayed empty.

Look closer at the pattern across the hours. Morning promises power and delivers restlessness. Midday promises passage through work and delivers a pause that charges interest. Evening promises reward and delivers agitation. Night promises closure and delivers a box left open and a mind left busy. Hour by hour the same flat truth returns: the dose does not finish the wanting it wakes. It sharpens it.

Have you ever watched a drinker take the first drink to feel sociable and grow duller with each glass while swearing the next will bring the glow back? The shape is identical. The glow talked about at noon never appears at night. Only urgency appears, and urgency is not pleasure.

You will tell me: “But yesterday was stressful. Food is love. I deserved one.”

I hear you. We all spoke that dialect. “Sugary somethings to get me through.” “Treat when you feel you deserve one.” “I’ve managed to be good this week.” “Celebrating with chocolate.” “Short term lift then fast drop.” The words feel personal. They were taught. Behind every phrase the same promise shines.

Keep that empty column in front of you. We will test every pretty phrase against it before this chapter ends.

TEETH AND THE CEILING NO ONE LIVES UNDER

Now let me give you two flat facts, because feeling fine misleads, and I want you seeing with eyes open. I state them as fact, then we return at once to benefit, where freedom lives. I speak here of populations, not as a sentence on you, and if you live with diabetes or take medication that affects blood sugar, talk to your clinician and follow their advice first.

First, teeth. The fact is, frequent free sugars feed plaque acid and that acid eats enamel into decay. Brushing matters, fluoride matters, meals matter, other factors matter — I do not pretend otherwise. Still the mechanism runs: each sweet bath keeps acid on the teeth longer. A mouth washed in sweetness five times a day lives a different dental life from a mouth fed by plain meals. Cavity is a real bodily stake of frequent sweet doses. Not a scold. A stake.

Second, the ceiling. Guidance says keep free sugars below a tenth of daily energy, with further gain below a twentieth, because ordinary eating now sails far above both. The average kitchen lives above guidance while feeling normal. The average shop, the average lunchbox, the average desk drawer lives above guidance while calling itself moderate. Feeling normal does not mean intake is low. It means low has been redefined around us by a world soaked in sweetness.

Do you see what those facts do and do not prove? They prove BAD SUGAR does plenty TO you — to teeth, to load on the body, to the day, to the wallet, to the hours. They do not tell you what you must do. They do not sentence you. They do not say any single bite is poison or that a number on a packet decides your fate. They sit in the TO column, heavy and settled, at population level.

But I do not ask you to stop because of teeth. I do not ask you to stop because of ceilings. I do not ask you to stop because a chart says so.

Many readers beg me to scare them straight at this point. “Show me the worst. That will make me stop.” No. It will not. Fear makes you clutch harder at the very thing you fear losing. It puts one rope in your left hand called dread and leaves the old rope in your right hand called want, and you burn in the middle. I give you hard truth at full strength because you deserve truth, then I tell you plainly: do not change from fear. Change because there is nothing to lose.

If I proved harm all day and left benefit intact, you would still crave. You would crave while afraid. You would stand in the kitchen afraid and reaching, ashamed and reaching, knowledgeable and reaching. Knowledge of harm never emptied a box. Only the sight of no benefit empties the wanting.

We end that war today by pulling the real rope out entirely. No benefit. No war.

WHAT EXACTLY DO YOU MEAN BY BENEFIT?

Let us pin down the ghost. When you say it helps, what picture rises in your mind?

Is it the lift? That I-need-to-eat-something-RIGHT-NOW feeling at three o’clock, the hand shaking a little, the mind cloudy, then the bar and the rush and the sense that the engine has fuel again? We all know that rush. We all called it power.

Is it the treat? That treat when you feel you deserve one after a hard day, the cake on the plate like a medal, the celebrating with chocolate under bright lights with people you love, the warmth that feels like love made edible? We all know that warmth. We all called it joy.

Is it the passage? Those sugary somethings to get me through the meeting, the drive, the evening, the week, the feeling that without a sweet in hand the hours would be grey? We all know that grey fear. We all called sweetness colour.

Behind every picture sits one object, polished by years of advertising and birthdays and tired afternoons. It says BAD SUGAR is a genuine treat or fuel.

Say it slowly and hear how much it promises. A genuine treat or fuel.

A treat means joy given, love made edible, celebration made real. Fuel means power poured in, engine fed, body strengthened for work ahead. If BAD SUGAR were either, the price might be debated. That is why the phrase holds you. It promises both delight and power in one bright packet. It tells you life would be greyer without it. It tells you the grey you feel is your nature and the brightness you feel for ten minutes is the product.

Is it true?

A genuine treat leaves a glow that lasts. You remember it with warmth the next day. You do not hide its wrappers. You do not promise never to do it again while the taste is still in your mouth. You do not need a bigger treat an hour later to repair the last one. A genuine treat does not punish the hour after it.

A genuine fuel powers without payback. It builds steady work you can feel at noon and at four. It does not lift you for ten minutes then drop you lower than before you took it. It does not ask for another dose to fix the drop it caused. A genuine fuel does not leave the worker more tired than the worker it found.

Look at your audit again with those two tests in hand. Did any dose yesterday behave like that description? Did the glow last past the wrapper? Did the power hold past the hour? Or did the lift flicker and fall, did the joy curdle into urgency, did the treat demand repeating until the box emptied and the fuel leave you flat and reaching?

You know the answer because your body gave it hour by hour, not in theory but in lived heaviness. The morning tea did not carry you to lunch. The mid-morning biscuit did not make work sing. The afternoon bar did not make you sharp. The evening pudding did not make love. Love was in the room before the chocolate arrived. Hunger was met by the meal before the pudding claimed it. Energy was yours before the hit borrowed it and charged you for the loan.

So I ask you directly, with warmth and with total certainty: name one real benefit from yesterday that survived the hour. One lift that did not become a low in the same afternoon. One treat that left peace rather than want. One fuel that carried you cleanly to evening without calling for another fix.

You cannot. Not because you are inarticulate. Because there is none to name.

That phrase — a genuine treat or fuel — is the name of an illusion. It is what we were taught to call a brief ending of discomfort that the last dose had created. A tiny body echo misread as a gift. Both halves matter, and you can feel both today without needing their full story yet: the belief in the mind that says this will help, and the small tug in the body that arrives on schedule and passes itself off as need.

What if a man pushed you into a ditch each morning and then lent you a hand out — would you call him your rescuer and thank him with all your heart?

You laugh. Of course you would not. Yet what else do we do when we thank the dose for lifting the very heaviness the last dose laid down? Be harsh to the pusher, never to you. You are the decent neighbour who was told the pusher was a friend. You thanked with a sincere heart because you were given phoney information. The shame of thanking belongs to the fraud, not to your heart.

IT DOES PLENTY TO YOU. IT DOES NOTHING FOR YOU

We have walked the day. We have faced teeth and ceilings. We have named the ghost and watched it thin in daylight.

Now let the verdict fall where it belongs, clean and final for this room:

It does plenty TO you. It does nothing FOR you.

Read it again and feel how it rearranges the memory. TO you: the acid baths, the lift-then-drop, the money leaked, the hours nibbled, the mind busy with rules, the sleep lightened, the hunger confused. FOR you: nothing that survives the hour, nothing that hunger and real food and daylight did not already own, nothing you could point to yesterday and say, there, that lasted and asked for nothing back.

TO is crowded. FOR is empty.

If that is true, the whole harm debate collapses like a tent with its centre pole removed. You do not need to prove harm outweighs. You do not need to argue about grandad and his cake. You do not need to count grams or defend moderation. You need only see benefit was never there. A thing with no benefit needs no balancing. It needs leaving.

Does that frighten you? “If there is no benefit, why do I want it so badly?” Good question. Honest question. I honour it because I asked it myself with a pounding heart.

The wanting is real. The benefit is not. Wanting proves the trap works. It does not prove the prize exists.

Think of yesterday’s last handful. The hand moved. The mouth filled. The wanting rose rather than fell. The mind said, again, to settle it. Again did not settle it. The box emptied. Shame arrived and sold you tomorrow’s rule. Every step felt personal. Every step was mechanical. Mechanics do not prove value. They prove design. A mousetrap proves cheese was used as bait. It does not prove cheese loves the mouse.

You were never chasing joy. You were chasing relief from a low the chase itself maintains. Once seen, that cannot be fully unseen. You may still feel the tug for a while. You will no longer believe the tug tells the truth.

Let me press the point with two questions that close every escape:

If BAD SUGAR truly treated you, why does the treat need hiding, with wrappers folded small and evidence rearranged? If BAD SUGAR truly fuelled you, why does the fuelled worker crash before the day ends and need another fill to face the evening?

There is only one honest answer to both. The treat treats nothing. The fuel fuels nothing. The glow talked about at noon never appears at night. Only urgency appears, and urgency is not pleasure.

Hold that empty FOR column in your hand as we go forward. You entered this chapter saying, “It may harm, but benefits outweigh.” You leave it having to name one benefit that lasts the hour — and finding none to name. That emptiness is not loss. It is the door.

HOW LONG WILL YOU PAY FOR NOTHING?

Now look at time, because time makes the empty column unbearable in the best way, and unbearable is what finally moves a sensible person to joy.

Do you want to pay for an empty benefit for the rest of your life? Money week after week for something that leaves you lower. Mind busy year after year with when and how much and whether you were good. Teeth bathed, afternoons fogged, evenings argued with a box — for the rest of your life — for nothing that gives back?

Feel the weight of that horizon. Not one box. Thousands of boxes. Not one foggy afternoon. Thousands of foggy afternoons. Not one hidden wrapper. A lifetime of hiding from yourself. Every single day forever for something you do not even need, something that never once survived the hour as a true gift. That is what continuing means. Not a small treat. A lifetime’s rent paid to a landlord who never repairs the house.

We tell ourselves moderate rent is fine. Small doses of nothing are still nothing. A little rent for an empty room is still rent. Counting does not fill the room. Being good all week does not fill the room. The room stays empty and the rent keeps rising because each paid dose sharpens the want for the next.

Turn the horizon over and feel the other valence lift you. Picture walking clear and easy for the rest of your life. Mornings unargued. Shops unfeared. Meals enjoyed and finished with quiet satisfaction. Mind quiet around food, free to think about work and love and play. No small column to defend. No scales to hold. That horizon costs you nothing to enter, because you lose nothing real by leaving an illusion. You do not pay to leave an empty room. You rejoice that the bills stop.

Which rest of life do you want? The one paying for nothing, or the one free from the bill?

I know your heart answers at once. Of course it does — that is why you are here.

You may hear the old voice bargain even now: “But I am moderate. I count. I am harmless. Others are worse.” I hear you, and I answer with kindness and with steel. Count the audit again. Moderate still pays daily. Counting still keeps the mind busy from morning to night. Harmless still leaves the FOR column empty at midnight. Being less hooked than another prisoner does not make the cell a home.

You do not need a bigger scare. You do not need another study. You need a clearer demand. Demand the benefit. Keep demanding it at every wrapper, every queue, every tired hour. Let every memory answer. The answer will always be the same, and each time it sounds, the trap loosens one more wire.

So here is my third instruction:
JUDGE ONLY BY WHAT IT DOES FOR YOU
Forget harm versus benefit and ask for the benefit.

Carry that rule into every sweet thought from today on. Not how bad is it — what does it do FOR me? Let the question do the work that effort never could. You came to me trapped, not weak, hands moving before decision. You leave this room with an empty benefit ledger in your hand and a question the con cannot answer. Guard that emptiness. It is worth more than any rule. It is the axis on which the whole escape turns.

Read on with lightness. We have set the scales down. Now we clear the lies one by one, until wanting itself falls silent and joy returns where it always lived — in hunger met, in meals enjoyed, in days unargued.

SUMMARY
- Arguing about harm keeps you hooked because harm can be haggled while the supposed benefit stays hidden.
- An ordinary day watched hour by hour shows taking on one side and nothing lasting on the other.
- Teeth and daily ceilings prove a heavy cost at population level, yet fear never creates freedom.
- Delight plus power as a promise dies when each dose is tested by what happens in the hour after it.
- The crowded cost beside the empty gift ends the need to balance anything.
- A lifetime paying for emptiness looks very different from a lifetime walking clear.
- You arrived defending a benefit and you leave unable to point to one that survives the hour.
