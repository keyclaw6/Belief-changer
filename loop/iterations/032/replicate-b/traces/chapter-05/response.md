Chapter 5
THE LIFT THAT MAKES THE LOW
*That lift you feel is not power going in — it is the low from the last dose lifting for a few minutes before it drags you lower again.*

YOU KNOW THIS AFTERNOON BY HEART

Let me sit with you at the desk where it always happens, because we need to see it hour by hour, not as theory.

It is just after lunch. Nothing dramatic. The morning's work is half-done, the inbox is full, the light through the blinds is flat and yellow. You ate at one, something sensible, and by half past two a heaviness settles behind the eyes. Thoughts slow. The screen blurs if you stare too long. The shoulders ache from hunching. You yawn into your hand and you hear your own true voice say it, word for word as we all said it:

"I need sugary somethings to get me through."

You are not hungry. That is the strange part and you know it. Your stomach is full. Your limbs are heavy. What you feel is a tug higher up — in the head, in the mouth, in the mood — a restless, fussy, cannot-settle feeling that says something sweet will fix the afternoon. You tell yourself it is fuel. The body needs energy to work. The brain runs on sugar. Everyone knows that. You reach into the drawer, the bag, the pocket. A bar, a biscuit pack, a bottle, a handful from the jar by the kettle. A dose.

You have laid down blame. Keep it down. We are not here to accuse you. You come into this room as a forgiven investigator, a capable adult who now knows the weeks of rules and restarts proved nothing about character. We hold that kindness steady while we look, closely and without flinching, at the lift that fooled us all.

Listen to the next ten minutes, because they are the whole con in miniature.

Sweet floods the tongue. Chew. Swallow. Sip. There is a brightening. Almost a fizz behind the forehead. You sit up straighter. You answer two emails quickly. You laugh at something someone says across the desk. You stretch and feel almost fond of the afternoon. You think: there, you see? That is what I needed. Short, bright, real. You would swear on oath that BAD SUGAR gave you energy.

I believed that oath. I swore it myself for years. I told kind people with total sincerity that a sweet drink cleared my head and a biscuit made me kind again. I was not lying. I was misreading. There is a difference, and the difference is everything.

Watch the next hour with me, kindly, without blinking.

By ten past three the brightness thins. By half past three it is gone. The eyes are heavier than before the dose. The mind is noisier and foggier at once — foggy like cotton wool, busy like flies. You reread the same sentence three times. You feel edgy toward people you like. Your mouth feels sticky and wants another sweet taste to clear the sticky taste, which tells you everything if you listen. Your hands feel empty. That empty, twitchy, slightly shaky, need-something-sweet-now feeling crawls up from the stomach to the throat to the head.

You said to yourself at three o'clock, "short term lift then fast drop," and you laughed it off as your personality. It is not your personality. It is the timetable.

At four o'clock you reach again. Not from hunger. From fog. The second dose is not enjoyed. It is administered. Eaten standing up by the kettle. Drunk without tasting while the kettle boils. Barely noticed while the mind is already on the next task. Ten minutes of lift. Then cloud again, thicker this time. By five you are flat, snappy, tired in a wired way, stuffed and empty at once, promising yourself you will eat properly tonight and knowing the evening will start with picking at something sweet while dinner cooks.

Do you recognise your own hand in that afternoon? Of course you do. We all lived it. Different desks, same hours. Different packets, same wheel. Drivers know it at traffic lights, reaching into the glove box. Nurses know it mid-shift, reaching for the station tin. Parents know it at the school gate, reaching for the bar kept for the children. Shop workers know it behind the till. Students know it over books. The room changes. The hours do not.

Now look at it as the forgiven investigator you became, not as the defendant you were. What gave you energy in that afternoon? And what took it away?

THE TEN MINUTES THAT FOOL EVERYONE

Let us stay a little longer inside those ten bright minutes, because they are persuasive. I will not insult you by pretending they did not happen. They happened. The brightening happened. The sitting up happened. The quick emails happened. To deny it would make you distrust me, and rightly.

What I ask is a different question: where did those ten minutes come from?

We ate lunch and felt full. We worked through the early afternoon and felt our attention thin. We dosed sweet and felt attention return. It looks like cause and effect. Dose, then brightness. No dose, then dullness. Any sensible person would conclude the dose powers the brightness.

But look at the morning with the same honest eyes.

You woke and moved about the house. You washed, dressed, spoke, made decisions, carried bags, crossed roads, answered messages. You did all that before any sweet passed your lips. No dose powered it. Your body powered it. Your sleep powered it. Your own steady system powered it, quietly, without applause.

Then mid-morning came the first sweet with coffee, or the juice, or the biscuit at the meeting. Small, innocent, hardly noticed. Then lunch. Then the heavy two o'clock hour. Then the dose that seemed to save the day.

Do you see? Functioning was easy before the sweet wheel started turning for the day. Functioning grew harder with every turn of the wheel. If sweet were fuel, each dose would build on the last toward greater clarity. More fuel, more power. That is how fuel behaves in a car, in a fire, in any honest system.

What happens with BAD SUGAR is the reverse. The first dose of the day seems to lift a little. The second lifts less and drops further. The third barely lifts at all and leaves thicker fog. By evening you have taken in hundreds of calories of so-called energy and you have never felt less energetic. If this is fuel, why does the tank read emptier the more you pour in?

I hear you, because I said it myself:

"But work is hard. Afternoons are hard. Perhaps I am simply a low-energy person who needs help."

Are you? Were you a low-energy child? Did you need a bar to run across a field, to laugh for an hour, to ask a hundred questions without pause? No. You ran on plain meals and sleep and eagerness. No drawer was needed. No 3pm ceremony was needed. Energy was yours without purchase.

Are you low-energy on holiday, when you walk for miles in sun, swim, talk late, wake clear? Many sweet eaters tell me they forget sweet for hours on bright days, then remember it the moment boredom or tiredness returns to an ordinary desk. The body did not change on holiday. The loop quieted because life was loud. That tells you the tiredness is not your nature. It is the state between fixes.

And hear this other voice, the most respectable one:

"I do not care about theory. I feel the lift. Are you calling me a liar?"

I am calling you honest and mistaken, as I was. You felt relief and you named it power. Relief is real. The name is false. When a pain lifts for minutes, the lifting feels like a gift — unless you see who laid the pain down in the first place.

WHAT YOU CALLED FUEL WAS A LOW RELIEVED

I know how hard this is to accept, because I clung to the fuel story longer than any other. I believed BAD SUGAR powered work, powered kindness, powered patience. Without a sweet top-up I was convinced my brain would stall and my afternoon would die.

The fact is the afternoon did not die from lack of BAD SUGAR. It died from the last BAD SUGAR.

Understand this plainly and the whole fuel story collapses.

We eat or drink a dose. Blood sugar spikes fast and high. The body, which is a marvellous precision machine, rushes to clear the flood. Insulin surges to do its honest job. For some of us that surge overshoots and leaves a real after-dip in the hours after — shaky, hollow, irritable, cloudy — and for most of us the everyday 3pm slump is not clinical low blood sugar at all, only fog and tug as the loop calls for its next fix. I speak plainly here about bodies in general, not a verdict on you — if you live with diabetes or take anything that affects blood sugar your clinician's word comes first, this book changes belief, not medical care.

Each sweet binge re-fires the wanting circuit, so the thought of sweet lights up again before the packet is empty. Not pleasure. Wanting. A lit-up urge mistaken for enjoyment. The glow says do it again. The body says pay later.

Then the low arrives. Flat mood. Thin patience. Heavy lids. Twitchy hands. Mouth calling for sweet. We misread that low as need for fuel, as proof work is hard, as proof we are tired people who need help to function. It never occurs to us that the low is the echo of the lift.

So we take another dose. The low lifts for minutes. Relief floods through. We say: energy! Fuel! It works!

It does not work. It relieves.

Ask yourself, with love and with steel, the questions whose only honest answer ends the fuel story:

If that bar gave you real energy, why did you have less energy an hour after it than before it, every single day for years?

If sweet is fuel, why does a child full of real dinner run and laugh for hours without a single sweet top-up, while you, topped up twice, can barely keep your eyes open at your desk?

If you need sugar to function, how did you function perfectly well for the first hours of the morning before the first dose woke the want — and why did functioning get harder with every dose, not easier?

There is only one honest answer. The dose creates the emptiness it pretends to fill. We were never fuelling. We were relieving, briefly, a need the last relief created.

You thought BAD SUGAR was a genuine treat or fuel. It was neither. It was a loan shark selling you back your own afternoon at interest.

See how the wheel turns on this one belief. Morning dose lays down the afternoon low. Afternoon low sells the afternoon dose. Afternoon dose lays down the evening low. Evening low sells the evening pick while dinner cooks. Night of broken sleep and furry tongue lays down the groggy morning. Groggy morning sells the sweet coffee to start again. Round and round, not because you are tired, but because the lift manufactures the tiredness it then briefly lifts.

I want you to feel the kindness in this verdict, not the harshness. If the low comes from the lift, you are not a tired person. You are a lively person made tired by lifts and lows. Your tiredness never proved you need sweet. It proved sweet was at work in you.

THE TIGHT SWEET SHOES

Let me give you the picture that makes this impossible to unsee, because you will need it every time memory tries to polish the lift.

Imagine you decide to wear shoes a size too small. All day. To work, to the shop, up the stairs. They pinch. They rub. They throb. By three o'clock your feet ache and your head aches with the ache. People ask how you are and you snap. Work drags. You tell yourself the day is hard.

Then at last you sit down and pull the shoes off. Ahh. Relief rushes up the legs. Blood sings. You sigh out loud. Your whole face softens. For a minute the world is wonderful.

Now tell me truly: did the tight shoes give you pleasure?

No. They gave you pain, then sold you relief from pain as if it were pleasure. No one in their right mind would say: I must wear tight shoes to feel good. I need tight shoes to function. Tight shoes help my concentration. Everyone would see the trick at once.

That is exactly what we did with BAD SUGAR.

We put on the tight shoes with the morning biscuit, the sweet coffee, the juice that came with breakfast. We wore them through the morning while the wanting learned to tug. By afternoon the pinch was agony — fog, edginess, that gnawing call for something sweet RIGHT NOW — and the next dose was pulling the shoes off for ten minutes. Relief. Sigh. Brightness. Then lacing the shoes tight again for the next hour.

The lift is not energy. It is taking the shoes off. The low is not your nature. It is wearing them.

Carry that picture into every memory that still glows.

That bright feeling after chocolate at the desk was not power arriving. It was pressure lifting. That second wind after a sweet drink was not your brain fed. It was your brain released, briefly, from the last feed. That rush of fondness for the world after cake was not love in the cake. It was the ache pausing long enough for you to feel the world again.

You never rose above the normal of a person who never put the shoes on. You only climbed, for minutes, back toward normal, then sank below it again. And each climb cost the next sinking.

I hear your objection before you speak it, because it was mine for years:

"But I feel the lift. It is real. Are you saying I imagined it?"

I am saying you felt something real and named it wrong. The relief is real. The interpretation is false. Relief from a self-made ache feels exactly like a gift if you never see who made the ache. The pusher who shoves you into the ditch each morning then offers a hand out feels like a rescuer until you see him shove.

Be harsh here toward the pusher, never toward yourself. You are the kind neighbour who thanked the hand. The fraud belongs to the trap that dug the ditch and sold the rope.

Look at the shoes from one more angle, because this angle ends the argument about concentration.

You say sweet helps you concentrate. Watch what actually happens to attention on the wheel. Morning, before dosing, attention moves cleanly. Thought follows thought. Then dose. Then brief bright attention that feels sharp. Then scattered attention that cannot settle. Then dose to fix scatter. Then brief sharpness. Then scatter again, wider. By late afternoon attention is in shreds, jumping, rereading, forgetting names, losing the thread mid-sentence.

Is that helped concentration? No. That is concentration interrupted by the shoes, then briefly unpinched, then pinched again. A person who never wore the shoes keeps the clean morning attention through the day, with natural ebbs that rest restores. We mistook the brief unpinching for a gift to attention. It was a debt to attention, partly repaid for minutes, then charged again with interest.

It is the other way around.

WHY THE SECOND REACH ALWAYS COMES

Look closer at that moment between the first swallow and the second reach, because it holds the whole trap without needing a single laboratory word.

You eat the dose. Sweet hits fast. Wanting lights up. The brain says more, now, again. That is not character. That is the loop re-firing. Each hit teaches the circuit to call for the next hit louder. Not joy deepening. Want deepening. The more you feed it, the more it asks, the less each feed satisfies.

Have you noticed how the first square of chocolate in the morning can taste vivid, while the tenth square at night tastes like cardboard chewed from duty? If sweet gave pleasure, more would please more. But more pleases less. The mouth grows numb. The joy thins. The hand keeps moving after joy has left. That is not pleasure. That is the circuit calling for relief that satisfaction never brings.

Then the after-wave comes. Insulin clearing the flood. Opposing signals rising while the bright signal falls. Known in plain life as the 4pm fog and the evening edginess, the hollow though full, the wired though tired. The lift casts its shadow. The shadow is not accident. It is the other half of the same event.

Ask the trap questions and let them close:

If the first dose fixed the fog, why did the fog return deeper within the hour, every time, without exception?

If enjoyment drives the hand, why does the hand move fastest when enjoyment is thinnest — late in the box, late in the day, late in the evening when taste is gone and only the tug remains?

If will decides, why did will hold all morning and vanish in the three seconds between the first swallow and the second reach?

You know. Of course you know — your afternoons taught you. The dose re-lights the want and lays down the dip that the want will use as proof. One reach carries the next because the relief carries the low inside it like a seed.

And then the deferred load, which I will lay before you flat because you deserve the truth whole, then we will put fear back where it belongs.

The fact is heavy added-sugar eating across years loads the liver, lifts blood pressure, keeps low-grade inflammation simmering, and scrambles the appetite signals that should tell hunger from habit. Across populations that load shows up as more heart and stroke trouble among heavy sweet eaters as a group. I speak of heavy use across years in populations, not a sentence on you from one bar. One biscuit never gave anyone heart disease. The wheel gives it.

Do you feel a chill reading that? Good. Feel it fully. Let it land. Teeth that ache in the night. Gums bleeding on the brush. Money leaking coin by coin for something that left no joy. Hours nibbled minute by minute while standing at cupboards. Moods yo-yoing with the clock. A body asked to clear flood after flood, year after year, while the mind called the flood fuel.

I do not want you to escape because you are frightened. Fear never freed anyone — it only makes the shoes feel tighter and the next removal feel sweeter. I want you to escape because there is nothing to keep. The load is real, and the lift is false, and freedom is marvellous whether the load frightens you or not.

Hear me clearly: you are not changing from fear. You are escaping to relief.

Let that relief breathe. Notice how different it feels from the old dread of Monday rules. No gloomy sentence hangs over you. No hard weeks stretch ahead. No precious friend waits to be mourned. There is only a false lift seen clean and a true steadiness waiting underneath.

FLAT, FOGGY AND NEVER SATISFIED

Let me name where the wheel leaves you, because you have lived there for years without having words for the address.

Not hungry. Not full. Not awake. Not asleep. Mouth sticky. Head busy. Belly tight. Mood thin. Energy gone though calories poured in. Evening on the sofa reaching without deciding. Morning waking unrefreshed though you slept. Work done but never with ease. Laughter coming but quickly tired. Sweet in the cupboard, sweet in the drawer, sweet in the car, and no peace in any of them.

We call that normal. It is not normal. It is the permanent state of the sweet eater between fixes.

Think of the hours you have spent in that state without naming it. The long meeting where you smiled and nodded and retained nothing. The drive home where the road blurred and the packet rustled and you arrived with no memory of the turnings. The film you watched while eating and had to ask about the next day because eating had eaten the watching. The conversation with a loved one where half your mind was on the tin in the kitchen. The Sunday that passed in grazes and naps and vague intentions, gone by Monday without a trace. That is the tax. Not only fog in the head, but life thinned as attention leaks toward the next fix.

Think of the money. Not the grand sums the old teachers loved to total with a wagging finger — I will not lecture you about coins — but the daily leak you felt in your own pocket. The bar at the station. The bottle at the till. The cake with the coffee that doubled the coffee. The family pack that never saw the weekend. Small, smiling thefts, day after day, for something that left you emptier. Think of the time. The minutes at the cupboard. The minutes in the queue. The minutes rereading the same page. The minutes promising Monday. Added up they make weeks of a life spent serving a lift that never lasts.

Think of the body you live in. Tongue furry on waking. Thirst in the night. Skin tired. Stomach rolling after the evening flood. Sleep thin and broken though the day exhausted you. Teeth tender to cold. Gums quick to bleed. Hunger confused until every tug feels like emergency and no meal feels like arrival. None of this proves you are broken. All of it proves the wheel charges for its ten bright minutes in every currency you own.

There is a phrase I want you to carry for this state, because once named it can never hide as tiredness or age or personality again.

We end up flat, foggy and never satisfied.

Say it in your own mouth. Flat — no lift that holds. Foggy — mind clouded though body fuelled. Never satisfied — always calling for the next sweet to fix the last sweet. That is not fuel. Fuel would satisfy. Fuel would clear. Fuel would build. BAD SUGAR nourishes nothing. It is empty calories that gift no energy your body did not already own. Your body makes its own steady glucose from real food, hour by hour, without spikes, without crashes, without begging. The steadiness was yours before the first sweet taught you to doubt it.

If BAD SUGAR fed you, why are you hungry an hour after eating? If it gave energy, why do you need more energy to recover from the energy? If it is food, why does food leave you emptier than before you ate?

You know. Of course you know — your afternoons taught you.

Let me widen this one step further, because the fog touches more than work.

It touches temper. How many sharp words left your mouth in the low hour that a clear head would never have sent? How many small kindnesses died in the edginess between fixes? We blamed stress, blamed colleagues, blamed family, blamed the time of month, blamed age. Much of what we called stress was the low talking. Much of what we called our difficult nature was the wheel turning in us.

It touches joy. The sweet eater cannot fully enjoy even sweet. The first bite is vivid, the rest is chase. Cannot fully enjoy meals, because hunger is confused and satisfaction is drowned. Cannot fully enjoy rest, because rest is spent tugging. Cannot fully enjoy company, because part of the mind is counting, bargaining, hiding wrappers, planning the next quiet moment with the tin. The fog is not only in the head. It is over the whole day like thin smoke.

And it touches self-trust. Every broken vow whispered that you cannot trust your own hand. Every hidden wrapper whispered that you must manage appearances. Every Monday restart whispered that clarity lasts a day and fog owns the year. None of that was true about you. All of it was true about the wheel. Seen clean, trust returns without effort, because the hand that seemed faithless was obeying a loop, and the heart that seemed weak was enduring a low laid by a lift.

THE CREDIT BELONGS TO YOU, NOT THE DOSE

Let us reassign the credit where it always belonged, because the trap survives by stealing your own virtues and selling them back as its gifts.

That bright ten minutes after the bar — who made the sitting down, the pause, the deep breath, the break from the screen, the stretch, the laugh with a colleague? You did. The moment did. The body did, gasping for a rest. BAD SUGAR only ever sneaked a ride on your pause and claimed your relief as its power.

Think of the mornings you woke clear before the first sweet. Who made that clarity? No dose. Your sleep. Your body resetting in the night. Think of the days on holiday when you forgot the drawer for hours because you were walking, talking, laughing in sun. Who made that ease? Life did. Company did. Movement did. The wanting quieted because you were living, not because you were feeding.

I remember my own desk years ago, the drawer that hummed, the three o'clock vow that today would be different. I remember the bar eaten over the keyboard, crumbs in the keys, the brief lift, the long fog. I remember telling a friend I could not write without sweet, that my best lines came after chocolate. Then one clear week, free of the wheel, I wrote better, faster, with cleaner joy, and I saw the lie I had polished for years. The lines had never come from the bar. They had come from me, in spite of the bar, in the brief window before the fog closed. The bar had stolen the credit for my own mind.

Reverse it and see clean:

It is not that you concentrate because you eat sweet. You fail to concentrate until you eat sweet because sweet taught the circuit to interrupt concentration until fed.

It is not that you are a tired person who needs a lift. You are a lively person made tired by lifts and lows.

It is not that work is impossible without sugary somethings. Work feels impossible without them because every hour without them is spent wearing the tight shoes.

It is the other way around.

Land this with me as settled fact, not as hope: the energy you praised was yours all along. The body that steadies blood sugar from plain meals, that wakes, hungers, satisfies, rests — that body never left you. It only waited under the noise for you to stop dosing the noise louder.

And hear the second half of today's move, because it decides everything that follows. Escaping does not mean white-knuckling through afternoons while the want screams. It means the want is removed for good. Not fought. Not managed. Removed, because the belief that fed it dies. When you no longer believe the dose gives anything, the circuit has nothing to rehearse. The tug, unfed by belief, fades in days to a faint echo, then to nothing. You do not spend life holding out against afternoons. You get your afternoons back, clear, steady, yours.

Do you see how different that is from everything you were taught? The old teachers said the want stays and you must be strong. I tell you the want goes because it was never strength you lacked. It was truth.

Do you feel how light that makes the road? No dread. No gloom. No counting. No holding out. Only sight. You do not need to brace for suffering to see a trick. You only need to see it. And you have seen it — hour by hour, shoe by shoe, reach by reach.

So here is my fifth instruction:
BEGIN WITH ELATION, NOT DREAD
You are escaping, not losing.

Carry that into every memory of lifts and lows from today on. Not as comfort. As fact. Begin glad. Begin light. There is no gloomy sentence to serve, no precious friend to mourn. There is a pair of tight shoes to leave by the door and a whole clear afternoon to walk in your own feet.

You entered this room saying, "I need sugar to function." You leave it knowing the lift makes the low, the low sells the lift, and real energy was the body you never lost. With fuel killed, you can look with kind, hungry eyes at what eating was always meant to be — and find joy waiting there without a single sweet coat on it.

SUMMARY
- The afternoon buzz is relief from a low the previous dose created, not fresh energy arriving.
- A sweet top-up brightens minutes then clouds hours, which is why one reach always calls for another.
- Relief mistaken for benefit works like tight shoes, pain then removal sold as pleasure.
- Each dose re-lights wanting and the after-dip follows the high as its shadow.
- Heavy sweet use across years loads the body while feeding nothing the body did not already own.
- The eater's steady state becomes flat and clouded and always calling for more.
- Credit for any bright moment belongs to the pause and the person, never to the dose.
- Freedom means the want dies with the belief, leaving clear afternoons without a fight.
