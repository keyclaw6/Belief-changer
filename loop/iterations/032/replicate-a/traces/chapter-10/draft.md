Chapter 10
NO SAFE SWEET
*Inside BAD SUGAR there is no safe exception, and one kept sweet keeps the whole loop alive.*

THE FRIDAY YOU KEEP POLISHING

You have let the bright cinema go. You have seen the cups prove the tongue follows story. You have stood at the sink and watched the old favourite pall. And now, with almost everything clear, the negotiator in you steps forward, reasonable, smiling, with a diary in hand.

We all know him. I knew him well. He does not defend the daily graze any longer. He offers a deal.

"All right. I see the desk drawer. I see the sofa rustle. Let me keep Fridays. Just Friday dessert. Just one. Just the birthday slice. Just the one I allow, clean and chosen. The rest of the week I will be free."

It sounds so sane. It sounds so adult. One sweet island in a clean week. What harm could one island do?

Let me walk that Friday week with you, hour by hour, because I lived that week for years and I can show you the week from inside.

Monday you are virtuous. You tell friends you have cut down. You feel almost free, and the almost tastes good. But somewhere low a small clock starts ticking. Four days to Friday. You do not call it craving. You call it looking forward.

Tuesday the picture brightens. The Friday pudding glows a little in memory. You picture the menu. You rehearse which one you will choose. Ordinary food eaten Tuesday tastes a touch duller, not because it is dull, but because Friday is stealing its light.

Wednesday the glow grows. You are good, you tell yourself, you have been good this week. That old voice, I've managed to be good this week, starts to claim wages. You skip a real hunger to save room. You pass a shop and think, not long now. The week bends toward one evening.

Thursday the chant is loud. You tell yourself it is excitement, not need. You eat a little less at tea to deserve it. You feel the lift of anticipation, that tight, bright hum before a fix. You sleep with the menu in your head.

Friday arrives. All day you wait. Work drags because the evening shines too bright. You arrive hungry on purpose, nerves up. The dessert comes. First spoon, blast. Second spoon, already chasing. Third spoon, the old wrapper rustle in miniature, the hand moving faster than the vote. Ten minutes of roar, then the film of stale on teeth, the thirst, the sag. And then, strangest of all, not peace, but a flat, puzzled sadness. Was that it? After five days of dreaming, was that it?

Saturday morning you wake coated and low. The clock has reset. Six days to Friday. And now a new voice slips in, quiet, familiar: Thursday felt so long, shall we move it to Thursday too? Just this once? Saturday needs a little comfort after Friday's dip. Sunday is family day, surely that counts as special. By Sunday night Friday has become Thursday and Saturday and Sunday with Friday still to come.

Tell me truly, from your own Fridays:

Did the allowed one make the week free — or did it make the week a wait?

Did the allowed one taste better for being rare — or did rarity make it glow in fantasy and pall in the mouth, leaving you chasing a glow that never landed?

Did the week creep because you are weak — or because one kept dose teaches the mouth that BAD SUGAR is precious, forbidden fruit to dream about between hits?

There is only one honest answer. The exception did not tame the loop. The exception *was* the loop.

We were never free with a Friday. We were prisoners with day-release, and day-release makes the cell crueller. That is what cut-down does. It does not loosen the Sugar Trap. It tightens it with waiting.

ONE MEANS THEM ALL

You say, but surely one small one cannot hurt. Surely will is enough for one. Surely a single biscuit, taken calmly, proves control.

I honour the hope in that surely, because I said it a thousand times. Then I watched my own hand answer it.

You know the shape. A permitted sliver is allowed, taken to prove the rule works. The mouth wakes. The chant that was quiet all day lifts its head. You hear your own old line rising unbidden, I feel like i cant stop, and you laugh it off while the hand reaches again. Not from hunger. Not from joy. From a trigger pulled. The craving, once fed, carries the binge. That loss of steering is the signature, not your character.

I lived it in kitchens, in offices, in cars parked outside shops. One to be polite. One because it was offered. One because the children left it. One because I had been good. Every one wore a different coat. Every one ended the same way, hand to mouth to fog, promises broken before swallow ended.

Let me ask flat, as one who watched it nightly:

If one were truly safe, why do you never want one? Why does one always ask for its brother?

If one proved control, why does the evening after one feel less controlled than evenings with none?

If one were freedom, why does one need defending, explaining, hiding — while free evenings need nothing at all?

There is only one honest answer. Inside BAD SUGAR one is never one. One is the order to continue.

This is not a flaw in you. We all found the same. The sweet hit does not satisfy a need. It re-opens it. Each dose briefly quiets the low the last dose dug, then guarantees the low returns, louder. That is why the permitted one cannot stay one. It can only restart the lift-crash-repeat you already saw at the desk.

It does plenty TO you. It does nothing FOR you.

Let that sentence do its work where the negotiator pleads. TO you: the re-lit chant, the second reach, the evening bent around a packet, the morning paying for the night. FOR you: name what the one added that hunger, company, rest, laughter did not already hold. The column stays empty because the gift was never there.

Hear the verdict and let it stand: one keeps the belief alive, and the belief keeps the behaviour alive.

THE CLIFF WITH A SAFE FALL

Now let me show you the deal in a picture you cannot unsee, because pictures tell truth quicker than debate.

A friend takes you to white cliffs on a bright morning. Gulls crying. Sea far below, beautiful and terrible. He points to the edge and says, cheerfully:

"Jump. You will love it. And do not worry. You may fall, but only a few metres. Just a safe fall. Just Fridays. Just birthdays. Just one."

Would you jump? Would you thank him for the kind limit? Would you call him sane for offering a safe fall off a cliff?

You would laugh in his face. You would tell him there is no safe fall off a cliff. The limit is nonsense because the place is the fall. To jump is to fall. To allow the fall is to take the whole drop.

That is exactly what the negotiator offers with BAD SUGAR. Jump off, but only a little. Fall, but only on Fridays. Keep the special ones, but stay free. The words sound careful. The picture shows they are mad.

We all tried the safe fall. I tried weekday clean with weekend sweet. I tried only when offered. I tried only dark chocolate. I tried only after meals. Each rule was a new line painted on the cliff face, this far and no farther. Each line washed away in the first rain of want. Not because I was weak. Because I had already jumped by allowing the jump.

Ask and answer from your own cliff walks:

Have you ever kept a safe sweet for a month without the line moving — Friday staying Friday, one staying one, never creeping to Thursday, never growing in size?

Have you ever felt freer with a rule to guard — or did the rule make every shop a test, every party a maths problem, every evening a courtroom?

If the limit worked, why did you need a new limit, a stricter limit, a kinder limit, year after year?

There is only one honest answer. Limits make precious. Precious makes watchful. Watchful makes weary. Weary reaches.

The fact is, totality is not harder than moderation. It is easier, because there is nothing to guard, nothing to count, nothing to dream toward. One door shut is rest. One door ajar is torment. Close it and walk.

Hear the verdict and let it stand: there is no safe fall, there is only the cliff or the field.

TOMORROW, THE EMPTY HOUSE AND THE SWAP

The negotiator has three more masks. You have worn each. Let me meet each face to face and lift it kindly, because each mask speaks your own dialect and each answer is short once the cliff is seen.

"I don't keep it in the house. That solves it."

Does it? Whose legs walk to the shop at nine? Whose hand opens the drawer at work? Whose car holds emergency stock for the road? We all cleared cupboards and called it freedom while the chant rode with us to every till. I cleared mine a dozen times. The house grew clean. The office grew stocked. The bag grew heavy. The mouth grew louder outside the house than it ever was inside.

If the house were the trap, why did want follow you to shops, stations, parties, cinemas, every bright till in town?

If absence at home were freedom, why did the walk to buy feel more enslaved than the reach to a shelf?

Is the Sugar Trap in the cupboard — or is the cupboard in the mind that still believes one sweet occasion matters?

There is only one honest answer. The house never held you. The belief did. Move packets and you move the hunt. Kill belief and cupboards, shops, parties lose all power at once.

"I'll quit tomorrow. After the birthday. After the holidays."

Tomorrow is the negotiator's favourite room because tomorrow never comes. I lived in it for years. Monday I will start. After the trip. After stress lifts. After Christmas. Each tomorrow polished the next dose with last-time glow. Each last time tasted sadder and louder for being last, and therefore harder to leave.

If tomorrow were kinder, why has every tomorrow for years delivered another today dosed?

If the birthday needs sweet to shine, why do you remember faces, songs, laughter weeks later and forget the icing by nightfall?

If waiting helped, would you not be free by now after all that waiting?

There is only one honest answer. Waiting is the Trap talking. Delay keeps value alive. Freedom is now or it is fantasy.

"I'll wean. I'll swap to sweeteners, to light bars, to little sips."

This last mask looks most modern and is most false. Swap the product and you swap the packet while keeping the user. I tried every swap the market offered. Light this. Diet that. Sweetened without sugar but sweet in story. The mouth still got its blast. The hand still got its ritual. The chant still got its hour. The Nibbler still got fed on schedule, and the Sweet Con that feeds it grew fatter on the excuse that change was happening.

Watch your own swap weeks. Did the substitute close the door to quiet — or did it keep the hour, the place, the reach, the thought, alive?

Did sweeteners teach hunger and done — or did they teach the tongue to keep expecting blast without pause?

If the swap freed you, why did the old love return at full volume the first night the real thing stood near?

There is only one honest answer. The substitute idea itself is the flaw. You remain a user, still hooked, only wearing a new uniform.

We were never freed by moving the dose. We were freed by seeing there is no dose that gives. The body asks for food when hungry and quiet when done. The chant asks for blast on the hour whether the blast comes white, brown, liquid, solid, sugared or sweetened. Starve the hour, not the brand.

Some will tell you this all-or-nothing talk is extreme, that balance is mature, that totality frightens them. I hear that fear because I felt it. Then I ask kindly: extreme compared to what? Is it extreme to eat when hungry and stop when satisfied? Is it extreme to walk past a cliff rather than practise safe falls? Who taught you that needing a daily fix is balance and living without chant is extreme? That lesson was not yours. It was installed.

Hear the verdict and let it stand: move nothing, swap nothing, wait for nothing — end the exception and the loop ends.

THE SEATBELT, NOT THE STEERING WHEEL

You say, but life is messy. Crumbs cross plates. Sauces hide spoons. A child presses cake to your lips. Must I live in panic? Must I interrogate every meal?

No. You must live in ease, and ease needs a clear picture.

Think of the belt across your chest when you drive. It sits quiet. You do not pray to it. You do not test it. You do not steer toward walls to prove it works. It is there for the accidental jolt, the sudden stop no driver plans. It absorbs what accident brings and is forgotten again.

So it is with your body and an accidental crumb. The body is a marvellous machine that copes with a chance mouthful, a hidden spoon in sauce, a bite taken in unknowing. Such a blip revives nothing unless the mind climbs back in and takes the wheel toward the Trap. If you live with diabetes, take medication that affects blood sugar or appetite, are pregnant, or carry medical risk around food, talk to your clinician first and follow their advice — this book changes belief, it does not replace care.

Do you see the line? The belt is for accident, never for steering. You do not drive erratically because a belt exists. You do not plan a small crash to test it. You drive straight, glad, hungry-led, and forget the belt entirely.

We all feared the crumb would be our ruin. I feared it myself. Then I saw the truth on free weeks. A trace in a meal came and went like weather, no chant woke, no hand hurried, because the mind had not agreed to feed. But a deliberate one, however small, woke everything, because the mind had reopened the question. The difference was never the amount. The difference was the agreement.

Ask and answer from your own steering:

Has an unknowing trace ever started a binge on its own — or did binges always start with a knowing yes dressed as just this once?

Does panic about crumbs make you freer — or does calm guard of belief make crumbs powerless?

If the body were so fragile, how did hungry children, plain meals, market fruit ever satisfy for hours without maths?

There is only one honest answer. Guard the belief and the body looks after the rest. Guard the packet with panic and the belief slips back in through fear.

So do not steer toward the edge to prove courage. Do not take a deliberate bite to test freedom. Do not keep an emergency sweet for bad days. The belt does not license the crash. It forgives the jolt you never chose. Live straight. Eat hungry. Stop at done. When accident comes, smile at how little it moves you, and drive on.

Hear the verdict and let it stand: the body absorbs the accident, the mind must never choose it.

So here is my tenth instruction:
NEVER KEEP A SPECIAL SWEET
One exception keeps the whole Trap alive.

SUMMARY
* A Friday allowance turns the week into waiting and makes the sweet more precious, not less wanted.
* One permitted dose re-opens the chant and carries more, the loss of steering proves the loop, not weakness.
* Cut-down is a safe fall off a cliff, the limit is nonsense because the place is the fall.
* An empty cupboard never freed the hunt, the want follows belief to every shop and till.
* Tomorrow never comes, delay polishes the next dose and keeps value alive.
* Swapping to sweeteners or light versions keeps the hour and the reach alive and leaves the user still hooked.
* An accidental crumb passes like weather when belief stays shut, a deliberate bite wakes everything because the mind agreed.
