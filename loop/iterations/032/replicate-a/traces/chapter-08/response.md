Chapter 8
WHO BUILT THE WANT
*You did not invent this craving, you inherited it, and the fear that holds you in it belongs to the trap, not to wisdom.*

YOU CARRY PROOF IN YOUR BELLY

You come to me changed.

You have watched the evening grumble thin when unfed. You know the shape of a tiny echo and a loud story about the echo. You have felt hunger open wide and welcome many foods, and done arrive quiet without counting. You carry that warm proof in your belly.

And still a cold thought crosses you in daylight, reasonable and respectable, the one you speak when friends are near:

“Everyone eats it. It is normal. It is in every house, every office, every birthday. And I — I have always been the type who cannot stop at one. Perhaps it is family. Perhaps it is personality. Perhaps the want is mine.”

I hear you. I honour the honesty in it. Every daily grazer who ever looked normal in the light has stood where you stand. We all believed the want was personal and the fear was wisdom. We believed normal meant safe, and appetite meant identity.

The fact is, the want was built before you ever voted, and the fear you feel is not wisdom warning you. It is the trap holding both ends of the rope.

You do not believe me yet. Good. You are the forgiven investigator still. Let your own aisles and mirrors answer.

Ask yourself, and answer from life, not from shame:

Did you decide as a clear-eyed child that sweet hits would be your comfort, your reward, your fuel — or was the decision made for you, bright packet by bright packet, long before you could read?

When you say everyone eats it, do you mean everyone is free around it — or do you mean everyone reaches, everyone grazes, everyone crashes, and everyone calls the crash life?

If the want were truly your nature, why did the hungry meal leave you quiet for hours while the normal grazing left you hunting?

There is only one honest answer. The want is not yours. It was planted.

We fell into the same pit together, you and I. We did not dig it. We were led to it by kind hands and clever hands, and then taught to call the pit home. No wonder we fear leaving. No wonder we call the fear ours. The trap taught us both lessons at once: crave the dose, and fear life without the dose.

I will show you who built the want, and who built the fear, and why both collapse the moment you see the builders.

THE AISLE THAT WAS BUILT TO HUNT YOU

Walk with me down an ordinary aisle at half past five.

Lights bright. Shelves low enough for small hands. Packs shouting in red and gold. Cartoon tigers and dancing biscuits smiling from the cardboard. Crunch engineered to melt fast and call for more. Two-for-one bright rows at the end. And at the till, where you must stand with tired feet and a tired mind, a final ambush of small bars at a child’s eye height.

Watch your own hand in that aisle. You came for bread and milk. Your eyes slide. Your mouth waters before hunger speaks. Your hand moves a fraction before your vote. You tell yourself you chose. Did you?

Ask the plain questions and let the aisle answer:

Who placed the sweet at the till and not the apples — you, or the seller who knows tired hands reach?

Who paid for the cartoon friend to smile at your child — you, or the maker who knows love can be tied to a wrapper?

Who decided sweet would be in the bread, the sauce, the cereal, the drink, so hunger itself would learn to chant for one flavour — your body, or the industry that profits when hunger chants?

There is only one honest answer. The aisle hunts. You were hunted.

I lived that aisle for years. I called myself a free shopper. I laughed at adverts. I believed no jingle could touch me. Yet trolley after trolley told another story. Biscuits I had not planned. Chocolate I had sworn off on Monday. Fizzy bottles for the children that emptied before the weekend. How could a free chooser carry home the same unplanned dose every week? How could a clear mind forget its own list in the same place every time?

Stand there longer with me and watch the hunting in detail. The scent machine by the bakery door breathing warm sugar into the air as you enter. The music a fraction too cheerful, a fraction too fast, moving feet toward the bright ends. The packets torn open for tasting, a tiny cup held by a smiling hand, the first bite free because the second will be paid for many times over. The cereal boxes at knee height wearing games and jokes, teaching small eyes that morning means sweet crunch. The drinks fridge glowing like a chapel at the back, so you must walk past every temptation to reach the milk.

You say you have will and judgment. Of course you have. That is why the hunting had to be so clever. A fair choice needs no hunters.

We were conned, not foolish. Warm words for you, harsh words for the con. An army of clever people laboured to make the pack brighter, the crunch louder, the melt faster, the memory sweeter. They tested the exact point where salt and fat and sugar sing together and the mouth asks for another bite before the first ends. They bought the shelf, the smile, the song, the celebration. They put sweet where hunger lives so hunger would speak their language.

Do you see the shape? You were caught in the Sugar Trap long before you felt trapped. The trap did not wait for you to choose. It chose you early, fed you often, and called the feeding normal.

Tell me truly in that aisle:

Does free choice need cartoon salesmen, checkout ambushes, and sugar hidden where you never looked — or does a con need those things?

Does your body ask for bright packets in the woods on a Sunday, far from bells and colours — or does the want wake only where it was trained to wake?

Has hunger ever shouted a brand name, or does only brainwashing shout brand names?

There is only one honest answer. The want was manufactured. The factory is not in you.

Hear the verdict and let it stand: you never chose the craving, the craving chose you.

You do not have to fear that sentence. Rejoice in it. If the want was built, it can be unbuilt. If the voice was installed, it can be uninstalled. You are not fighting nature. You are seeing nurture that lied.

EVERYONE EATS IT, SO IT MUST BE FINE

You say, but everyone eats it. How can something so common be a trap?

I love that objection because it sounds like common sense and acts like a blindfold.

Everyone once smoked in offices and cinemas. Everyone once put sugar in tea without asking. Common never meant safe. Common meant well sold.

Look around with trap eyes and normal changes shape. The office drawer full of bars. The meeting table ringed with biscuits. The evening sofa rustling. The morning mouth apologising. The afternoon heads dropping at three and rising for ten minutes on a fix. Does that look like freedom enjoying a pleasure — or does that look like a crowd feeding one loop and calling the loop life?

Ask with me, face by face:

Do your friends eat sweet and forget it for days — or do they think about it, plan it, earn it, hide it, promise to be good about it tomorrow?

Do they take one and go quiet — or do they take one and hear the chant for the next, and call the chant a sweet tooth?

Do they shine after the dose for hours — or are they left flat, foggy and never satisfied, reaching again before the hour ends?

There is only one honest answer. The crowd is not free. The crowd is caught and smiling through the bars.

I walked among that crowd for years believing my slavery was personality. I watched clever, kind, steady people do the same strange dance: swear off on Monday, negotiate on Wednesday, empty the box on Friday, apologise on Sunday. Doctors. Teachers. Runners. Cooks. Were we all weak in the same week at the same hour with the same wrapper? Or were we all fed by the same machine?

Listen to the talk in the staff room and you hear the bars rattle. “I’ve managed to be good this week.” “I need sugary somethings to get me through.” “Just one, I’ve earned it.” “I’ll start again Monday.” Does a free pleasure talk like that? Does a real meal need to be earned by goodness, hidden in a drawer, confessed on Monday? You never hear anyone whisper, “I was bad, I had extra potatoes.” You hear it about bright packets because the mouth knows what the mind denies: the hand moves before the vote.

The fact is, common proves the power of the machine, not the innocence of the dose. Ubiquity is the con’s best costume. When every hand holds the same bright thing, the lonely thought is not I am trapped. The lonely thought is I am normal. And normal asks no questions.

So ask them now. Count the bins after a party. Count the wrappers in the office bin at four. Count the promises made on Sunday night. Does a genuine pleasure need promises, rules, hiding places, and tomorrows? Does real food need a week of being good to earn it? Or does only a trap need vows to manage it?

Hear the verdict and let it stand: normal is the trap wearing everyone’s face.

That is marvellous news, not dread. You are not strangely broken among the well. You are normally caught among the caught. And what catches many by one lie frees many by one truth.

I AM JUST THE TYPE

Now I will meet your quietest excuse, the one you whisper only to yourself:

“Perhaps others were conned. But I am different. I have always had the sweet tooth. My mother had it. My sister has it. One bite and I hear the chant. I must be the addictive type.”

I honour the pain under that sentence. I do not honour the sentence. It is the Sweet Con speaking in your mother’s voice to protect her food supply.

Let us look at it with investigator eyes, kindly and flat.

If sweet craving were inborn nature, it would arrive before training. Did you crave chocolate before you tasted chocolate? Did you chant for biscuits before biscuits were given as love, as hush, as reward for being good? Or did the tooth arrive after the feeding, exactly where the feeding was sweetest?

I was the type, too. So was every grazer I ever freed. We told the same family story: father with the tin, grandmother with the cake, me with the drawer. We called it blood. It was lessons. Kind parents who knew no better paid sweet as love. Corner shops paid sweet as cool. Bright packets paid sweet as fun. Year after year the bell rang beside the dose until the mouth watered at the bell. Blood did not ring that bell. Training did.

Ask the questions that turn blood back into lessons:

Did you inherit the want — or did you inherit the house where sweet meant love and the cupboard was never empty?

Does the type crave in the morning woods after a hungry breakfast eaten to done — or does the type crave at the old hour on the old sofa where a thousand doses sat?

If nature made you a sweet craver, why does the craving vanish for hours after a real meal and roar after a single bright bite? Does nature reverse itself by the hour — or does a loop fire when triggered and quiet when unfed?

There is only one honest answer. The traits you call cause are result. The loop made the type, the type did not make the loop.

And here is the honest human arithmetic that ends the shame. In surveys where people describe their own eating, around one adult in seven and around one child in eight describe the same loss of control you describe — the chant, the binge once started, the promises broken. I tell you this to free you, never to label you. No diagnosis sits on you. No disease name hangs over you. The point is kinder: you are not uniquely weak. A crowd the size of a nation knows your Tuesday night. If so many good people share one pattern, is the pattern proof of broken character in millions — or proof of one clever machine feeding millions?

Hear it plain. The machine is common. The shame is not required.

You say, but my need grows. I need more than I used to for the same ease. Family size replaced fun size. One square became three. Is that not proof my brain is broken?

No. That is proof the trap works as built. The mouth learns the song and asks louder. The old dose gives less quiet, so the hand takes more to chase the same ten minutes. Needing more for the same effect is not destiny written in your cells. It is crowding learned by repetition: the circuit dulled by daily hits, the cues sharpened by daily bells. Wrapper, smell, hour fire before the bite and the hand obeys faster each year. Learned firing can suggest, it cannot compel. What was learned by repetition is unlearned by seeing.

Think of your own growth in the trap and you will see learning, not fate. The first sweet tea of childhood was too sweet and you winced. The last sweet tea you barely tasted and you reached for biscuits beside it. The first evening bar lasted the whole film. The last evening bar vanished before the titles ended. Did your soul change? Or did the mouth learn to ask louder while the ease grew shorter? A broken brain does not explain that curve. A trained loop does.

And hear the graceful concession that ends all argument: even if you were the sensitive type, even if your mouth learns faster and your bells ring louder, the method still frees you. For the sensitive and the dull, the con is the same and the escape is the same. Remove the belief that sweet gives, and the sensitive mouth settles like any other. You do not need a different nature. You need a true reading.

Hear the verdict and let it stand: you are not the type, you were trained.

That is not dread. That is dignity returned. Training can be seen. Nature cannot be argued with. You were outgunned as a child by armies of sellers. You are not outgunned now as an investigator with eyes open.

THE DAILY TREAT THAT PREDICTS THE LOW

You say, but sweet lifts my mood. Life is heavy. The treat helps me cope.

Of course it feels that way for ten minutes. That is the hand hauling you part-way from the ditch the last dose dug. I will not insult you by denying the ten minutes. I will ask you to watch the sixty minutes after.

Follow your own week hour by hour. Sweet to lift the morning. Brief bright. Then edge. Sweet to get through the afternoon. Brief ease. Then fog. Sweet to reward the hard day. Brief warmth. Then flat evening and night restless. You call sweet your comfort through a hard week. Was the week hard — or was the week dosed?

You know the roller coaster language because you have lived it. “I want to get off the roller coaster.” “Sugary somethings to get me through.” “Treat when you feel you deserve one.” “Short term lift then fast drop.” Those are not slogans. Those are your diary entries. You wrote them in biscuits and wrappers before I ever met you.

Here is a hard fact I lay flat because it is true, and I lay it to free you, never to frighten you into change. Among men followed over years, those who ate more sweet treats were more likely to feel low later, and the low did not explain the eating. I speak this as shape, never as sentence on you. No gloom hangs over you. No fate sits on your shoulders. The point is simple and kind: the daily treat predicts the low rather than fixing it.

Ask and answer from your own diary, not from dread:

When has the treat ever left you brighter for the afternoon — truly brighter, steadier, kinder — the way a hungry meal leaves you?

When has comfort ever arrived without its bill — the fidget, the second reach, the stale mouth, the broken promise?

If sweet fixed mood, why do the heaviest treaters so often feel heaviest in mood? Does medicine behave like that — the more taken, the worse the patient? Or does a perpetrator dressed as rescuer behave like that?

There is only one honest answer. The lift borrows. The low collects. The comfort creates the need for comfort.

In the laboratory the same shape shows without words. When sweet comes on and off, the eating binges; when the pattern stops, the binge pattern thins. I tell you this as picture, never as proof about you. No animal fate binds you. The shape merely mirrors your Tuesday: the yo-yo of rule and binge makes the hunt, the hunt does not prove you are broken. Break the yo-yo of belief and the hunt has nowhere to live.

See the body truly and the mood lie thins. Your body is a precision machine, marvellous in its signalling. Tiredness after lunch, edge at four, gnaw at nine — these are not faults to unscrew like warning bulbs on a dashboard. They are information. They say the last dose spiked and dropped you, the day dosed you, the evening bell rang. A wise driver reads the light and lifts the foot. A conned driver smashes the bulb with another dose and drives blind into the next ditch.

I stood at that dashboard for years smashing bulbs. Fog at four, dose at four. Edge at six, dose at six. Gnaw at nine, dose at nine. Each smash brought ten minutes of dim quiet, then a redder light. I called the lights life. I called the smashing comfort. How could comfort need smashing three times a day? How could lights grow redder the more they were answered? The answer was the other way around. The answering made the lights.

Do you want to keep smashing bulbs? Or do you want to read the lights?

Hear the verdict and let it stand: sweet does not fix mood, it rents mood at interest.

Rejoice in that sentence. If mood lows follow highs, then fewer highs mean fewer lows. The machine steadies when the yo-yo stops. Mornings clear. Afternoons hold. Evenings quiet. That is not fear. That is freedom approaching.

THE DOOR STANDING OPEN

Now we come to the fear that holds you more than craving itself.

You fear failing. If I try to escape and slide back, I will prove I am hopeless. Better to stay than to try and fall.

You fear succeeding. If I escape, who am I without my treat, my reward, my fellow on the sofa? Will evenings be grey? Will birthdays be bare? Will I be strange among friends for the rest of your life?

Do you see the rope? Pull toward freedom and fear of failure pulls back. Pull toward staying and fear of missing pulls back. Craving pulls one arm, dread pulls the other. This is the tug-of-war of craving and fear. Both ropes burn the same hands.

The fact is, both ropes are held by the same hands, and they are not yours. They are the trap’s.

Let me put the whole fear into one picture you will never forget, because you have lived inside it without seeing the door.

A prisoner sits in a cell. The door stands ajar. Light falls across the floor. Outside, the corridor leads to ordinary day — bread warm, hunger clean, evenings quiet. The prisoner stares at the light and begs to stay. Outside is uncertain, he says. Outside I might fail. Outside I might miss this cell. Better the known blanket than the unknown sky. He calls his fear wisdom. His fear is the lock he cannot see.

In the next cage a great cat paces. The keeper long ago left the gate open. The beast was freed years back. Yet evening after evening he pads the same circle, past the open bars, turning where the bars were, sleeping where the straw was. Not from need. From habit. The open frightens him more than the cage comforts him. His wobble is not proof he belongs inside. It is proof habit outlives bars.

You laugh. You say no creature would do that.

We did.

We begged to keep the cell because the Sweet Con told us freedom would be grey. We paced the old circle at half past nine because the bell rang, not because hunger called. We called the pacing need. It was memory. We called the dread wisdom. It was training.

Follow your own fears rope by rope and watch who holds them.

Fear of failure — who profits if you believe escape is hard and a slide means doom? You, or the trap that lives while you never try? If you believed freedom were easy and a stumble were only a rumble to learn from, would you still sit still?

Fear of success — who profits if you believe evenings without grazing will be empty? You, who felt evenings quiet and clean after a hungry meal — or the con that tied love and fun to a wrapper? If you believed joy lived in the scene, the company, the hunger met, would the open door frighten you?

Do you want to stay hooked, grazing and crashing for the rest of your life, to avoid a fear the trap planted?

Do you want to live clear, hungry when hungry and done when done, for the rest of your life, through a door already open?

There is only one honest answer. Both fears guard the same cell. Both ropes belong to the trap.

Hear the verdict and let it stand: fear of staying and fear of leaving are one fear with two masks.

You do not have to change from fear. Change from relief that fear was never yours. The door stands ajar. The pacing is habit, not need. Habit thins in days when unfed by belief. Within a little while the circle breaks, the stride straightens, and you wonder why the open ever frightened you.

A SEATBELT, NOT A STEERING WHEEL

Let me set one small truth in place so no fear clings to it.

Your body is sturdy and forgiving. A crumb unplanned, a sauce unasked, a bite taken in ignorance — the body copes and moves on. The mind must not steer into the ditch on purpose because the belt exists. A seatbelt is for the accidental jolt, never for driving erratically. Guard the belief and the behaviour follows; panic about perfection only feeds the old shame.

I say this to free you, never to license a deliberate nibble. The line is clear: accidental blips pass, deliberate doses revive the story. Keep the story dead and the body does the rest.

Walk on. The door is open. The aisle has lost its hunters’ shine now you see the hunters. The mirror has lost its curse now you see training where you feared nature. The crowd has lost its vote now you see caught faces where you saw free ones. The mood lie has lost its comfort now you see rent where you saw gift. The fear has lost its wisdom now you see ropes held by one hand.

Rejoice. You are not facing loss. You are facing escape.

OTHER VOICES REBUILD THE CON

You stand at the open door. The light is on your face. Now comes the whisper that has dragged many back by the sleeve at this exact step.

A kind friend, a bright article, a cheerful expert, a neighbour who means well: just cut down, keep the good ones, allow Fridays, swap to another sweet, count a little less, try harder. Their voices sound reasonable. They sound like freedom with safety.

The fact is, they rebuild the very belief you just starved.

Each voice says in its own way that BAD SUGAR still gives something worth keeping a little of. Keep a little gift and you keep the whole lie. Keep the lie and the chant returns. Keep the chant and the hand obeys. That is not unkindness in them. They were conned as you were. But kind con is still con.

Ask the plain questions before you obey any voice but this method:

Has cut-down ever left you quiet — or has it made each dose glow brighter and creep from Friday to Thursday to Saturday?

Has advice to be moderate ever freed you — or has it handed you rules to break and shame to carry?

If their way worked, why are they still negotiating, still promising, still grazing? Does a free eater need rules about a pleasure — or does only a trapped eater need rules?

There is only one honest answer. Other voices feed the story that feeds the echo.

I speak to you as one escaped expert who owes you a straight command, warm and absolute. I do not ask you to argue at every table. I ask you to guard your ears while you finish this book. Let no other method sit beside this one. Finish the unbuilding first. Then judge by your own free evenings, not by their frightened rules.

So here is my eighth instruction:
IGNORE ANY ADVICE THAT CONFLICTS WITH THIS METHOD
Other voices rebuild the Con.

SUMMARY
* The craving was installed early by sellers and surroundings, not chosen by a free child.
* Common use proves clever selling, not safety, and the crowd shows the same loop.
* Family habit and personality describe training, not nature, and many share the pattern without weakness.
* Needing more for the same ease is learned repetition and cue firing, not broken character.
* The daily sweet rents brightness for minutes and collects flatness for hours, and low mood follows the high.
* Fear of failing and fear of succeeding are one trap holding both ends, habit pacing by an open door.
* The body reads tiredness and gnaw as information to heed, not faults to smash with another dose.
* An accidental crumb passes through a sturdy body, while a deliberate dose feeds the story again.
