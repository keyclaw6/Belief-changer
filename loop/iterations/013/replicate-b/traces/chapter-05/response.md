Chapter 5
THE TREAT THAT WAS NEVER A TREAT

**IN THIS CHAPTER**
We listen to the voice that says you earned it, we strip the wrapper off Maya's post-bath chocolate to see who was really warm, we put the tight shoes back on as a treat, and we walk the shouting aisle without envying anyone in it.

*The comfort was the evening and you — the sugar was only sneaking a ride.*

### THE REWARD VOICE

We have put the energy lie to bed. You saw it without my help: the lift never took you above normal, it only returned you toward it, like taking off tight shoes you did not need to put on.

And now the next story steps forward, softer and more tender, and that is why it lasts longer.

"I've earned it."

"I've been good this week."

"I deserve it."

"just a treat."

We say it after the bath, after the argument, after the school run that never ended, after the email where you finally held your ground. The house is finally quiet. The chocolate is in your hand and it feels like someone is being kind to you at last — perhaps the only person who has been kind all day.

Maya knows this moment by heart. Bath done, towel warm, kitchen clean, one small bar on the side that feels like love that asks nothing back. Or the other version: the day went badly, voices were raised, you cried in the bathroom, and the packet in the drawer says, "Here. This will make it better." Or the bright version: you did something brave and good, and the till at the celebration says, "celebrating, rewarding myself, going to the cinema, etc with chocolate" — and it feels true in your chest.

I am not going to argue with your need for comfort. I am going to pull the comfort into the light and show you who was actually giving it.

Let me ask you as you answer to yourself, not to me:

When you felt soothed on that sofa, was it the dose that was warm — or were *you* warm, *you* tired, *you* relieved that the day had finally released you?

If the same ten minutes had happened — same lamplight, same quiet, same clean kitchen, same sense of *mine at last* — with a small bowl of real food you like, or with nothing in your hand at all except the tea steaming beside you, would the evening have been empty, or would the evening still have been yours?

And have you ever noticed that the soothe lasts until the last square, and then, within the hour, you feel a little more unsettled than before you started, not more settled?

You know the answers because you have lived them. The moment held the warmth. You held the warmth. The dose signed its name on a feeling it did not make.

This is the work of the Confectioner. Not a physical hunger, but the sweet story that sold you the meaning of that moment — that love and reward live inside a wrapper, that stress can be stroked away by sugar, that goodness must be paid for in doses. The Confectioner fed the story; the Nibbler gnawed quietly underneath and made the story feel true in the body. Two creatures, one job: to keep you believing that a genuine pleasure, treat or lift was arriving when, in fact, nothing was arriving at all.

### WHO WAS ACTUALLY SOOTHING YOU

Take Maya after the argument. She sits on the edge of the bath. Eyes hot. She tells herself she is being gentle with herself by breaking off chocolate. She feels a softening as it melts. For a minute kindness seems to exist.

Now edit the scene with the sugar removed and nothing else changed. Same bath. Same apology waiting to be given tomorrow. Same quiet house. Her shoulders are still falling. Her breathing is still slowing. Her hand is still learning, again, that she can be tender with herself without a purchase. The softness that arrived with the bite is still there — because it was the pause, the permission, the return to herself that softened her. The sugar did not create care; it borrowed it.

Take Maya after the win — the promotion, the tidy inbox, the run she finally did. She says, "I deserve it," and reaches. The pride is real. The body is humming with done. She eats the "treat" and the pride dulls by a notch, not brightens. She tells herself she celebrated. What she did was confuse the signal of having pleased herself with the act of feeding the trap. Your body, your instinct and real food would have let that pride stay clean and complete — a meal eaten to appetite, water, a piece of whole fruit if you wanted sweetness, and the whole evening still ahead.

The fact is we learned this story early and without blame. Food was love in your house, as it was in mine, as it is in most houses — offered when you cried, offered when you shone. No one meant a con. They were passing on the brainwashing they were given. But a kindness repeated at the wrong address becomes a belief: that comfort must come as BAD SUGAR, that celebration must be centred on it, that stress has a sweet antidote.

We can honour where that story started without keeping it.

There is another thing you have noticed and then explained away. The reward does not hold. In longer looks at men's lives, those who sweetened more often were more likely to feel low later on — and that is not a sentence on you, just what showed up in the numbers — not for everyone, not proof of a straight cause, men only, and life is tangled. Still, it lands where you already live: the "treat" that promised to lift the mood is the one that most reliably predicts the mood sinking after. The Confectioner told you the dose would repair stress. The aftermath tells the opposite story, in your own evenings.

And before the bite there is that strange tug you know too well — the wrapper sound, the smell from the tin, the sight of the packet on the counter, and wanting fires before you have even decided. We see that firing most clearly in animals when a cue lights up the wanting circuit, as an analogue for what you feel at the supermarket or the desk drawer — not a diagnosis, not a human withdrawal proven in a clinic, just the honest shape of cue-driven wanting doing its work. The tug is not proof you need it. It is proof the story has trained your attention.

### TIGHT SHOES AS A TREAT

Let us put the picture back on so the treat-frame cannot sneak past.

We wear tight shoes all day for the pleasure of taking them off. We call that pleasure a treat. We wrap it, we price it, we give it to ourselves for being good.

Do you see it now? A treat that first has to make you pinch is not a treat at all. It is a con selling you relief from its own pinch.

BAD SUGAR does the same with comfort. It creates that mild, empty, slightly insecure, slightly gnawing feeling, hangs it on your afternoon and your evening, then arrives with a ten-minute quiet and asks to be called love. You were not comforted by the dose. You were relieved of a little discomfort the dose had helped create, inside a moment that was already comforting you.

If it were a genuine pleasure, treat or lift, it would add to the evening without first subtracting. It would leave you fuller than it found you. It would not ask you to return an hour later for the same repair. A genuine treat does not behave like that. A con does.

Ask the question whose answer you already know:

If the packet had not been in the house at all, would the bath have been less warm, the quiet less quiet, the pride less proud?

The warmth was yours. The sugar was only sneaking a ride.

### LONELY WHITE CARBS INVITE COMPANY

There is a second sleight of hand tucked inside the treat story, and it explains why the "just one" feels impossible once it begins.

Some foods are lonely. White bread, crisps, pastries, the pale soft carbs that were engineered to be light and fast — they do not satisfy the way real food does, they invite company. They call sugar over. Sugar calls fat over. Together at the bliss point they shout louder than either alone. That is the House-Party Gatecrasher: the lonely white carb opening the door, the sweet fix gatecrashing, the whole plate turning into a dose that leaves you wanting more, not less.

You did not fail a test of character when "I just crave sweets and when i eat them, i feel like i cant stop" turned out to be true and "one bite becomes the box" followed. The plate was designed to do exactly that. Your body, your instinct and real food do not do that — they know the signal for enough, they receive it, they let you get on with your evening. The loneliness was not in you.

### THE AISLE THAT SHOUTS

Walk it once with me, not to avoid it, but to see it plain.

Supermarket aisle. Lights bright. Wrappers shouting in colours that were chosen for your eye. Smells engineered to fire before the bite. Tongs of engineered colours. The Nibbler stirs, the Confectioner translates the stir as "you deserve a treat," and for a second it looks like pleasure is on sale.

Look again, with the story off.

Does that really look like pleasure? People stooped, scanning, gripping baskets, feeding a gnaw that will be back before they reach the car park. They are not indulging. They are servicing a Trap that never lets them feel done. You are not being deprived watching them — they are.

That is the pity script I want you to rehearse, briefly, before our next chapter forecloses the last escape route for good. Not envy, not scorn — pity that is kind: "There goes someone still being told that love lives in a wrapper." You know the voice that told you the same, and you know whose warmth was really there all along.

We have not said you may never enjoy sweetness again. Whole fruit in its whole form, a meal that satisfies, the aftertaste of real food — those are not BAD SUGAR and were never the problem. We are saying something simpler and more freeing: dessert as self-payment keeps the loop alive. The love you thought you were eating was never inside the dose.

You were the comfort. You were the celebration. You were the soothe after stress. The moment was the treat — and it is yours without paying the con.

**SUMMARY**

* The "I've earned it" and "just a treat" feeling was not added by BAD SUGAR — the warmth, quiet and pride were already in the moment; the dose only borrowed them.
* Reward-as-dessert keeps the loop going; long-term looks in men link more sweets with later low mood, which matches your own aftertaste — without being a diagnosis or a sentence on you.
* The pre-bite tug fired by wrappers and smells is cue-driven wanting, seen clearly in animals as an analogue — not proof you need a dose, but proof the story has trained your attention.
* Lonely white carbs invite sugar and fat to gatecrash the same plate, which is why one bite becomes the box — your body, your instinct and real food do not behave that way; they let you feel done.
