Chapter 4
THE ENERGY LIE

**IN THIS CHAPTER**
We open the 3pm drawer and listen to what it really says, we ride the roller coaster you thought was carrying you, we put the tight shoes on in the office so you can feel the trick, and we let your body show you what steady really feels like.

*That lift wasn't fuel arriving — it was the low you fed going quiet for a few minutes.*

### THE 3PM WHISPER

Maya, it's 3:07. The meeting has been going on too long. Air warm. Tea going cold. Someone pushed the biscuit tin to the middle of the table to be helpful. You tell yourself you are fine, and then the drawer in your head rattles.

"I need sugar to function."

"I need something sweet to get me through."

"I need sugary somethings to get me through — just this one, then I'll be fine."

We have all heard that voice. It does not shout. It whispers, reasonable, almost kind. It points at the wrapper crackle as if the wrapper were oxygen.

I know how convincing it feels in the body. That mild, empty, slightly insecure, slightly gnawing feeling arrives on schedule. Mouth a little flat. Focus a little thin. A faint urgency that says, "I-need-to-eat-something-RIGHT-NOW." You answer it with a biscuit, a chocolate, a sweet drink, and for ten minutes the fog lifts. Your shoulders drop. You can think again. Of course you conclude that BAD SUGAR gave you energy. What else would you conclude?

But we are going to look at that ten minutes without the story that sold it to you, because the story is doing all the work.

Let me ask you as you would answer without polishing, just from your own afternoons:

When that lift arrived, did you feel brighter than you feel on a morning after a good meal when you haven't had any BAD SUGAR at all — or did you just feel less empty than you did two minutes before the bite?

If BAD SUGAR were genuine fuel, why did you need another dose an hour later, and why did the day end tired, wired and dulled instead of steady?

If a person at the next desk ate real food at lunch and never opened the drawer, are they sitting there unable to function for lack of a biscuit?

You know the honest answers. The lift did not take you above normal. It returned you toward normal. And then it left you below it.

The fact is you were not being lifted. You were being relieved — briefly — from a low the previous dose had created. That relief felt like energy only because the low felt like needing energy. It's the other way around.

### THE ROLLER COASTER YOU THOUGHT WAS FUEL

You have called it by its true name yourself: "the roller coaster" and "I want to get off the roller coaster." Let us ride it once with the lights on.

We eat a dose of BAD SUGAR. For a few minutes there is a small climb — sweetness, brightness, a sense of being topped up. Then the drop. The gnaw returns. Restlessness. That flat, edgy hunger that is not real hunger. The thought arrives uninvited: need something sweet. We answer with another dose. A shorter climb, a steeper drop. By late afternoon the car is rattling — up, down, up, down — and we call that rattling "my energy problem" and reach for the very thing that built the track.

You can watch this from the outside in one line that people who have escaped often say in their own words: "I would have something quick and sweet but now know that will only give a short term lift which will inevitably be followed by a fast drop." That is not theory. That is what the ride feels like when you stop calling the climb fuel.

Here is the quiet truth about the drop that makes the whispers so believable. When you binge BAD SUGAR in that stop-start way — be good, then give in, then be good again — the body does show a real dip. A small dip. A measurable dip in the brain's reward chemistry after the binge, a brief opposite push that leaves you lower than before. In animals you can even precipitate it and watch it. In you it arrives as that gnaw, that wanting, that sense you cannot stop once you start. The dip is real, and it is small, and it passes. It is not a diagnosis and it is not clinical hypoglycemia.

And that matters, because the whisper borrows medical language to frighten you: "my blood sugar crashed." For most healthy people, that familiar 3pm slump happens without blood sugar anywhere near a low clinical number. Some people do get a real reactive dip after a sweet meal. Most of us get the symptoms — fog, edginess, the urgent sweet craving — without that low value at all. The feeling is real; the label is wrong. The roller coaster you feel is not your blood failing you. It is the trap using its own drop as proof you need another climb.

Do you see the con? The Confectioner tells you the coaster proves you need the lift. The coaster itself proves BAD SUGAR is doing TO you what you thought it was doing FOR you.

And if you have looked at brain pictures and worried that you have blunted or damaged something, hear this gently: those pictures are interesting and contested. Some show a kind of crowding where the same dose feels less enjoyable and the wrappers and smells fire wanting before you even take a bite. That fits exactly what you have lived — "need family size," "the smell hits and I'm gone" — but imaging alone does not prove damage, and it is interpretation, not a sentence on you. The wanting is cue-driven and it is learned, and what is learned can be unlearned when the belief goes.

So we can say it flat, without hedging and without frightening you:

BAD SUGAR never gave you energy. It leased you back a fraction of the ease it had taken, for a few minutes, on the condition you would pay again.

That tidy story it sold you for every occasion — that this was a genuine pleasure, treat or lift — collapses right here on the energy claim. A genuine pleasure, treat or lift would leave you more than you were. This left you less, then asked to be thanked for the brief pause in taking.

It's the other way around.

### TIGHT SHOES AT YOUR DESK

Let us put the picture where you can feel it in your body.

We put on tight shoes two sizes too small for the morning. They pinch with every step. By eleven your feet throb. At 11:15 you slip them off. Ahhh. Relief floods in. You sigh. You say, "That feels wonderful."

Did the tight shoes give you a pleasure? Or did they manufacture a pain and then rent you the ending of it?

We laugh at the question for shoes. We would never say, "I get a genuine pleasure from tight shoes." We say, "I get relief from taking them off — relief I would not have needed if I had never put them on."

That is the 3pm desk slump.

We put the tight shoes on with the last dose — yesterday evening, this morning, at lunch — on phoney information that said a sweet would comfort or carry us. That dose created the faint physical stir and then the belief that another dose would fix it. From then on every hit felt helpful because it took the gnaw away for ten minutes. But the gnaw was its own doing. You never rose above the steady ease of a person who never put the shoes on that day. You only returned, briefly, toward it.

Think of that meeting again. Tea, biscuits, focus. Who did the focusing? Who listened, weighed, found the word? You did. Your body had steady fuel from the meal you ate hours earlier, your instinct knew when you were satisfied and when you were not, and real food had done its quiet work without applause. The biscuit arrived after the work and signed its name on your feeling. It was only ever sneaking a ride.

We have all done this. We ate a satisfying lunch — soup, bread, fruit, water — and for hours we did not think about food. No counting. No gnaw. No roller coaster. That was not willpower. That was your body, your instinct and real food agreeing with each other. The drawer stayed quiet because there were no tight shoes on.

So ask once more, with the shoes off:

Did the dose give you the concentration, or did it briefly quiet the distraction it had created an hour before?

Were you energized, or were you relieved of tired, wired and dulled for ten minutes and then returned to tired, wired and dulled?

If the shoes truly helped you walk, why did you always need to take them off again?

The verdict is short, and you can feel it as settled fact now.

The lift is the low.

### WHAT WAS ACTUALLY KEEPING YOU GOING

If BAD SUGAR was not doing it FOR you, what was?

Your body already makes the steady glucose it needs from the food you eat as meals — from protein and the rest of what real food contains — so that going without BAD SUGAR is not deprivation at all. That is not a diet prescription and it is not a new rule to count. It only tells you what is already true: you were never dependent on a dose to function; you were dependent on a story that said you were.

The fact is the ease you call normal is still waiting, intact, underneath the ride. When the Confectioner stops talking over it, your body, your instinct and real food do the work simply and without drama. You eat. You are satisfied. You get on with your day. The brightness you remembered after the biscuit was not borrowed brightness. It was your own attention returning when the gnaw went quiet.

And because I have spoken about dips and gnaws and brain chemistry, let me land this where it belongs so fear does not take the wheel: I do not want you to stop from fear, and that small dip is not a life sentence — it is mild, it is brief, and it dies quickly when you stop feeding it, and wonderfully quickly the background hum of wanting fades. If you have diabetes, take medication that affects blood sugar, have an eating disorder, or are under medical advice about food, do not change medication or prescribed eating on the basis of a belief-change book — speak to your clinician. This book changes a belief about BAD SUGAR, not a medical treatment. — and that is not a warning to frighten you, it is a kindness to keep the belief work clean.

You do not need to brace. All you have to do is follow all the instructions and keep watching the axis we turned last chapter: doing TO you versus doing FOR you. On the energy axis, the good column is empty.

We are not leaving you without fuel. We are revealing you were never without it.

Maya, the next time the drawer whispers "you need sugar to function," you will hear it for what it is — the tight shoes pinching, asking to be taken off. You will not argue with the whisper. You will not fight it. You will see it: a trap doing TO you what you thought was FOR you, renting you ten minutes of quiet and calling it a gift.

And that seeing is already freedom, because a person who sees tight shoes does not crave tighter shoes.

**SUMMARY**

* The 3pm lift was not fuel — it was relief from a low the previous BAD SUGAR dose created, like taking off tight shoes you did not need to put on.
* The familiar roller coaster — climb, drop, craving, repeat — is the trap's form, not your character or your blood failing; everyday slumps often happen without any clinical low value.
* The dip after a binge is real but small and brief and it passes when you stop feeding it; imaging talk about blunting is interpretation, not proof of damage.
* Steady energy was never imported by a dose — your body makes what it needs from real food, and your body, your instinct and real food keep you going without the ride.
