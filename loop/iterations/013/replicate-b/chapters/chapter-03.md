Chapter 3
WHAT GOOD IS THERE AT ALL?

**IN THIS CHAPTER**
We turn your private weighing over and ask a different question, you try on a pair of shoes that are two sizes too small, you ride the familiar roller coaster without the story that sold it to you, and you feel what your body was quietly doing for you the whole time.

*There is nothing to weigh up — BAD SUGAR is doing plenty TO you and nothing FOR you.*

### THE QUESTION YOU THOUGHT YOU WERE ASKING

Maya, you came to this book with a scale in your head.

On one side, the harm. You know the restless afternoons. You know the hidden packets. You know tired, wired and dulled is not how a person wants to feel for the rest of your life.

On the other side, the good. The little lift. The comfort after a hard day. The way a biscuit makes tea feel like tea, the way a chocolate at your desk feels like concentration, the way a sweet on the sofa feels like being kind to yourself.

So the private negotiation goes: "I know it's not great for me, but is the pleasure worth it?"

We have all lived inside that weighing. We kept telling ourselves the harm might be manageable if the benefit were real. We told ourselves we were making a balanced choice.

I want to show you there is no balance to make. Not because the harm is heavy, but because the good is not there at all.

The fact is we were measuring the wrong thing. We were asking does it do more harm than good. The question that frees you is different: what good is there at all?

Let me ask you as you would answer at your kitchen table, honestly and without polishing:

When you picture the best dose you had this month, what was good about it — the substance itself, or the moment it happened in?

If the same biscuit had been offered to you straight after a large, satisfying meal when you were warm and rested and loved, would it have felt like a genuine need?

If BAD SUGAR truly gave you something, why did the wanting always return, a little stronger, an hour later?

You already feel the answer. The pleasure you remember was not in the dose. It was in the pause, the warmth, the company, the brief rest. The dose only sneaked a ride on top of them and then took the credit.

That is the whole trap in one switch. It is doing plenty TO you. It is doing nothing FOR you.

### TRY ON THE TIGHT SHOES

We need a picture you can feel in your body right now.

Imagine you put on a pair of tight shoes two sizes too small and wear them all morning. Every step pinches. By lunchtime your feet ache. Then, at last, you kick them off. Ahh — the flood of relief. You sigh. You say, "That feels wonderful."

Did the shoes give you a pleasure? Or did they manufacture a pain and then rent you the relief from it?

We know the answer instantly for shoes. We would never say, "I get a genuine pleasure from tight shoes." We would say, "I get relief from taking them off — relief I would not have needed if I had never put them on."

BAD SUGAR works the same way.

We put the tight shoes on with the first dose taken on phoney information. That dose created a faint physical stir — that mild, empty, slightly insecure, slightly gnawing feeling — and the belief that the next dose would soothe it. From then on every hit seemed to help because it took the gnaw away for ten minutes. But the gnaw was its own doing. You never rose above the ease a person who never put the shoes on feels all day. You only returned, briefly, toward it, and then the ache returned and asked for another dose.

Think of your own day. The 3pm drawer. The 10pm packet. The relief after the first bite. Was that a lift? Or was it the shoes coming off?

It's the other way around. It didn't lift you. It lowered you, then briefly stopped lowering you, and called that a lift.

And here is where the phrase we will use from now on finally earns its place. For years the Confectioner has sold you one tidy story to cover every occasion — energy, comfort, reward, sociability, habit — and compressed it into five words so it could survive any disappointment. He called it a genuine pleasure, treat or lift. Say it plainly now and hear how small it is when spoken aloud. A genuine pleasure, treat or lift. That is the claim we are testing. Not "a little harmless sweet." A genuine pleasure. A genuine treat. A genuine lift. We have just tried it against the tight shoes, and it did not survive the test. The relief you felt was real; the source you gave it to was wrong.

That is not a loss. That is good news. If the benefit was borrowed, you lose nothing by escaping. You only stop putting the shoes on.

### THE ROLLER COASTER THAT RIDES YOU

Maya, you have used the words yourself: "the roller coaster." We all have. Up, down, up, down. We believed BAD SUGAR was the thing that got us up. Let us ride it once without the story and see who built the track.

You have a biscuit with your tea. For a few minutes you feel a small climb — sweetness on the tongue, a brief brightening. Then, inevitably, the drop. The gnaw returns, a little heavier. Flatness. Nippiness. The thought arrives uninvited: "I need something sweet to get me through." You answer it with another dose. The climb returns, shorter this time, then the drop again. By evening you are tired, wired and dulled, and you wonder why willpower feels so hard when all you wanted was a little energy.

The Confectioner tells you that ride proves you need the lift. The truth is the ride itself proves the trap is doing TO you what you thought it was doing FOR you. It creates the low it then relieves. It never gave you energy; it leased you back a fraction of the ease it had taken, then charged you again.

You can see this most clearly at the 3pm desk. The drawer rattles. A wrapper crackles. The whisper says "you need sugar to function." And for ten minutes after the dose, you do feel function return. But whose function? The person at the next desk who ate real food at lunch and never rode the coaster at all is not yawning into their hand. Their blood is steady without you having to do anything clever. The lift you felt was not fuel arriving. It was the gnaw going quiet because it was fed — the shoes off for ten minutes.

The fact is your body already knows how to keep you going without importing sugar. It manufactures the glucose it needs from the food you eat as meals — from protein and from the rest of what real food contains — so that going without BAD SUGAR is not deprivation at all. It is simply not putting the shoes on. That does not prescribe a diet and it does not promise a new eating religion. It only tells you what is already true: you were never dependent on a dose to function; you were dependent on a story that said you were.

And that story is now thinner than it looked. Because there are people — many people — who disagree completely about whether sugar can ever be called addictive in a clinical sense, and they still agree on this shape: when doses come and go — be good, then have just one, then be good again — the urge to keep going comes back hard, and one bite wants to become the box. When the same sweetness is simply there all the time with no rule around it, that frantic cannot-stop does not appear in the same way. That is an honest, open question in science, not a verdict either way, and I am not asking you to take a side. I am asking you to notice what you have lived: the intermittency — the rule, the restraint, the "just this once" — is what re-fires the wanting. The sugary something does not prove you are broken. It proves the trap's form works as designed.

So ask again, with the coaster in view:

Was the climb a pleasure the dose gave you, or relief from a drop the previous dose had built?

Did you ever, once, finish a day of riding and think "I am more energized than a person who never rode at all"? Or did you finish precisely where they started, only more tired?

If it were truly doing something FOR you, would you need to keep queuing for the next climb?

You know the honest answer. The coaster is doing plenty TO you. It is carrying you exactly nowhere FOR you.

It steals your ease and sells it back in ten-minute rentals. It leaves you tired, wired and dulled and calls that normal. It is not normal. It is the state of wearing tight shoes all day and calling the moment they come off happiness.

No genuine pleasure. No genuine treat. No genuine lift. Only shoes on, shoes off.

### WHAT WAS ALREADY DOING THE WORK

If BAD SUGAR was not doing it FOR you, what was?

The moment you remove the dose from the picture, the credit has to go somewhere — and it goes exactly where it belonged all along.

The tea that warmed your hands after the children were finally asleep. The lamplight. The quiet house. The breath out after a long day. You were the warmth in that scene, not the tin. Your body, your instinct and real food were the instruments that made the moment feel good; the dose only sat on top and signed its name on your feeling.

Think of the concentration you thought came from tea and biscuits in the meeting. Who did the thinking? Who listened, who found the word, who stayed with the problem until it gave way? You did. Your body had steady fuel from the meal you ate earlier, your instinct chose when you were hungry and when you were satisfied, and real food had already done its quiet work without applause. The biscuit arrived after the work was done and claimed the credit as it passed.

The fact is we have confused the rider for the ride. We rode days of real life — work, love, rest, hunger, satisfaction — and BAD SUGAR sneaked a ride for a few minutes and told us we could not have ridden without it. You can. You already did, on every day you ate properly and forgot to crave. You already do, in every moment the Confectioner is not talking over your body.

Do you remember an ordinary lunch that satisfied you — soup, bread, fruit, water — after which you felt calm and done and did not think about food for hours? That was not willpower. That was your body, your instinct and real food agreeing with each other. No counting, no fighting, no roller coaster. No tight shoes.

The belief we are leaving in this chapter is simple and total: there is no good to weigh up. There is only a trap that does TO you what you thought was FOR you.

And that changes everything, because if there is no genuine pleasure, treat or lift to sacrifice, there is no sacrifice. There is only escape. We are not giving anything up. We are getting off a ride that was riding us.

You do not need to fix your feelings about this. You only need to see it.

### IGNORE THE NOISE WHILE YOU SEE IT CLEARLY

You will hear other voices as you read. A friend will say "a little of what you fancy." A wrapper will say "reward yourself." A headline will say sugar is harmless, or sugar is poison, or you have an addictive personality, or you just need discipline.

While you are reading this book, let them pass.

5. IGNORE ANY ADVICE THAT CONFLICTS WITH THIS BOOK WHILE YOU READ
Hear them as the Confectioner talking through other mouths and keep your eyes on what your own experience just proved.

We will isolate every single claimed benefit and watch the credit return to its true owner — concentration, habit, sociability, reward — one by one, until not a single cord is left plugged in. For now, it is enough to have turned the ledger over and seen that the good column was empty. Hold that. The next chapters give the detail; this chapter gave you the axis. The axis is everything.

**SUMMARY**

* You are not weighing harm against a real pleasure — there is no genuine benefit to weigh; BAD SUGAR does plenty TO you and nothing FOR you.
* The "lift" you felt was relief from a low the previous dose created, like taking off tight shoes you did not need to put on — you never rose above the ease of not wearing them at all.
* Your body already makes the steady fuel it needs from real food, so going without BAD SUGAR is not deprivation; the roller coaster you called normal is the trap's form, not your character.
* The warmth, comfort and concentration you credited to the dose belonged to the moment, to you, and to your body, your instinct and real food — the dose only sneaked a ride.
