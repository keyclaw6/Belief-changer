### THE AISLE THAT KNEW YOUR NAME

We walked it together last chapter with a hundred cords still lit. Now let's walk it again, slower, and read what is actually written on the walls.

Maya pushes a trolley that squeaks. The lights are too white, the music too happy. At the end of the aisle the wrappers are shouting. Red, gold, cartoon bears. A smell of warm sugar is pumped where there is no bakery. The delivery app on her phone pings: *just for you — you loved these — add for £1.* It knows the exact Tuesday she cracks.

We were taught to call this choice. We stand there telling ourselves, "maybe it's my lack of discipline."

Ask yourself with that packet in your hand, answering only to yourself:

Did you design that colour to hit a child's eye at three feet?

Did you calculate the sweet-salt-fat ratio that makes one dose call for the next before the sweetness fades?

Did you pay for the shelf at eye level, the smell fan, the ping that lands at 9:47 pm?

You did none of it. The aisle did it for you.

The fact is the Supermarket Aisle was built to fire wanting before you bite. That's its job. In animals the loop runs on the body's own opioid system and on cue circuits — a wrapper, a smell, a sound can fire the wanting even after stopping. In us that is an analogue, not a proven human withdrawal — we do not medicalise your craving — but it is why the ping hits like a tug before you have touched anything. The wrapper does half the work of the dose. The Confectioner does the rest, translating the tug: *you need this, you deserve this, you can handle this one.*

We spent years blaming Maya for hearing that translation. We told her she was wilful where she was willing, indulgent where she was engineered. We were harsh to the person and soft on the trap. It's the other way around.

Warm to you. Vicious to the trap that built this aisle around you.

If this feels frightening to admit — that the craving was manufactured — good, feel that flash fully. And don't change from fear. You are not switching prisons to escape fear. You are seeing that this was never about your character at all, so freedom is relief, not penance. The fear dies when the lie does.

You were not failing in that aisle. The aisle was succeeding on you.

### THE LAB WITH THE DIALS

Behind the aisle is a quieter room. White coats, not white lights. They call it product development. I call it the Bliss-Point Lab.

Picture it plainly. Long tables. Little pots. A dial for sweet, a dial for salt, a dial for fat, a dial for crunch. They turn them until volunteers say one sentence above all others: *I can't stop at one.* That's not an accident. That's the product brief. Not taste. Not food. Hitability. The exact point where one dose guarantees the next — where "one bite becomes the box" stops being your confession and becomes their design spec.

This is not a conspiracy film. It is a food business doing what a food business measures. They are not hiding that they engineer bliss. They publish it. They patent it. The industry that profits from the dose is the same industry that funded the dials. Be angry at the engineer, not at yourself for being engineered.

And inside that engineered hit they hid a second trick we have already lived.

The trap does not make every day a feast and hope you binge. It makes an on-off schedule where you do. In careful animal comparisons — not human law, a control-group pattern — bingeing grew when sugar came and went intermittently, while animals with boring, free access did not binge. That control matters. It says schedule is the trap's form. Restriction itself manufactures the binge. Your "in moderation" week is that schedule. Your "I don't keep it in the house" Monday followed by the family-size Thursday is that schedule. You did not need a stronger will. The yoyo was building the urgency you then called your weakness.

The same crude hit blunts while it beckons. This seems to be how the crowding works — and I give you this as consistent with imaging, not proven by it — the reward circuit that lights for a hit can quiet with repetition. That would explain why you need the family size to feel what the small pack once did, why you say "not enjoying like before." At the same moment, and this is the cruelty, the cue system gets louder. The wrapper, the smell, the aisle lights fire *wanting* before any liking arrives. So you want more while enjoying less. That is not brain damage and not a life sentence — this is an interpretation, not proof of injury — but it matches exactly what you described: wrappers that shout, tins that call from the kitchen, a craving that arrives before hunger.

You are not dulled because you are weak. You are dulled because the dose hits a circuit small enough to be crowded and a cue system clever enough to be trained. Both reverse when you stop feeding the story.

You are not the engineer. You are not the dial. You are the person who walked into a trap that was built to look like a treat table.

One verdict covers both rooms — aisle and lab:

You were not choosing badly. You were being chosen for.
You were not lacking discipline. You were selected by design.

### LONELY CARBS INVITE COMPANY

There is a third dial the lab learned to turn, and it explains why BAD SUGAR is never just sugar.

Look at the lonely foods. White bread. Crisps. Plain crackers. The pastries that taste of sweet air. On their own they are dry, starchy, tired, wired and dulled after twenty minutes. They are lonely. And lonely carbs invite company.

Carbs are inherently lonely, they invite company. Mix in fat and sugar or syrups or chocolate and the lonely mouthfeel suddenly has a partner. The combination seems to hit harder than either alone — "seems to" because the fat-plus-sugar supra-effect is an interpretation, not a settled measurement — but you feel it plainly: the biscuit with chocolate hits different from sugar water, the doughnut hits different from bread. The Trap knows this. That is why the House-Party Gatecrasher never comes alone. Sugar brings the gatecrasher, starch holds the door, and fat turns the music up.

This is why "I'll just have the soft stuff" or "I'll cut sugar but keep the starchy snacks" never holds inside BAD SUGAR. The Confectioner tells you the savoury packet is not the problem. The packet knows it is. The starch behaves like sugar once it is engineered to. That is why we drew the line where we did on day one — BAD SUGAR means the whole engineered family — and why the line is not pedantry. It is self-defence.

Maya stands with that packet in the bright aisle. She reads *lightly salted* and thinks *lighter trap.* The Nibbler stirs at the word *lightly* because the Nibbler does not read ingredients — it reads cues. The Confectioner adds the footnote: *this one is fine, it's not even sweet.* Both are lying. The lonely carb invited company and you are holding the invitation.

You know this shape because you have lived it. Did the plain crackers ever end at plain crackers? Or did they end with something sweet twenty minutes later to answer the dip the first hit made? That was not appetite. That was the house-party. You invited one quiet guest and the whole party arrived.

The fact is your body, your instinct and real food do not need a gatecrasher. Real food finishes as it should. It satisfies and leaves you alone. The engineered packet finishes by asking for the next packet. When you see that, you stop arguing with yourself about which packet is "allowed." You see the loneliness was built in so you would feed it again.

### THE WARNING LIGHT YOU CALLED A FLAW

Here is the part that restores you.

For years you felt a mild unease in that aisle, in that queue for the next hit, in that evening where the tin emptied while you watched yourself. You called it lack of discipline. You called it being greedy. You told yourself others could take it or leave it while you could not.

We ran the numbers on that story. On validated self-report scales — not a medical diagnosis, and we do not label you — about 14 in every 100 adults and about 12 in every 100 children score in the addiction-like eating range, and those numbers sit alongside the self-report numbers for alcohol and tobacco. This is the only prevalence claim in this book, and it lives here for one reason: prevalence is not proof of disease, but it proves you are not uniquely weak, not uniquely shaped, not uniquely lacking. If your unease felt outsized, it was accurate perception in a trap that is common.

You are not defective for feeling the tug-of-war. The tug-of-war is the trap's signal. Every rope belongs to the Sugar Trap.

I want you to feel the weight shift.

It takes immense will to stay in something that goes against all your instincts, day after day, managing, counting, hiding packets, promising Mondays. It takes will to queue for the roller coaster you want to get off. The Confectioner told you that will was not enough. I tell you the will was never the problem — the Confectioner was feeding the Nibbler while you blamed your own hand for shaking.

So we name both now and never again confuse them.

The Nibbler is trivial. A small physical gnaw. That mild, empty, slightly insecure, slightly gnawing feeling that is uncomfortable but not mighty. Starvable. Already dying when not fed.

The Confectioner is the big story. The belief that each dose gives a genuine pleasure, treat or lift, that Friday deserves it, that sweetness lives in the packet. That story is what orders food for the Nibbler. Starve the story and the gnaw has nothing to stand on.

Ask yourself one last set, and answer only to yourself:

If desire was manufactured, why would you ever use willpower to fight a manufacture you can simply see through?

If lonely carbs invite company by design, why would you negotiate which gatecrasher to keep?

If your unease was accurate all along — a warning light, not a flaw — what happens if you honour it instead of scolding it?

You see it. The con deepens exactly as Carr's confidence trick always deepened: first you are conned on phoney information, then you are kept by a story that you *need* the con to feel normal. You were not foolish for buying. Anyone would have bought when every aisle, app, and advertisement told you this was food, this was love, this was reward. You are not foolish for staying either — the schedule and the bliss point kept you queueing. You leave because you see the con, not because you beat yourself.

That is the belief moved in this chapter, and it moves once and decisively:

You did not fail the Sugar Trap. The Sugar Trap was built to make you feel you had failed.

We widened the indictment where it belongs — to the lab with the dials, to the aisle that knew your name, to the lonely foods that invite company — so the shame leaves your shoulders. We did not widen it to frighten you. We widened it to free you. The health details come next, at full force and then relief. Do not carry them early. Carry this: your taste was true, your instinct was true, and the engine that muted both was crude and countable — a few dials, a few wrappers, a few pings.

Hold that seeing. You will need it in the next aisle.
