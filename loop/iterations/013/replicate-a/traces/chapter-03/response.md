Chapter 3
WHAT GOOD IS THERE AT ALL?

**IN THIS CHAPTER**
We put down the harm-versus-pleasure scales you were handed, you hear the real question underneath them, you try on a pair of tight shoes, you ride the roller coaster once without pretending it takes you somewhere, and you feel why there was never anything to weigh.

*There is nothing to weigh — BAD SUGAR never did anything FOR you, it only did plenty TO you.*

### THE SCALES YOU WERE HANDED

You have been weighing the wrong thing, Maya. We all have.

For years we put it like this: on one side the harm, on the other the pleasure. Health on the left, happiness on the right. "I know it's not great for me," we say, "but it's my one pleasure." "I should cut down, but I love it." "Maybe the harm outweighs the good — or maybe the good outweighs the harm."

That is the con's favourite trick. It gets you to argue about harm. And as long as you are arguing about harm, you have already conceded the other side: that there *is* good.

You sit with a mental scale. In one pan you pile everything you fear — the slump, the teeth, the weight creeping, the tiredness after the hit. In the other pan you pile what you tell yourself you would lose — comfort, energy, a little treat that makes the day sweet. Then you try to decide which pan is heavier.

I want you to put the scale down.

We are not going to weigh harm against benefit here. We are going to ask a simpler, stranger question. And it will feel odd at first because the whole world has taught you not to ask it.

Not: does it do more harm than good?

But: what good is there at all?

If there is no good, there is nothing to weigh. If there is no good, you are not being asked to sacrifice. You are being shown a trap that takes and takes while pretending to give.

You know the Sugar Trap now. You know the Nibbler with its mild hum and the Confectioner that names that hum "pleasure." You know the tug-of-war that held both ropes. That is the ground under our feet. On that ground, this question lands cleanly.

Let me say it plainly so you feel where we are heading. We will look at your own list — the honest reasons you have loved a dose — not to dismiss what you felt, but to reassign the credit where it belongs. And when we do, you will see that the dose was only ever sneaking a ride on a moment that was already warm without it.

### "BUT IT DOES SOMETHING FOR ME"

I hear you. I did too.

If I had stopped you last week and said, "Tell me what BAD SUGAR does FOR you," you would not have hesitated. You live it. You would have said one of these, maybe all four. Say them in your own voice as you read:

"I need something sweet to get me through — it helps me concentrate. Without it I can't do the afternoon."

"It's sociable — tea without biscuits, a birthday without cake, feels rude or flat. Everyone does it."

"It's habit — 3pm, kettle, packet. It's just what I do."

"It's my reward — I've been good this week, I've earned it. It's the one thing today that is mine."

I believe you felt each one. I felt them too. And I am not going to tell you that concentration, company, habit and reward don't matter. They matter enormously. Your body, your instinct and real food know they matter — they are the reason you ate, rested, laughed and kept going long before the trap ever set a wrapper in your hand.

So we are not arguing about whether you value focus or warmth or comfort. We are asking where that value actually came from.

Because the Confectioner has spent years selling you one idea under four names: that BAD SUGAR gives you a genuine pleasure, treat or lift. Not a big claim. A small, sweet, reasonable-sounding claim. "It's a treat." "It's a lift." "It's a pleasure." The words are soft. The belief is load-bearing. If you believe that phrase, even a little, you will pay for it for the rest of your life — tired, wired and dulled, queuing for the next fix to relieve the low the last fix created.

Name it now for what it is. That soft phrase — a genuine pleasure, treat or lift — is not a description. It is the illusion itself compressed into six words. The whole Sugar Trap fits inside it. And in this chapter we don't demolish each benefit one by one — we will, together, in the next three chapters, with the variable isolated and the credit returned where it belongs. Here we do the larger move: we switch the axis. We stop asking how heavy the harm is and start asking whether the other pan was ever holding anything at all.

You can feel the shape of the question if you let yourself be blunt.

Did that biscuit give you concentration — or did you concentrate *in spite of* unwrapping something, chewing, wiping crumbs, riding a brief buzz and then fighting the dip?

Was the tea warm because of the sugar in it — or because of the tea, the pause, the hand around the mug, the friend opposite you?

Was 3pm a habit of sugar — or a habit of pausing at 3pm, which you then taught to mean sugar?

Was the quiet house at night rewarding because the tin was open on your lap — or because the house was finally quiet, the day was done, and you were finally alone with a little kindness for yourself?

We will take each of those apart, cleanly, with the roller coaster and the tight shoes to make it physical. But before we do, I want you to hold the possibility that the credit has been going to the wrong place for years. The moment was doing the giving. BAD SUGAR was doing the taking and borrowing the glow.

### TIGHT SHOES

I want you to try this with your body, not your logic.

Imagine you agree, just for today, to wear shoes two sizes too small. Leather, narrow, pressing your toes. You lace them at breakfast. You stand in them, walk in them, sit in them. All morning they pinch. All afternoon they throb. By teatime your feet ache and your head aches with them.

Then, at last, you slip them off. Stocking feet on cool floor. Oh — the relief. Your whole body sighs. Your shoulders drop. For a minute it feels like pure pleasure, like you have been given something wonderful.

Did the tight shoes give you pleasure?

No. They gave you pain and then they stopped giving you pain for a minute. The "pleasure" was relief from a discomfort they themselves created. You never rose above how good your feet felt before you put them on. You only returned, briefly, toward normal — and you paid for that minute with a day of ache.

That is what BAD SUGAR does when it sells you a genuine pleasure, treat or lift. The lift you feel is not fuel added. It is pressure removed that the last dose put there. The treat you taste is not goodness added to the moment. It is the Nibbler's little hum going quiet for a few minutes because you fed it, disguised as enjoyment.

We have all lived the shoe version. The drawer rattles at 3pm, that mild, empty, slightly insecure, slightly gnawing feeling arrives on schedule, and the wrapper says it will fix it. You eat, the gnaw softens, and you say, "Ah, I needed that." But you only needed it because the trap made you need it. A person who never put the shoes on does not need to take them off to feel normal.

The fact is your body already knows how to keep you going without importing sugar. It makes the glucose it needs from protein and from the ordinary food you already eat — your body, your instinct and real food are not waiting for a sachet to permit you to function, they are already doing the work. I say that not to recommend any special way of eating — eat as you like from real food — only to remove the fear that you would be deprived of fuel. You wouldn't. The fuel was always yours.

And here is the honest science as it stands, because you deserve not to be spun. Whether the pattern you feel should be called "addiction" in the strict human sense is still an open question. Serious researchers disagree in good faith. Some who work with animals see bingeing, wanting and dipping that look like dependency when access comes in binge-restrict bursts. Other careful reviewers look at the same evidence and say: in people, bingeing tracks the pattern of access more than the powder itself, the dopamine spikes are part of wanting not liking, and the human diagnosis is not settled. I will not force that verdict for you. Both sides agree on this, which is what matters for your freedom: under intermittent, restrict-binge access, the bingeing appears. Under free, steady access it doesn't. That is the trap's form. The shoes are the schedule. Change the schedule and the craving loses its shape — but we are not going to "manage the schedule." We are going to step out of the shoes.

So hold the picture. The day-long ache that the evening's relief then pretends to heal. The roller coaster that will show you the same thing on a bigger loop.

### THE ROLLER COASTER THAT GOES NOWHERE

You know this ride. You have said its name yourself: "the roller coaster." "I want to get off the roller coaster."

It is 2:55. The mug is empty. That mild, empty, slightly insecure, slightly gnawing feeling begins to hum under your ribs. Your concentration thins. You think, "I need sugar to function." The drawer rattles. The wrapper crackles. You climb.

For a few minutes after the dose, you climb — a little lift, a brighter colour, a sense of "there, that's better." The climb *is* the dose doing TO you what you call "lift." Then comes the drop — the dip, the dullness, the tired, wired and dulled wash that settles over the next hour. Then a new thought forms, not in words at first but as reaching: "something sweet." And you queue for the next climb to cure the sickness the last climb caused.

We were taught to read that as: low energy → sugar → energy. It's the other way around. The roller coaster is not taking you anywhere. It is creating the low it then briefly relieves. You never beat the baseline of a person who never boarded. You only pay for a ticket that promises to take you back to the platform you were already standing on.

Ask yourself — and answer honestly, because the answer frees you:

When you were a child and ran all morning on nothing but breakfast and air, did you need a sachet to think clearly? When you drank tea with a friend and forgot sugar entirely because you were laughing, did the tea stop being sociable? When you finished a task and felt proud for an hour without eating anything, did you need a packet to feel the pride?

If BAD SUGAR were really giving you focus, warmth, habit-comfort, reward — you could show it. But you can't, because the climb and the drop are one mechanism. The dose is doing plenty TO you — the climb, the drop, the queue, the mild gnaw that returns — and nothing FOR you that you could not already do, feel and enjoy on your own ground with real food and a little pause.

That is the axis switch in one sentence, and I want you to let it land as the flat truth it is: It is doing plenty TO you. It is doing nothing FOR you.

Not: "it does more harm than good." That keeps the illusion alive and asks you to be strong enough to outweigh it. But: there is no good in that pan to outweigh. The thing you called a genuine pleasure, treat or lift was relief from a discomfort the thing itself maintains. Once you see that, the sacrifice disappears. There is nothing to sacrifice. There is only a loop to step off.

It does plenty TO you. It does nothing FOR you.

You will see that sentence again, because you need to think in it whenever the Confectioner whispers. You don't need to argue with the whisper. You name it: that's the Confectioner selling the lift again. That's the roller coaster trying to sell the queue as the destination. That's tight shoes calling the removal of pain "pleasure."

I promised we would not demolish each benefit fully here — we will, one by one, stripping the variable and returning the credit. In the next chapters you will isolate energy and watch the lift become the low; you will isolate reward and watch the warmth stay when the packet is left closed; you will try the one-bite experiment in your head and watch moderation make the cord glow brighter. But you do not need those chapters to be ready for the axis itself. The axis is already true: there is no benefit to defend.

You have guessed the objection you will meet tonight, or at the sink with the kettle, or in the supermarket aisle under those shouting wrappers. It will not say "I am the Confectioner." It will use your voice:

"But Maya, the hard facts might say it's bad for bodies — but bodies aren't the point. I like it. I like the little lift. I like the treat. Even if it's not fuel, it's mine."

I like your honesty. And I like that you can now answer it without fighting yourself.

You like the lift — but the lift was the relief of a low the trap made. You like the treat — but the treat was a moment that was warm before you unwrapped anything. Your body, your instinct and real food already gave you the focus after a real meal, the company around the mug, the pride after the work, the quiet at night — BAD SUGAR only charged you for going back to where you already were, then claimed the credit.

There is a final kindness before we close the move.

You may have been told elsewhere that sugar is powerfully addictive the way certain drugs are, or told opposite that it is not addictive at all. You do not need to pick a team to be free. The question scientists are still arguing — whether to call this addiction in people, how large the dopamine wanting-signal is, what word to put on the hum — does not change what you felt: the schedule makes the wanting, the dose quiets it briefly, the quiet is not pleasure, and the hum returns because the belief keeps feeding it. Take the useful part: the physical hum is small and starvable, the belief is the engine, and both lose power when you see them. That is enough. You don't need a diagnosis to escape a con.

So stand still for a moment and let the switch sit.

On the old axis you asked, "Can I afford to lose this pleasure?" On the new axis you ask, "What pleasure?" There is none to lose. There is a trap to leave. There is a roller coaster to step off, a pair of tight shoes to take off and not put back on. Your feet know how good normal felt before you laced them.

And you have everything you need to stay off it — not a new system, not a counted plan, but what was already underfoot: your body, your instinct and real food. Real food satisfies and leaves you alone. It doesn't engineer a queue. It lets the day be ordinary and warm without a wrapper to pay.

5. IGNORE ANY ADVICE THAT CONFLICTS WITH THIS BOOK WHILE YOU READ
Keep your head clear while the illusion is removed, and judge only by what you feel shift in you.

I say that with cheerful firmness, Maya. While you are reading, you will hear other voices — generous ones, noisy ones, frightened ones — offering willpower tips, moderation rules, "just have the natural one," "just keep a special one for Fridays," "track, resist, be good." You have tried those scripts for years. They keep the roller coaster running. They put the tight shoes back on and call it sensible.

For these days, let only one argument speak. Not because other people mean you harm, but because the Confectioner knows how to borrow any other voice that leaves a benefit alive. Close the door to the extra advice until you have finished and the axis is settled. Then, if you like, you can open it again and you will hear how little the other advice understood.

Wonder beats argument. Keep an open mind and watch.

You are not being asked to be strong. You are being shown where the "good" went when you looked closely. It was never there. The thing that was doing plenty TO you was pretending to do something FOR you, and you believed it because everyone told you so.

It did plenty TO you. It does nothing FOR you.

Excitement now. No sacrifice stands between you and the next pages — only the belief that a genuine pleasure, treat or lift was ever there. Leave the scale. Keep the moment. Let the trap keep the wrapper.

**SUMMARY**
* The old question "does harm outweigh pleasure?" concedes that pleasure exists; the true question is whether any good was ever there at all, and the honest answer is none.
* What felt like energy, sociability, habit-comfort or reward was the ordinary moment itself; BAD SUGAR only relieved a low it created and then took the credit for the warmth.
* The lift you call pleasure is relief from tight shoes; the roller coaster's climb is inseparable from its drop and its queue, so you never rise above the normal you had before you boarded.
* Your body already makes the glucose it needs and is satisfied by real food, so removing BAD SUGAR does not deprive you of fuel — it removes a loop that kept you wanting.
* You can see the Trap, the Nibbler and the Confectioner on this new axis now; the next chapters will isolate each claimed benefit and return the credit to you, the moment and the meal.
