Chapter 4
THE ENERGY LIE

**IN THIS CHAPTER**
You will sit at Maya's desk at 3pm when the drawer rattles, you will ride the roller coaster you thought was taking you somewhere, you will try on tight shoes to feel the trick with your feet, and you will see why the lift never made you stronger — it only stopped the drain for a minute.

*That lift you called energy was not energy at all — it was the ache pausing because you fed it.*

### THE 3PM DRAWER

It is 3:02, Maya.

The meeting is still going. Someone has put tea and biscuits in the middle of the table. You are nodding and taking notes and you are not really taking notes. That mild, empty, slightly insecure, slightly gnawing feeling has started to hum again under your ribs. Your eyes pull to the packet. Your hand knows the weight of the wrapper before you touch it.

You think, in your own voice, "I need sugar to function."

"I need something sweet to get me through."

"I-need-to-eat-something-RIGHT-NOW feeling."

"I need sugary somethings to get me through."

If you are honest you have said all four this month. We have all said them. We say them because they feel true in the body. The concentration thins, the patience thins, the afternoon stretches, and the dose promises, "I will lift you."

You open the drawer. The wrapper crackles. You eat. For three minutes the colour comes back. You think, "There. Now I can think."

We need to look at those three minutes with clear eyes, because this is where the Confectioner does its cleverest work. It sells you a genuine pleasure, treat or lift as if it were fuel, and you buy it because the relief arrives so fast it feels like proof.

But we have already changed the axis. You know now to ask not whether the harm outweighs the good but whether there was any good at all. So ask it here, where it hurts most.

Did that dose give you energy?

I was where you are. I believed it did. I ran on tea and biscuits from breakfast to that drawer, and I called the brief brightening after a biscuit "functioning." I believed I was tired and then I was less tired because sugar had *given* me something.

The fact is it gives you nothing. That is the whole lie in one line. And I will show you how you already know.

### THE ROLLER COASTER YOU THOUGHT WAS FUEL

You call it "the roller coaster" yourself. "I want to get off the roller coaster." That phrase is perfect and you named the trap without knowing it.

Here is the ride you have been riding for years.

You board at the dip. Not a crash you caused — a dip the last dose caused. A little flatness after the morning hit wears off, a little edge after lunch settles. That mild hum asks to be fed. You climb — the wrapper, the bite, the sip — and the climb is what you call a lift. Brighter, a little buzzed, a little steadier. Then the track tips. The buzz thins into that tired, wired and dulled wash you know too well. Eyes gritty, patience short, a new ache that says "again." And you queue for the next climb to cure the drop the last climb made.

We were taught to read it backwards: low energy → sugar → energy. Low → dose → lift. So we tell ourselves sugar is fuel. It is not.

It's the other way around.

The dose does not cure the low. The dose *is* the low, on a timer. You never rise above where you stood before you boarded. You only pay to be returned briefly toward normal, then you pay again with the next drop.

Let me prove it with your own life, not my assertion.

Answer honestly, and answer for yourself:

When you were a child and ran all morning on breakfast and air, did you need a sachet to concentrate on the game in front of you?

When you sat with a friend over tea and forgot the biscuits entirely because you were laughing, did the tea stop being sociable the moment the packet stayed closed?

When you finished a piece of work you were proud of and felt clear for an hour without eating anything, did you need a bite to feel the pride?

You know the honest answers. The concentration was yours. The sociability was the friend, the steam, the pause, the laugh. The pride was the finished work. BAD SUGAR only ever sneaked a ride and claimed the credit when you fed the hum it kept alive.

And there is a strange detail that gives the game away. The wanting fires *before* you eat. The wrapper crackle, the smell from the kitchen, the tongs of colour in the aisle — they make your mouth water before a crumb touches your tongue. If the lift were fuel going in, why would the craving arrive at the *sight*? Because the trap runs on wanting, not on liking. The circuit lights up at the cue and promises relief, then quiets for a minute after you feed it, then lights again.

Which is why the same dose stops delighting you. You remember when one biscuit was lovely and now you say "I don't enjoy it like before" and need the family size to feel anything. That is not your failure. When the circuit is crowded by repeated hits it blunts — you feel less — while the cue-fire before the bite grows brighter. Less liking, more wanting. That picture fits what many people feel, and brain pictures in some groups have been read to match it, but I will be honest with you: that reading is still interpretation, not proof, and it comes mostly from studies in obesity and not from you. I give it to you only as a way to see why "one bite becomes the box" can happen and why the wrapper can shout before the taste does — not as a sentence about your brain.

Look again at the afternoon. You said, "I need sugar to function." But function was what you *lost* on the drop. A person who never boarded the roller coaster does not wander through the afternoon needing a lift to function. They function, and when they dip they eat real food and the dip passes without a queue.

You have guessed the objection that will come tonight, in your voice again:

"But I felt the lift. I literally felt better. Are you saying I imagined it?"

I am saying you felt relief and you were taught to call relief "energy." You did feel better for those minutes — because the ache stopped for a minute. A man who wears tight shoes all day feels better when he takes them off. He feels wonderful. Did the shoes give him pleasure? No. They gave him pain and then they paused it. You felt the pause, Maya, not a gain.

### TIGHT SHOES AT YOUR DESK

Try it with your body, not your logic, because your body already knows.

Lace shoes two sizes too small at breakfast. Stand, walk, sit, work. All morning they pinch. By two o'clock your feet throb, your head throbs with them. Your concentration thins because pain thins concentration. Then — at 3pm — you slip them off. Cool floor under stocking feet. Blood returns. Your shoulders drop. For a minute it feels like marvellous energy, like someone switched a light on.

Did the tight shoe give you energy?

Did the removal of pain count as fuel?

It felt like it did. That is the whole trap in miniature. BAD SUGAR is the shoe you put on with the last dose so that the next dose can sell you the relief of taking it off.

In the office that looks like this: you sit at a desk, real work to do, your body wanting movement, water, a pause, maybe a proper bite of real food eaten without hurry. The Confectioner whispers, "a genuine pleasure, treat or lift — just a treat, just to get through." You feed the Nibbler's hum. The hum quiets. You call the quiet "concentration returned" and get back to the email. But your concentration returned because the interference stopped, not because sugar added anything to your brain. Real food would have left you satisfied and alone. The dose left you queued again an hour later.

Ask yourself again, with this picture on your feet:

If sugar were true fuel, would you feel worse an hour later than before you took it? Does petrol make the car emptier after you fill it? Does water make you thirstier?

If sugar were a treat, would you need to hide the empty packet? Do we hide the evidence of real pleasure?

If sugar helped you concentrate, why does the meeting where you eat the biscuits leave you more tired, wired and dulled than the walk where you ate nothing at all but breathed and talked?

There is only one honest answer and it is not cruel — it is freeing. The lift was the dip, paused. You never beat baseline. You only paid to get back toward it for a minute.

I want to give you the honest science here because you deserve not to be spun, and honesty is what makes belief stay.

The fact is some people do get a real, measured after-dip after a sugary hit — blood sugar dropping sharply as insulin does its job. That shape exists. But for most healthy people without diabetes, the everyday slump you feel at 3pm often happens *without* any low number on a meter; the symptoms arrive even when the glucose reading is still in the normal range. In other words, your feeling is real, but it is not proof you are clinically low and it is not proof you need sugar to correct it. It is proof the roller coaster is doing TO you what you read as need.

And that chemical dip that has been measured after repeated sugar binges — the low dopamine with a rise in the opposing transmitter — what was seen was in rats, after intermittent binge access, and when the dip was precipitated with a drug that blocks the effect. It was small. The laboratory itself calls that kind of dependency "mild but well-defined." Not mighty. Mild. Small and starvable. I tell you that to shrink the fear, not to grow it. The hum you feel is real, but it is not a giant you must battle with willpower. It is a thin, insecure gnaw that dies when you stop feeding the belief that keeps ordering it.

You don't need me to tell you sugar is strong the way some other drugs are — and I will not. And you don't need me to diagnose you to leave a con. You only need to see the direction of the trick: the rescuer is the perpetrator in a different hat. The lift *is* the low, delayed.

Feel how that lands. If the lift is the low, there is nothing to give up to be free of it. The sacrifice you feared does not exist. You are not being asked to function without fuel. You are being shown you were functioning *in spite of* a loop that kept stealing your function and selling it back.

### WHAT REMAINS WHEN YOU LEAVE THE RIDE

You will still eat at work. You will still have 3pm.

Only the meaning changes, and meaning changes everything.

Tomorrow at 3pm the hum may still arrive on schedule — that mild empty note — because the body is used to the timetable. You do not need to fight it, you do not need to resist it, you do not need to out-discipline it. You see it. You name it: "that's the Nibbler complaining because the Confectioner told it to expect a dose." You drink water, you eat real food if you are hungry — soup, bread, fruit, whatever honest food is to hand — and you let the hum pass. Real food satisfies and leaves you alone. It does not engineer a queue. It lets the afternoon be ordinary and warm without a wrapper to pay.

You do not need to change your life to prove this. Change nothing else. Eat as much real food as you like, whenever you like, and never count, crave or punish yourself again — your body, your instinct and real food already know how to keep you steady without importing BAD SUGAR, and they were doing it long before the trap taught you to call tight shoes "support." I say that not as a diet plan — I prescribe no plan at all — only as the simple physiology that you are not depriving yourself of fuel; your body makes the glucose it needs from what you already eat.

All you have to do is follow all the instructions, and the chief instruction here is to see the direction clearly, because once you see it you cannot unsee it.

You felt you needed sugar to function. It's the other way around: you functioned better the minute sugar stopped interrupting you.

A word to keep you safe while you test this, not scared. If you have diabetes, take medicine that affects blood sugar, have an eating disorder, or are under medical advice about food, do not change medicine or a prescribed way of eating on the basis of this chapter. Speak to your clinician about any health steps. This book changes a belief, not a treatment. If food feels out of control or distressing, seek support promptly.

And if you feel a flicker of fear as you read — "what if I'm broken, what if I need it" — let me lift it in the same breath. The fear is the tug-of-war trying to hold both ropes, telling you that you need rescue from the very thing offering the rescue. Don't change because you are frightened; change because you are free the moment you see the con. The hard fact is real, the prison door is open, and the plains beyond it are not frightening — they are ordinary life without shoes that pinch.

So leave the roller coaster. Step out of the shoes and leave them unlaced. The warmth of the tea, the laugh across the table, the pride after work — those were yours. BAD SUGAR only charged you to feel normal again.

It was doing plenty TO you. It did nothing FOR you.

**SUMMARY**
* The 3pm "lift" is not fuel added — it is the brief quiet when the low the last dose created is paused, so you never rise above your normal baseline.
* The roller coaster keeps you tired, wired and dulled because each climb guarantees its drop and the queue for the next climb; what you call "I need sugar to function" is the loop needing feeding, not your body needing sugar.
* The tight-shoe test proves relief is not benefit: removing self-inflicted pain feels like pleasure but leaves you no better than before you put the shoes on.
* Everyday slumps often happen without a clinical low reading, and the small measured dips seen in animal models are mild, precipitated, and tied to binge-restrict schedules — the hum is real but small and starvable, and the driver is the belief that a genuine pleasure, treat or lift was ever there.
