Chapter 5
THE TREAT THAT WAS NEVER A TREAT

**IN THIS CHAPTER**
You will sit with Maya after the bath, after the argument, after the small win that felt like it deserved chocolate — you will take the treat out of the moment and see what warmth is left, you will try on tight shoes for comfort instead of energy, you will walk the bright aisle where every wrapper shouts, and you will catch the quiet gatecrasher who invites sugar to every lonely bite.

*The comfort was never in the chocolate — it was you, it was the moment, and sugar only borrowed the credit.*

### AFTER THE BATH, AFTER THE FIGHT, AFTER THE WIN

You know this one, Maya.

The bath is done. The house is finally quiet. The kids are asleep, your hair is damp, you are soft inside your own skin for the first time all day. Or it came after the sharp words in the kitchen — the sting still in your chest, the front door closed too hard, you standing there alone. Or it came after you finally finished the thing — the inbox cleared, the form sent, the good news landed — that small proud lift in your ribs that says *I did it*.

And in each of those moments the same voice arrives, in your own words:

"I've earned it."

"I've been good this week."

"I deserve it."

"just a treat."

"celebrating, rewarding myself, going to the cinema, etc with chocolate."

We have all said it, and we said it with complete sincerity. Because it feels true. The chocolate actually does feel like love in that minute. Like kindness. Like someone — you — finally looking after you.

I want to be warm to you here and harsh to the trap that sold you that sentence, because there is nothing weak about wanting comfort. Wanting comfort is human. The con is what you were taught to call comfort.

If the last chapter took away the energy excuse — the lift that was only a pause in a low the last dose created — this one takes away the softer, dearer excuse. The one that whispers, *maybe not fuel, but it is my reward, my love, my soothe*. That one is harder to question because it feels kinder. Look at it with me anyway.

When you ate that square on the sofa, what actually soothed you?

Was it the sugar — or was it the bath, the quiet, the fact that no one needed you for ten minutes?

When you ate after the argument, what calmed you — the chocolate, or the minutes passing, your breath slowing, your body doing the ancient, competent work of settling itself while you stared at the lampshade?

When you ate after the win, what made you glow — the bar in your hand, or the fact that you had done a hard thing and knew it?

Answer for yourself. Not for me.

The fact is you were already giving yourself the thing you thought the dose was giving you. We were conditioned to read it backwards. We put the kind moment and the dose together so many times that the brain did what brains do — it smeared them together and credited the wrong one.

That story — that a mouthful of engineered sugar is a genuine love and a genuine comfort — that story was the Confectioner. Not you being silly. The Confectioner is the sweet story that feeds the little creature underneath. You believed it because it was sold to you a thousand times before you could choose, in kitchens and adverts and lunchboxes where love was offered with a biscuit on the side. No blame. You were taught.

And underneath, when the packet is empty and the lamps are still warm, that mild, empty, slightly insecure, slightly gnawing feeling may hum again. That hum is not heartbreak. It is not need. It is only the Nibbler, the trivial creature, complaining because the Confectioner told it a dose was coming at this exact kind of moment. It is small. It passes. It has no power except the story you lend it.

### DID IT ACTUALLY FEEL BETTER?

Let me take the treat out of the moment and show you what remains.

Keep everything else the same. Same bath. Same soft towel. Same quiet house, tea steaming, lamplight low. Same tired, relieved you. Only remove the dose. No tin on your lap. No wrapper.

Is the bath less warm?

Are the ten minutes of belonging to yourself less yours?

Does the quiet house not still hold you?

You know. The warmth stays. You were the warmth.

Take the other scene. Same argument, same sting, same slow evening. You do not eat. You stand at the sink, water hot on your hands, you breathe, you let the heat come. Ten minutes pass. The sting softens from a knife to a bruise to a memory, because that is what feelings do when you do not interrupt them.

Did you need sugar to get there? If the bar were the soother, how did the nights you had no bars in the house end the same way — a little raw, then calm, then sleep?

Or the win. Same finished task, same small pride. You tell someone, you smile to yourself, you make a coffee and look out of the window. No chocolate. Is the pride smaller because there is no wrapper?

You already know the honest answers. And there is a kinder truth under them.

Ask yourself this, and let yourself answer honestly:

If that square were truly a reward, would you need a second square to believe you had been rewarded?

If that dose were truly love, would you hide the empty packet so no one could see how much love you took?

If that treat were truly comfort, why did the evening you had it end with you feeling *less* comfortable with yourself than the evening you didn't?

That is the credit trick. The moment was doing the work; the dose was sneaking a ride. We learned to say *chocolate comforted me* when the fact was *I comforted me, in a warm room, after a long day, and the chocolate happened to be there*.

A little science to keep you honest, not frightened — and I give it to you without a lecture, because you deserve the truth without scare. There is a careful observation that in men, a higher proportion of calories coming from sweet foods predicts a later low mood, even after checking that low mood did not come first — observational, men only, not proof that sugar caused anything for you or for anyone, and I say that gently, not as a sentence on you, only to show why the "treat will lift me" can misread: what feels like a lift tonight can sit beside a lower tomorrow without you connecting the two. The dots are not yet joined for everyone, and not at all for women in that data, so I do not use it to frighten you — only to help you see that calling it a treat does not make it one.

And there is another piece. That sudden tug you feel before the first bite — at the aisle, at the advert, at the smell from the tin — that wanting that fires before sugar even touches your tongue. In a laboratory it has been watched in rats, where a wrapper-like cue can fire the wanting circuit even after the sugar stops. That is a rodent analogue, not proof about you, and I do not present it as your diagnosis. I use it only to explain why longing can feel so real before you eat: the cue, not the need, lit the fuse. Your body had not yet tasted anything. The brain had already been reminded to want.

So when you think *I want chocolate because I'm sad and it will soothe me*, it can be the cue speaking, not sadness needing sugar. Sadness needed time, warmth, breath. The cue just borrowed sadness for its script.

### TIGHT SHOES IN SLIPPERS

Put the tight shoes on again, but for comfort this time.

You have been in slippers all evening. Warm, loose, kind on your feet. Someone tells you that wearing tight shoes for ten minutes will make slippers feel even better, so you lace them. They pinch. They throb. After ten minutes you take them off and slip back into your slippers. Ahh. Relief floods your feet. You sigh. It feels like the slippers have become more loving.

Did the tight shoes give you comfort?

Did hurting and then stopping the hurt add any comfort that wasn't already in the slippers?

That is exactly what the "treat" does. You were already in slippers — a bath, a quiet house, a finished job, a body that knows how to settle. The trap laces a little pinch — that hum that says *something sweet would make this perfect* — so that taking it off by feeding it feels like a reward. The relief is real. The benefit is not. You are not more comforted than you were before the pinch began; you are merely back to where you were, minus a dose.

The fact is the treat borrowed the slippers and claimed they were its gift.

### THE AISLE WHERE EVERYTHING SHOUTS

Walk the supermarket with me for thirty seconds. Not to avoid it. To see it.

The lights are too bright. The wrappers shout. Smells that were engineered in a white room pull before you see. The tongs of engineered colours sit at child height. From the end cap a sign says *you deserve this* and somewhere a screen says *just a treat* in your own voice.

Do you see anyone being treated?

Look again with the story stripped off. Those people with baskets are not being rewarded. They are feeding a queue. The manufacturers designed a point where you cannot stop — we will meet that lab properly soon — and the aisle is its shop window. That tight feeling in your chest at the checkout is not excitement. It is cue-fire. It is the Nibbler hearing the bell.

Try the pity script now, not as meanness but as sight. When you see hands dropping family packs into a trolley, do not envy them. They are not getting love you are missing. They are buying laces to tighten their shoes so they can pay to take them off again for a minute. You pity the trap, not the person — we were all there, we know how it holds.

That re-seeing is freedom practice. It does not require discipline. It requires clear eyes.

### THE LONELY BITES THAT INVITE COMPANY

There is a last piece for this chapter, and it is not about willpower. It is about company.

White carbs are lonely. Bread, crisps, pastries, the pale soft things that behave exactly like sugar once you eat them — they invite company. That is why the biscuit calls for chocolate, the sandwich calls for crisps, the salty calls for sweet. It is not you being greedy. It is the plate being lonely. A real meal — soup, bread made of real grain, vegetables, meat or fish or eggs or pulses, fruit whole as it grew — satisfies and leaves you alone. It does not lay a table for the next hit. The engineered combination — fat plus sugar, starch plus sugar, salt plus sugar — was built to hit a point where you cannot stop, and it uses your hunger to pull the next dose in.

That is the House-Party Gatecrasher. You opened the door to a quiet bite — plain crackers after lunch, a handful of crisps — lonely on their own, and they said, *let us invite sugar over*. Before you knew it the party was not yours.

Does that sound like a treat? Or does it sound like someone else arranging your evening?

When you eat real food till you are satisfied — and I mean as much as you like, whenever you like — your body, your instinct and real food do not bargain. They do not count or crave or punish. They signal *enough* and they mean it. The lonely bite never arrives, so the gatecrasher has no door.

### WHO GETS THE CREDIT?

So let me return your moments to you.

That post-bath peace — that was you, clean and warm and finally alone. That after-argument calm — that was your nervous system doing its quiet repair while you let minutes pass. That after-win glow — that was pride, earned and felt in your own chest.

Sugar did not warm the water. Sugar did not quiet the house. Sugar did not finish your work.

You did.

I know the objection that rises, because it rose in me: *But I like the taste. Surely I like the taste.*

Do you like it the way you like an apple when you are hungry and the juice is sweet on your tongue because you are tasting something your body recognizes? Or do you like the *idea* that this is your permission to be kind to yourself, wrapped in sugar?

Because if it were taste, why does the third bite taste less kind than the first, and the fifth less kind than the second, and the empty box taste of nothing but tired, wired and dulled?

The taste is not the point. The permission was. And you do not need to purchase permission from a packet. You can keep the permission and leave the packet.

Think of the dearest scene the trap still guards — tea steaming, lamplight, tin open on your lap, kids finally asleep, the house finally yours. We will meet that scene again and strip it clean together. For now just hold this: the tenderness in that scene is the tenderness of your own life coming back to you after a day of giving it away. It is not in the tin. You brought it with you. The dose only sat on its edge and borrowed the light.

If every drop of warmth belonged to the moment, the person, the bath, the breath, the win — if sugar only sneaked a ride — then there is no comfort to lose by leaving it.

The treat was never a treat.

It was your moment, mislabelled.

Let the moment keep its name. Let sugar keep none.

**SUMMARY**
* The reward and comfort you felt was the moment, the bath, the quiet, the pride, the person — sugar only sneaked a ride and claimed the credit.
* Calling it "just a treat" or "I deserve it" does not make it a treat; the wrapper that makes you want it before you taste it is cue-fire, not proof you need it.
* Tight shoes in slippers proves relief is not benefit — you were already comfortable before the pinch that sugar taught you to cause.
* Lonely white carbs invite sugar to the party; real food eaten to appetite leaves you satisfied and alone, so your body, your instinct and real food need no imported sweet to feel steady.
