Chapter 4
THE ENERGY LIE

*The lift you feel after something sweet is not power arriving, it is the top edge of the dip you are about to pay for.*

IN THIS CHAPTER: the office at two in the afternoon, the plate of leftover cake by the kettle, the bright minutes and the fog an hour later, the little ride that only climbs so it can drop, and the honest look at what your afternoon is really costing you.

The Two O'Clock Desk

You know this desk because you have sat at it through a hundred tired afternoons, and I know it because I sat at one just like it for years alongside you.

It is just past two o'clock on an ordinary working day, and the morning has already taken what it wanted from you. You ate lunch or you half ate lunch while you answered messages, you returned to the screen with your shoulders a little heavy, you felt your attention thinning as the room grew warm and the air grew still, and you told yourself that you needed something to get through the afternoon. You believe that sentence describes a real shortage of fuel, and you are right to notice that the tiredness is real because the heaviness sits in your body and you cannot argue it away.

We have all read that heaviness as a fuel gauge, and we have all answered it in the same kind way. You stand up to stretch your legs, you walk toward the small kitchen or the break table at the end of the corridor, you see the plate someone left there after a birthday or a meeting, and you stop because the sight answers the tiredness before you have thought. The cake is mediocre and you know it is mediocre even as you reach, the sponge is a little dry at the edges, the icing is too thick and too yellow, the slices have been cut unevenly and left uncovered so the surface has toughened, and you would never have chosen this cake in a shop if you were hungry and free to choose. You eat it standing up with a paper napkin in your hand while the kettle boils, you take the first bite quickly and taste the bright sweetness jump forward, you take the second bite while you are still chewing the first, and you notice that your mind has already left the slice in your hand and moved on to what you will have next.

You think, I just had a few cookies because I like sweet things and I felt I needed a break, and the sentence sounds honest because you do like sweet things and you do need a break. You think, I need sugary somethings to get me through, and the phrase arrives in your own voice because you have spoken it so often that it feels like reporting weather. You think, I need a pick-me-up, and you believe the words point to fuel the way a petrol gauge points to empty. I thought those sentences myself, and I believed them while I spoke them because the tiredness behind them was genuine and the bright minutes after the first bites felt like proof.

There is the steady afternoon when work moves calmly and your hands do not reach for anything between meals.

Watch what actually happens at the break table while you still eat as normal, because your own eyes will teach you more than any argument from me. Notice how you are tired and bored a little hungry and pulled toward whatever sweet is left on the table, notice how you eat the mediocre slice without pleasure yet keep eating, notice how you are already thinking about a sweet drink or a biscuit or chocolate while you chew. If you truly liked the cake, you would slow down to taste it, and you know that you speed up. If the cake truly gave you what you needed, your mind would settle on the work ahead, and you know that your mind settles on the next sweet. If hunger were driving you, a sandwich or an apple or a handful of nuts would sound just as good, and you know from your own mouth that they do not sound good at all in that moment. What you call liking is wanting that has outlived tasting, and we have mistaken one for the other for years.

Would a real fuel leave you planning the next fill before you have swallowed the first. Would a genuine pleasure be eaten standing up without attention while you think about something else. Would true energy arrive as a rush that makes you restless and then leaves you reaching again. You know the honest answer because you have lived this hour a thousand times and you have felt the pattern without naming it. The slice did not power the afternoon, it interrupted the tiredness for minutes and then returned it with interest, and the interest is what brings you back to the table tomorrow.

I am not asking you to fight that hour by effort, and I am not asking you to sit at your desk through the tiredness while you miss something you love. You know how that hard way keeps the sweet precious in your mind while your hands shake around it, and I will never ask you to live that way. I am asking you to see the hour clearly for what it is, because once you see that the sweet never gave energy but only borrowed it, you will have no reason to miss it.

What The Lift Costs An Hour Later

Every sweet eater knows the bright minutes, and I want to honour them before we look at what follows because you are not imagining them.

You swallow something sweet on a tired afternoon, and for ten or fifteen minutes you feel brighter. Your mood lifts a little, your thoughts move a little faster, the heaviness loosens around your shoulders, and you tell yourself that fresh energy for your body has arrived just when you needed it. You believe the brightness proves the theory, and you are right to trust that you felt something because the lift is real as a sensation and I will never tell you that you invented it. We all felt that lift, and we all took it as proof that BAD SUGAR works as fuel while we ignored what came next.

Now follow the same afternoon one hour further while you still eat as normal, because the hour later tells the truth that the bright minutes hide. The brightness thins without any fresh decision from you, your concentration blurs at the edges, your eyes feel a little heavier than before the sweet, your mood dips a little lower than where you started, and a faint restlessness moves in your stomach and your hands. You reach again or you think about reaching again, not because you are greedy and not because you are weak, but because something in you wants the brightness back. You think, the sugar I need to see me through my daily slump, and you believe you are describing a second shortage when you are really describing the cost of the first fix.

You described this yourself more honestly than any expert could, and I want you to hear your own wisdom back. You said that something quick and sweet gives only a short term lift which is inevitably followed by a fast drop, and you knew from your own afternoons that the two belong together. The lift and the drop are not two separate events, they are one event seen from two ends, and we have been calling the first half fuel while we pretended the second half came from nowhere. The fact is that the sweet creates the low it then claims to cure, and once you see that order you can never unsee it.

There is the clear head at four o'clock that stays level without any bright minutes or fog.

Ask yourself what that sequence proves about fuel. Would real food need to be eaten again an hour later to repair the hour before. Would genuine energy leave you foggier than thirst and hunger leave you. Would a true lift be followed by headache and irritability and renewed reaching as a matter of routine. You know the honest answer because your body has kept the record even while your mind repeated the old story. A proper meal satisfies and releases you back to your day, while a sweet fix excites and then re-arms you for the next fix, and the difference between satisfying and re-arming is the whole secret of the afternoon.

Let me speak plainly about the dip because honesty here protects everything else I tell you. The fog and the flatness after sweets are real, and your body's own swing after the sweet explains them. The sweet rushes in fast, your body answers fast to handle it, and the answer overshoots a little so you land a little lower than where you started. This is not a medical low in the clinical sense, and I give you no medical label for it and no number to fear. It is your own regulatory swing doing its ordinary work after an extraordinary rush, not proof that you ran empty and not proof that you must refill with more sweet. The crash is real, but it is the cost of the lift, not a new hunger, and we have been refilling a dip with the very thing that dug it.

You think the swing proves you need sugar to stay level, and I understand why you read it that way because I read it that way myself for years. Look again at the order while the memory is fresh. You were a little tired before the sweet, you were bright for minutes after the sweet, you were foggier an hour later than you were before, and you wanted more sweet to lift the fog. The tiredness before was small and ordinary, the fog after was heavier and specific, and the sweet turned the first into the second while taking credit for the minutes between. That is not supply, that is borrowing at cruel interest, and you feel the truth of it in your own afternoons.

The Ride That Only Climbs To Drop

I want to give you one picture you can keep in your head at the desk, because a clear picture will do more work than ten arguments when the plate is in front of you.

Think of the small roller coaster at a local fair, the kind you ride with your hands up for a minute because someone you love is laughing beside you. The car climbs slowly with a loud chain pulling it upward, you feel the click and the anticipation in your chest, you reach the top and see the whole field for a second, and then you drop fast with your stomach lifting and your throat making a noise you did not plan. The climb is brief, the drop is longer and steeper than you remembered, the car rattles through the lower turns while you wait for the feeling to settle, and as soon as you step onto the platform a little shaky you hear the voice calling for the next ride.

We ride BAD SUGAR that same way, and we pay for the ticket with the dip. The climb is the bright minutes after the first bites, the view from the top is the brief sense that you can face the afternoon, the drop is the fog and the flatness an hour later, and the rattling lower turns are the headache and the restlessness and the renewed reaching that carry you to the evening. You believe you buy the climb when you reach, and you are right that the climb arrives because the car does go up. What you miss while you queue is that the climb exists only to make the drop possible, and no one would queue for a ride that was only the drop.

You say, I need it to get through the afternoon, and I hear how reasonable that sounds when you are standing in the queue with tired legs. Look at the ride from the platform while someone else rides, and you see what the rider cannot see. The rider climbs, the rider drops lower than the platform where he started, the rider queues again to fix the lowness the last ride created, and the fair keeps his money while he calls the motion energy. We have all stood in that queue, and we have all mistaken the climb for help while we forgot that level ground was free.

There is the quiet desk at three o'clock where attention stays whole without climbing or dropping.

Ask what the ride proves about help. Would you call a lift honest if it guaranteed a longer fall. Would you call a friend helpful if he lent you brightness in the morning and collected heaviness all afternoon. Would you keep buying tickets if you saw that the platform never rises and only the drops deepen. You know the honest answer because you have ridden enough times to feel the track in your sleep. The sweet does not raise your baseline, it borrows from it and returns less, and the only way to stay level is to step off the ride rather than to ride more smoothly.

I felt the truth of this picture on an ordinary Tuesday that I still remember with tenderness toward myself. I had eaten something sweet at half past two to face a dull report, I had felt the brief brightness and told myself the trick worked, I had sat in the fog at half past three with my jaw tight and my eyes hot and my mind reaching for a sweet drink to correct the morning correction. I believed I needed a second fix to finish the day, and then I saw the order with unusual clarity. The report was not harder than other reports, the afternoon was not darker than other afternoons, the only new thing in the hour was the sweet I had eaten to help. The helper had done the harm, and the harm was asking for more help, and I felt something loosen in my chest as the circle showed itself.

What you seek in every tired hour is a real treat or lift, and that phrase now names exactly what the ride pretends to sell. You want a small kindness that raises you without charging you, a little brightness that stays, a little help that asks nothing back. The ride sells you the words while it delivers the opposite, a brief climb that guarantees a dip, and once you see the whole track you stop asking how to ride safely and start asking why you queue at all. The answer is that you were taught to call the climb fuel, and teaching can be undone by looking.

Why A Drink On Top Never Fills

The afternoon has a second trap that is even easier to miss, and I want you to see it because it keeps the ride running when food should have stopped it.

On many tired afternoons you do not even eat the cake, you drink something sweet on top of the day. You fetch a large sweet fizzy drink from the cold cabinet, or you order a tall sweetened coffee with syrup and cream, or you pour a big glass of sweet juice that looks like fruit and drinks like dessert, and you take it back to the desk as an innocent companion to work. You believe a drink is lighter than a slice, and you are right that it feels lighter going down because there is nothing to chew and nothing to clear from the table. What feels light in the mouth lands heavily in the afternoon, and you know from your own days that the drink never closes the hunger it claims to touch.

The fact is that sweet drinks add energy on top and take the place of nothing. A proper bite of food quiets hunger for a while and releases you, while a sweet drink slips down quickly, lifts you briefly, swings you in the same way as the cake, and leaves you both full of calories and oddly ready to eat again. You know this from your own days because you have drunk something sweet at three and still wanted the biscuit at four. The drink did not substitute, it re-armed, and the re-arming is what keeps the desk drawer full.

There is the calm stomach in the late afternoon that asks for dinner at its proper time and nothing before.

Think of how differently your body answers real nourishment. You eat a plain satisfying lunch with enough in it, and hunger quiets for hours without argument. You feel the quiet stop signal that tells you enough has arrived, you return to work without thinking about food, you grow hungry again in a clean straightforward way when dinner approaches. You know that sequence from days when sweets did not interrupt it, and you trust it because it asks for nothing between meals. The sweet drink breaks that sequence without feeding it, it excites the mouth while it confuses the stomach, and then it leaves you to read the confusion as need for more.

You say, fresh energy for my body, and you point to the drink as proof that you are caring for yourself through a hard day. I honour the care behind the sentence because you do want to look after yourself and work well. Look at what the care actually bought while the memory is honest. Did the drink leave you more settled an hour later. Did it leave you less interested in the drawer and the machine. Did it leave you clearer at five than water and a proper bite would have left you. You know the honest answer because you have watched the hour after the drink as carefully as the hour after the cake. The care was real, but the drink was not care, it was the trap wearing the clothes of care, and we have all been fooled by the costume.

We have all told ourselves that liquid is different, and we have all paid the same price for the story. The can and the tall cup and the juice carton look modern and harmless beside the plate of icing, they carry no crumbs and no guilt in the moment, they can be drunk while you type so they feel like part of work rather than a break from it. That very convenience is what makes them perfect fuel for the loop, because they can be taken without decision and repeated without notice. One on top of the afternoon becomes two on top of the week, the week becomes the habit, and the habit teaches your tongue to call plain water dull by comparison while your body carries the load you never chose to lift.

Tired Flat And Craving

Let me name where the ride leaves you if you stay on it, because this is the state we have been calling normal and it deserves an honest look.

You wake a little coated after evenings of sweets, you face the morning with coffee and something quick, you climb and drop through the forenoon without noticing the track, you reach the two o'clock desk already tired, you top up and fog and top up again, you arrive home with your nerves a little thin and your patience a little short, and you pay the evening with the same coin that spent the afternoon. Day after day the pattern repeats without drama, no single day feels like disaster, and yet the months add up to a life lived a half step below where you could live. You believe this is just how afternoons feel for adults who work hard, and I understand why you believe that because everyone around the break table looks the same way.

The truth is plainer and kinder than that story. The tiny physical craving whispers between fixes, the brainwashing voice translates the whisper as need for fuel, we feed the loop and call the feeding choice, and the loop returns us to the same low platform with remarkable punctuality. The low is not character and it is not age and it is not the honest tiredness of a life well worked. It is the permanent hooked state showing itself between doses, the flat background hum we stopped hearing because we never step far enough away to hear silence.

There is the evening at home when your mood stays kind without any sweet to smooth it.

State the cost flatly with me for one minute, because flinching helps nothing and seeing helps everything. BAD SUGAR coats your mouth after the packet is empty, it leaves you thirsty in the night, it brings the flat hour after the bright minutes as surely as evening follows afternoon, it calls again at the same tired hour the next day, it teaches your tongue to call plain food dull by comparison, it turns one into several without any fresh decision from you, it taxes the teeth and the liver and the blood pressure and the appetite control that should be yours by right. You know each effect from your own weeks, and you do not need my word for any of them. Those effects are busy upon you every day, loud and ordinary and impossible to miss once you look.

Now hold beside that busy record the feeling you carry between fixes, the one you have learned to call personality. You move through the day a little tired even after sleep, a little flat even when the news is good, a little edgy toward people you love without knowing why, and always faintly pulled toward the next sweet as if the day cannot run without it. That is what life inside the loop really feels like day after day: tired, flat and craving. Read those four words slowly, because they carry the whole price of the ride in language your body already knows. Tired is the morning that never quite clears, flat is the afternoon that never quite lifts, craving is the whisper that never quite quiets, and the three together are not you but the trap living in you.

You think those words sound harsh, and I want to meet that feeling with tenderness rather than pressure. I speak harshly about the trap because the trap is harsh, and I speak warmly about you because you deserve warmth. You are not tired because you are lazy, you are not flat because you are dull, you are not craving because you are greedy. We were all conned into feeding a loop that manufactures exactly that state and then sells the next fix as the cure for it. What you feel an hour after the sweet is not hunger and not failure but a faint, empty, slightly edgy feeling that belongs to the loop, not to you. Once you hear the whisper as the loop's voice rather than your own need, you stop obeying it with the same automatic hand.

The First Days Are Small

You may feel uneasy as the ride shows itself, and I want to honour that unease before we finish because it holds the last doubt that keeps people queuing.

You think, if I stop feeding this every afternoon, the tiredness will grow huge and I will not cope at work. You think the whisper will become a shout that follows you from desk to kitchen to bed. You think the fog will settle permanently and you will have traded a small daily cost for a large unending one. I thought those thoughts myself, and I understand why they frighten because every fix taught you that the low is terrible and only the sweet removes it. The teaching was back to front, and the fear belongs to the teaching rather than to the future.

The fact is that the hump after you stop the daily fixes is small and short, not large and lifelong. For a few days you may notice headache or irritability or the whisper growing a little louder at the old hours, you may notice the afternoons feel strange without the climb and the drop, you may notice your hands reaching from habit while your mind knows better. You know from every change of habit that strangeness is not danger, and you are right to trust that knowing here. The strange days peak within two to five days and then they pass, the whisper thins as the tiny physical craving starves without its feed, the brainwashing voice loses volume as you stop obeying it, and level ground returns on its own without any replacement and without any hard method.

There is the fifth morning when you wake clear and wonder why the afternoons ever frightened you.

Do not change from fear of the cost list I spoke, and do not change from fear of the strange days ahead. I tell you the cost plainly because you deserve truth, not because I want you frightened into action. Fear never removed the want because fear never touched the belief that something precious would be lost. What removes the want is seeing that nothing precious was ever there, that the lift was the start of the dip, that the drink on top only re-armed you, that the tired flat state was rent you paid for brightness that never stayed. Once you see that, you do not step off the ride to be safe, you step off because the ride was never fun.

So watch the next afternoon with the eyes of an investigator rather than the eyes of a judge. Keep eating as normal while you read on, keep the drawer and the machine exactly as they are, keep every habit unchanged except for attention. Notice the bright minutes and time them, notice the fog an hour later and name its hour, notice the hand reaching while the mouth still chews, notice the drink that fills nothing yet calls for more. You will find that attention alone begins to loosen the knot, because you can no longer call fuel what you have watched become fog.
