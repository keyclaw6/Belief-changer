Chapter 11
"BUT I LOVE FOOD"

**IN THIS CHAPTER**
The family table and the phrase we all heard growing up — the sweet-tooth story told as heritage — the tangle of traits on the table that we turn the right way up — three myths asked out loud — a letter from the other side with numbers and taste buds

*Your sweet tooth didn't send you to BAD SUGAR — BAD SUGAR manufactured your sweet tooth and then sold it back to you as identity.*

### The Last Place To Hide

We have come a long way together, and I can feel where you are standing now.

You have watched the treat reveal itself as a thief. You have felt the lift show itself as the thing that steals. You have learned the names for the whine and the lie, you have seen who built the cage, you have felt the stakes in your own mouth and chest and mood, and you have taken the shame off your own shoulders and placed it where it belongs — on the method that told you strain was the answer.

And still, very quietly, a last thought hovers. Not loud. Almost tender. It does not argue. It explains.

"I've always loved food. I'm just a sweet person. It's in my family. It's who I am."

I hear it with warmth, because I said it for years. My mother said it about herself, my father said it about us. At our table the story was that we Lawsons *had* a sweet tooth, the way other families had blue eyes. It felt kind to say it. It felt true because it was familiar. It let me order the pudding without deciding — the decision was already made by my nature.

If you have ever said that sentence — "I love food," meaning *I love BAD SUGAR* — I am not going to scold you for it. I am going to turn it gently in the light and show you which way up it goes.

Because this is the last identity the trap has to offer. When every justification has been taken — treat, energy, celebration, company, "just a little" — the trap does not argue. It shrinks into you and says: *perhaps you are just built this way.*

We need to meet that kindly and dismantle it completely, so you leave this chapter with nothing left to miss and no one left to blame — least of all yourself.

### The Table Turned Upside Down

Let us sit at that family table for a minute.

Picture it. Sunday lunch. Bowls in the middle. Someone says, "We have such a sweet tooth in this family," and everyone laughs and nods, and the cake is passed as proof of who we are. The story and the sugar arrive together, so the sugar feels like heritage.

Now let me lay out what is actually on that table, the way you would lay out photographs to sort them.

You think you see a row of causes: anxious, restless, reward-seeking, emotional, hedonistic, lacking discipline around food — people born that way who therefore got caught. That is the tale the trap tells about you.

Look again. Put the same photographs the other way up and read the captions the trap wrote in invisible ink.

Anxious? Of course you feel edgy when the day is a yo-yo of spike and dip. Anyone fed a cycle of brief lift and fast drop will feel on edge.

Restless and needing something? Of course you do when that empty, edgy, slightly needy feeling is flickering every couple of hours and you have been taught to read it as hunger or personality.

Reward-seeking? Of course you reach for a treat when every packet says "you deserve this" and every celebration has been staged with BAD SUGAR centre frame.

Emotional around food? Of course food feels emotional when eating has been tangled with guilt, secrecy, hiding wrappers, and the negotiating voice that says "just this once."

Hedonistic? Of course pleasure feels louder around BAD SUGAR when the products have been engineered to hit harder than real food and then leave you less satisfied, so you need the family size a month after the small one stopped working.

Lacking discipline? We have already settled that. With the Willpower Method you were handed, persistence *was* discipline — and it kept the loop going.

The fact is… those traits are not the cause of the trap. They are the result of it.

We went into the trap as ordinary eaters and came out looking like "sugar people." You were not born into the Sugar Trap; the Sugar Trap was built around you and then taught you to describe its effects as your character.

Let me ask you as a friend, the way I would ask you over that same table after the bowls are cleared. Answer honestly, not cleverly:

Did you describe yourself as a sugar person *before* years of grazing, binging, and negotiating with BAD SUGAR — or only after?

If "sweet tooth" were truly your nature, why did it need armies of engineers, bright packets at child's-eye height, bliss-point formulas, and a lifetime of ads to keep it alive?

If your personality *needed* BAD SUGAR, why does that same personality feel steadier, clearer, and calmer a short while after BAD SUGAR leaves the stage?

There is only one honest answer. You are not a nature that needed the trap. You are a person on whom the trap left marks, and marks fade when the wound stops being reopened.

Watch how complete the inversion is. It was you that made the meal warm — the company, the laughter, the hunger that makes real food delicious — and BAD SUGAR merely gatecrashed. It was you that made the evening soothing after a hard day — the rest, the kindness to yourself — and BAD SUGAR stole the credit for the soothe while fermenting the next day's unease. It's the other way around.

You do not love BAD SUGAR because you love food. You love food, and BAD SUGAR has been blocking that love and selling it back to you, dose by dose.

And here is the graceful concession I promised, because the method never needs to win a debate about your soul to free you.

Even if you *were* the most reward-sensitive, sweet-loving person in the room — even if you drew the supposed personality — it would change nothing about your freedom. Understanding still kills the want. Once you see that BAD SUGAR gives you no genuine pleasure, treat or lift, there is nothing in any personality that can make you want a con you have already seen through. The most pleasure-prone person is the *first* to drop a trick that robs pleasure. I have watched "the biggest sweet tooth I know" walk away laughing because they finally saw the trick, not because they changed their nature.

You have nothing to square with your identity. Freedom does not ask you to become someone else. It asks you to see what made you *look* like someone else.

### What The Honest Evidence Actually Says

I want to keep you on honest ground here, because honesty is what makes the freedom hold when other voices try to wobble you later.

You may hear two completely opposite claims about sugar and addiction, both stated with great confidence. Some labs show, in animals kept on a binge-restrict schedule, a repeating pattern — bingeing, a mild dip after, cue-triggered wanting — and they describe that dependency plainly as mild but well-defined, small in magnitude compared to drugs, already dying when unfed. Other leading scientists look at the same animal work and say: hold on, the schedule drives the pattern, and human evidence for a diagnosis is thin and contested; dopamine firing is not the same as pleasure, and "sugar is addictive" remains an open question, not a settled verdict.

I tell you both because both are true to the limits of what we know. The honest place to stand is this: you do not need the word "addiction" to explain what you have lived. You have lived the signature — one bite becomes the box, the family size replaces the small, the wrappers glow before the bite — and the lived pattern is enough. Whether science finally calls it addiction or not does not change the trick, and it does not change the ease of escape. That honesty protects you from being recruited back by either slogan — "it's addictive so you are helpless" or "it's not addictive so it's your willpower."

On the human side, the same honesty applies. As you saw earlier, many report this pattern on validated scales of highly palatable food — self-report, not a formal diagnosis, and you are not uniquely weak for being among them. That settled token matters because shame told you this was a private flaw. It is not. It is a common response to a common trap.

And that escalation — "why am I not enjoying it like before, why do I need the big bar now" — has a lived explanation that fits without overclaim. Tolerance-like blunting and hyper-reactivity to cues are consistent with imaging work, in the sense that they *fit* the pattern where the same hit feels less and the packet rustle feels more, but imaging does not prove you are compelled or permanently altered. The picture is useful only for this: it explains why the trap needs more to feel less, and why the thought arrives before the taste.

So we do not build your freedom on a contested label or a brain scan. We build it on what you can verify in your own kitchen: the cue fires before the bite; the bite never beats the steady baseline of someone free of BAD SUGAR; the whine is mild, days-long, and starves when unfed while the belief is what truly fed it. Give the belief nowhere to live and the rest quiets on its own.

I lived this as "I am the sugar person" and I left it as "I was a person being tricked in a schedule." That re-label is not theory. It is relief.

### The Myths Q&A — Asked In Your Voice, Answered Plain

This room is deliberately separate, because after identity thoughts come the little technical myths that sound like nutrition but function like permission slips. I will quote each myth in your voice and answer it flatly. Each answer is two to six sentences. No lecture. No diet plan.

**"Fruit will spike me too — so I may as well have the cake?"**

No. Fruit is not BAD SUGAR in the sense this book uses it. An apple arrives as real food — water, fibre, chew, slow satisfaction — with its natural sweetness inside it, not engineered sweetness poured over it to make you chase. You do not lose control to apples the way we just described — "I can't stop once I start" — because the trap is not the sweetness, it is the engineered trap product around BAD SUGAR. You are free to enjoy fruit as food. The cake is not fruit.

**"I need sugar for my brain — it runs on glucose."**

Your brain runs on glucose, and your body makes the glucose it needs from real food without you ever pouring BAD SUGAR in from above. That is why quitting BAD SUGAR is not starvation and not deprivation — your system returns to its steady baseline and feeds your brain quite capably. The "I need sugar to function" feeling was the Worm's whine borrowing the language of need. Give it a few days unfed and watch how function returns.

**"Dark chocolate is healthy, so the little square is fine — even good for me?"**

If a laboratory tells you a square of dark chocolate contains a useful molecule, the manufacturer will put that molecule on the front and the trap logic on the inside. The health claim is what keeps the deliberate exception alive, and the deliberate exception is what keeps the Big Sweet Lie alive. As you saw with the cliff: your body can cope with an accidental blip, but your mind will not cope with a deliberate "just one" that feeds the Worm and reopens the decision. If you love cocoa, you will not miss the engineered bar once taste comes back.

**"I've cut down to only weekends / only a little — isn't that the sensible middle?"**

With this trap, cutting down makes the thing more precious and each hit more intense — forbidden-fruit, louder relief, faster creep. Anyone who has said "it's just a little, once a week is fine" knows the quiet march to "I don't keep it in the house" to keeping it in the house. Sensible for many things is not sensible for a trap designed to escalate on a schedule.

**"Isn't this just swapping one willpower for another — now I have to be disciplined about whole foods?"**

No. That is exactly the confusion sold by the Willpower Method, and we leave it here. You are not switching prisons. You are walking out of the one where you counted, resisted, and bargained, and returning to ordinary hunger and ordinary satisfaction, which need no discipline. Change nothing else in your life; inhabit meals as you were built to — hungry, delighted, finished — and let the applause for "control" go quiet.

**"What if I slip — does that prove I'm still a sugar person?"**

It proves you are a human who stepped on a rumble strip, not that you crashed. An accidental bite revives nothing if the belief stays dead; a deliberate "just one can't hurt" is the one that feeds the pattern. Notice the slip, keep the belief dead, and the whine passes. Shame is the trap trying to rehire you.

### A Letter From The Other Side

*I want you to hear one ordinary escape in one ordinary voice, with the wobble left in. Every number and sensation below is particular, not a promise — your wobble may be milder, your timeline different — but the shape is what matters: early wobble, then stability, when the belief dies. I include it here in the main flow because this is not an appendix. This is what freedom looks like from inside, right before you live it.*

> I am Sara. I am 38. I have two kids, a job in a primary school office, and a kitchen that used to beat me.
>
> For twelve years my day was the same shape. Cereal that was basically pudding, a "light" biscuit at 11, a sandwich on white at lunch with crisps, then at 3:07 the drawer. I can time it because the clock over the hall does that thick tick. At 3:07 my shoulders would drop and this thought would arrive fully formed: "sugary somethings to get me through." I didn't decide. I complied.
>
> Evenings were worse. I would put the kids to bed, tell myself "I've been good this week," and then do the sofa circuit — chocolate, then biscuits, then that hot, ashamed hunt for anything sweet left in the house. I once ate the edges off a birthday cake at 10:40pm while telling myself I wasn't really eating cake. I hid the wrappers in the bin under paper towels. I told friends "I don't keep it in the house" and then did a late shop for "milk."
>
> My family story was the sweet-tooth story. My mum says she has one, my sister says she has one, so I had one. It explained everything and changed nothing.
>
> The first week after I saw the trap — truly saw it, not tried it — was not a straight line. Day one was bright, almost giddy. Day two and three were wobbly. That empty, edgy, slightly needy feeling came in waves around the old times — 11 and 3:07 — and I noticed how much it was like a mild itch, not a command. I did not wrestle it. I did what this book had shown me and thought, "Good — it is dying." The wobble peaked on day three, softened on day four. By day five the 3:07 tick came and my shoulders stayed up. I made tea and answered the phone and realised ten minutes later I had walked past the drawer without the talk-track. That had not happened in a decade.
>
> What changed was not my discipline. What changed was my taste. On day nine I ate a peach on the playground bench at break. It was cold from my bag. I bit it and the juice ran down my chin and the kids laughed, and I thought: this is absurdly sweet. I had not tasted a peach in years because my mouth was coated. On day twelve I tried a square of the old chocolate from the cupboard to test the theory — honestly, to see if I missed it — and it was sickly, waxy, too much. My mouth wanted water. That was the first time I felt the pity instead of the envy. I saw the packet and felt relief it was not mine anymore.
>
> Numbers, because you asked for numbers. Not weight-loss promises — just my numbers. At the start I was 76 kilos, snoring, waking tired, wired and crashing every afternoon. Three weeks in I was 74 and sleeping through. Six weeks in the 3pm crash was simply gone. Not "managed." Gone. My shopping bill dropped by about £22 a week — I counted because I love counting something that makes me happy. The clearest number was the quietest: zero negotiations. No drawer debate. No "just a treat."
>
> The wobble did come back once, on a bad day. My daughter was ill, I was up in the night, and at 8pm the thought arrived in my old voice: "you deserve a treat, you've been good." I heard it, named it — "there is the Lie offering a rescue by the same hands that pushed me in" — and I chose tea and toast with butter and fruit. Ten minutes later the thought was gone and what stayed was a small, steady pride. It was not grim pride. It was warmth.
>
> I love food now more than when I called myself a sugar person. I eat roast vegetables until I am satisfied and stop. I love bread that is bread, not cake that pretends to be bread. I love that fruit tastes like dessert again. I did not become someone else. I came back.
>
> If you are reading this and thinking "but I do love food," I hear you. I did too. The love was real. The address was wrong.
>

I keep Sara's letter here for a reason. She was not a willpower hero. She was an ordinary eater on a trap schedule who stopped feeding the Lie and watched the Worm starve while life got sweeter without BAD SUGAR. Notice how she speaks about BAD SUGAR now — as something that took, not gave. Notice how she speaks about ordinary food — as something she enjoys easily, immediately and permanently. That is not a diet achievement. It is taste and energy returning to baseline.

You can test the same split in your own body tonight, not as homework but as noticing. Eat a plain, satisfying meal when genuinely hungry. Pause halfway. Ask: is this the Worm's whine — empty, edgy, slightly needy, impersonal — or is this true hunger with a location and a patience? Hunger warms and waits. The Worm nags and rushes. You will feel the difference once you are looking, and looking is all that is required.

### Even If You Were — You Are Still Free

Let me close the identity door and bolt it with kindness.

We have said the traits you thought were cause are result. We have named the trap honestly, without pretending certainty where there is honest disagreement. We have cleared the technical myths that keep a deliberate exception breathing.

There remains only the human worry: *What if there is something in me that will always pull me back?*

Here is my answer, as your guide who has watched thousands walk this same short step.

You do not need to be a different person to be free. You need to see one con correctly — that BAD SUGAR never was a genuine pleasure, treat or lift, and every drop of sweetness you loved was you, the moment, and real food being robbed and re-bottled.

When that is seen, the "sugar person" story collapses because its only evidence — the craving, the escalation, the 3:07 drawer, the "I can't stop once I start" — is now correctly attributed to its author. The portraits on the table admit who painted them. You stop defending a portrait of yourself that the manufacturer drew.

And if a flicker of the old story visits — on a tired evening, at a family table where someone says "we've always had a sweet tooth" — you will not need to argue your genetics. You will simply see the offer for what it is and feel what Sara felt: pity for the trap, relief for yourself, and a quiet laugh that you ever thought the packet was love.

There is a sentence I want you to carry into the next chapter, where we meet your most beautiful lie head-on and watch it dissolve in your own hands.

You were never a sugar person. You were a person the trap taught to talk about itself in sugar's language.

It is wonderful to stop translating.
