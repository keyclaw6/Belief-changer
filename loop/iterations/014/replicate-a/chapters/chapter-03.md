Chapter 3
WHAT GOOD DOES IT ACTUALLY DO?

**IN THIS CHAPTER**
The supermarket aisle — the label in your hand — the trolley that fills itself — the frog in warming water — the quiet list taped to the fridge

*If BAD SUGAR really did something good FOR you, you would feel better with it than without it — and you don't.*

### The Wrong Question

We have been asking the wrong question.

We stand in the supermarket aisle and turn the packet over and ask, is this bad for me? Is it too much? Should I feel guilty about this one? We weigh the harm on one side and the pleasure on the other, and because the pleasure feels real in the mouth, harm never quite wins. The scales are rigged.

I asked it that way for years too. Is it bad TO me? Of course it does things TO me — we all know it does plenty TO us. But that is not the question that sets you free. A thing can do plenty TO you and still be sold as if it does something FOR you. The trick is we never made it prove the FOR.

Try it plainly now with the packet in your hand.

Not, how bad is this TO me? But what is this actually doing FOR me, right now, in my body and my life, that I could not have without it?

If you ask it the first way, you can argue forever. If you ask it the second way, the trap has to produce an answer. And we are going to make it produce one for every story it ever told.

The fact is… BAD SUGAR has never needed to prove it does anything FOR you. It only needed you to believe it did.

We talk about it as if it were a genuine pleasure, treat or lift. That phrase — a genuine pleasure, treat or lift — is the name we gave to the feeling we think we get. We say it gives pleasure, so we excuse it. We say it is a treat, so we deserve it. We say it gives a lift, so we need it. Keep that exact phrase in your ear from now on, because every justification you have ever used is just a costume for that same claimed benefit. There is no other benefit. If that one phrase is not true, there is nothing left to defend. And we are going to test it, one costume at a time, until you see it clearly.

Do you want to know what you actually get when you unwrap it? Do you want to know what it does FOR you once the taste — three seconds on the tongue — is gone? And if it truly were a genuine pleasure, treat or lift, why would you need to bargain with yourself before you have it and feel a little less steady after?

### The Frog That Never Jumps

Let me show you why you feel fine while the trap is deepening, because this is why the TO question never woke you up.

Put a frog in cool water and heat it slowly. If you dropped it straight into boiling water it would kick and jump out. Heated slowly, it sits. The water gets warmer by a fraction, then a fraction more. The skin adjusts. The legs get drowsy. By the time the water is hot enough to harm it, the frog has decided this warmth is normal. It does not leap because nothing ever felt like a leap was needed.

We lived that.

No one woke up one morning and decided to feel tired, wired and crashing every afternoon. No one chose to need something sweet to get through a film, to finish emails, to be kind to themselves after a hard day. It happened a biscuit at a time. A sweetened yoghurt that wasn't sweet to your tongue anymore. White bread that stopped tasting like food unless it had a hit with it. A drink that was "just a drink" until the day without it felt flat. Each step was so small you called it normal. Each "just one" added a fraction of heat you stopped noticing.

I hear you say, but I feel fine. Most days I do. I function. That is exactly what the slowly heated water says. The body adjusts and calls the warm water normal, even while you are paying with steady taste, steady energy, steady calm — the very things BAD SUGAR pretends to give you.

The fact is… feeling fine is what a slow trap is designed to let you feel, right up until you see how much steadiness it has already taken.

Would you call water that is slowly cooking you kind because it has not boiled yet? Would you say the frog is enjoying the bath because it has not yet jumped?

### What We Tell Ourselves

If we are going to test whether BAD SUGAR is a genuine pleasure, treat or lift, we need your real reasons in your real words, not my summary of them. Here they are, exactly as we say them to ourselves. I have not tidied them. Listen for your own voice in them:

"It's a treat / I deserve it / it's how I reward myself"

"It gives me energy / I need sugar to function / it helps me concentrate"

"It's sociable / it's part of celebrating / everyone does it"

"It's just a little / once a week is fine / I don't keep it in the house"

"I love food / I've always had a sweet tooth / it's who I am"

Read that list again slowly. Which line is yours? Keep it in front of you.

I am not going to demolish those lines in this chapter. We will take them apart one by one where they belong — the treat story, the energy story — on ground that is now solid, with your own body as the proof. Today I want something simpler and more powerful: I want you to stop letting harm argue with pleasure, and start making pleasure prove itself.

Look at the label again. The packet is engineered to be eaten as a lift or a treat — sweets, chocolate, biscuits, cakes, pastries, puddings, ice cream, sugary drinks, sweetened yoghurts and cereals, white bread, white rice, chips, and the other ultra-processed carbs that behave like sugar in the trap. When this book says sugar, take it to mean BAD SUGAR. Good sweetness inside whole fruit, vegetables, plain dairy as part of real food is not BAD SUGAR and is not quit. That line matters because it removes the last fog: we are not testing food. We are testing the confection around food.

Hold the packet and ask the honest question we never asked before. What is this doing FOR me that real food, the moment itself, my own body at rest, would not do anyway? If the answer is nothing — and it is — then what you are holding is not a pleasure. It is a dose that first created the need it then briefly quietens, and called that quiet a lift.

### The Timetable That Builds The Craving

And here is how the timetable builds the need you think is you.

You already know the pattern: a little, then more, then "I can't stop once I start" in the evening. I hear you think, that is just weak willpower. The fact is… that pattern is what a schedule does, not what a character does.

When access comes in bursts — restrict, then binge, then restrict again — the loop learns to binge. Intermittent access trains loss of control; the binge gets bigger, the craving fires faster, the stop signal goes quiet. It is the timetable that is the trap. The same sweetness taken without that restrict-binge timetable does not produce the same escalation. This does not give you a diagnosis and it does not label you — it simply shows you why moderation rules so often make things worse, why "I'll just have a sliver" so often becomes the box, and why you are not imagining the pull. The pull is schedule-made.

Ask yourself. When you ate steadily and kept rules loose around real food, did the craving shout? Or did it shout loudest after the day you were "good," the morning you skipped, the week you moderated, the evening you told yourself just one? Whose voice was that — yours, or the timetable re-triggering the loop?

We were taught to blame ourselves for the binge the schedule taught.

The fact is… the craving you feel after a sweet is not proof BAD SUGAR gave you something good. It is proof the last dose made sure you would need another.

Once you see that, the whole list above changes its shape. You are not looking at five reasons to keep BAD SUGAR. You are looking at five costumes for a single claim — a genuine pleasure, treat or lift — that is about to be tested in daylight, one by one, where it cannot hide.

And if that claim falls?

There is nothing to give up. That is not a slogan. It is what is left when a claimed benefit turns out to have been empty. You do not lose a lift that was really a dip. You do not lose a treat that was taking taste, energy and calm while it sold them back to you. You escape it. The packet promised FOR and delivered only TO. Seeing that is not sacrifice. It is relief arriving early, before we have stripped a single packet from your hand.

We will strip them, easily, immediately. Not by fighting them. By seeing them. Each story will get its room, its scene, its honest reassigning of the credit to where it always belonged — to the moment, to the company, to your own body steady without interruption. By the time we have walked those rooms, you will not be negotiating with BAD SUGAR. You will be hungry to be free of it.

For now, I want you to leave the aisle exactly as you are. Keep eating exactly as you have been while you read. Do not cut down. Do not try to be good. I need you clear-headed, not deprived, so the belief can change before the behavior does.

And keep the rule that makes every other page work.
