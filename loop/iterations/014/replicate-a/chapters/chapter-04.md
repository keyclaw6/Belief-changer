Chapter 4
THE TREAT THAT TAKES

**IN THIS CHAPTER**
The school gate at half past three — the tin on top of the fridge — the sofa at eight o'clock — the house that was full before the gatecrasher arrived

*The love you felt was the evening itself — BAD SUGAR just learned to take the credit for it.*

### The Love Story We Were Sold

We all learned the same story when we were small.

We came out of school at half past three, tired and cold, and someone who loved us opened the tin on top of the fridge. A biscuit. A sweet from a pocket. A slice of cake held out on a plate. And with it came the voice: well done, you were good, you deserve this. We did not learn a taste that day. We learned an equation. Sweet equals love. Sweet equals comfort. Sweet equals you have earned kindness.

I learned it too, and I carried it for years without ever questioning it.

That equation is why the adult on the sofa at eight o'clock still hears the same voice. The day has been hard. The inbox is full, the traffic was cruel, the children needed everything. You sink down, and the thought arrives in your own kindest tone: "It's a treat / I deserve it / it's how I reward myself." Or later, softer, "celebrating, rewarding myself, going to the cinema, etc with chocolate." That is exactly how we say it to ourselves. I hear you, because I said it word for word.

And because it arrives as kindness, you do not argue with it. Who would want to argue with kindness after a hard day?

But listen gently now — not to whether the thought feels kind, but to what it actually does FOR you once the wrapper is empty. You thought BAD SUGAR was a genuine pleasure, treat or lift. That is the phrase we have used for it since we were children, and this chapter takes only that one costume off the con. Keep the other costumes — the energy story — for its own room. Today we test only the treat story, because if the treat story is false, the kindness you think you would lose never belonged to BAD SUGAR in the first place.

I know what you want to tell me here, and I want you to say it out loud so we can look at it together:

"But it *is* a treat. I look forward to it. It makes the evening feel like mine."

I understand that feeling completely. We are not here to tell you that you do not feel it. We are here to see where that feeling really lives.

So let me ask you plainly, and answer honestly just for yourself.

When you picture the birthday table — candles lit, everyone singing, your name called out warm and loud — what made your chest lift? Was it the sponge and icing, or was it being loved in a room full of people who came for you?

When the lights went down at the cinema and you had chocolate in your lap, what made you feel held? Was it the chocolate, or was it the warmth of the seat, the laughter, the hand you were holding, the rare hour when nothing was asked of you?

And on that eight o'clock sofa, after you swallowed the last bite, did you feel more soothed and steady, or did you feel a little more edgy, a little more needy, already half-thinking about whether there was more?

The fact is… BAD SUGAR made no part of that evening loving. The evening made you think BAD SUGAR was love, because BAD SUGAR was there when love happened. It was only ever sneaking a ride.

That reassigning is the whole trick of the Sugar Trap. We lived the love, then we credited the dose. The moment was the treat. BAD SUGAR took the credit.

It is not your fault you credited it wrongly. We were all taught to. The brainwashing started at the school gate.

And once you see who really provided the warmth — the company, the rest, the permission to stop for an hour — there is nothing left for BAD SUGAR to claim. The treat was the break you gave yourself. BAD SUGAR sold it back to you.

### The House And The Gatecrasher

Let me show you how that credit-theft works inside your body, because it will make you smile when you see it.

Think of real food as a house. A house that is already full. There is fruit on the table, vegetables roasted sweet by heat, plain bread that is food, yoghurt that is clean and calm. The house is warm, it feeds you, it finishes and you feel steady. That house is not missing anything. It has its own sweetness — GOOD SUGAR — already inside it as part of something whole.

Plain starches and quick carbs on their own are a bit different. They are lonely. They loiter. They call you to the kitchen, they invite company over. "Carbs are inherently lonely — they invite company over." That is their nature. And who gatecrashes the party?

BAD SUGAR does.

BAD SUGAR does not knock. It bursts in with loud music and bright paper, shoves the host into the hallway, eats everything on the table, and then tells you the party would have been dull without it. Sweets, chocolate, biscuits, cakes, pastries, puddings, ice cream, sugary drinks, sweetened yoghurts and cereals, white bread, white rice, chips — and every other ultra-processed carb eaten as a snack, a lift, a treat — all behave the same at that door. They gatecrash.

We fell for it because the gatecrasher is noisy and sweet on the tongue for three seconds. But watch what happens after the noise. The house that was steady before is now messy. The taste that was clear before is now dulled. The calm that would have settled is now craving. The gatecrasher did not add to the house. It robbed it and then sold you the idea that the robbery was the party.

You can test this in your own mouth without changing anything you eat this week. Eat a real meal slowly when you are truly hungry — not a dose, a meal. Notice how hunger arrives cleanly, how chewing satisfies, how enough feels like enough. That is the house working. Now remember the eight o'clock sofa after BAD SUGAR. Did it end cleanly like the house? Or did it leave you prowling the kitchen, a little restless, a little flat?

The house gives and finishes. The gatecrasher takes and wants more. Once you see the difference, you cannot confuse them again.

And that is how the Sugar Trap keeps selling love while delivering the opposite. It arrives where real sweetness already lives and calls the sweetness its own.

### Why One Bite Starts Talking About The Box

If the treat really were kindness, one would be enough. Kindness does not need a family size to prove it was kind.

But you know the other voice too, the one that says, "I just crave sweets and when i eat them, i feel like i cant stop." That is not you failing at moderation. That is the gatecrasher changing the house after it arrives.

Here is a plain way to hold it. One way of describing why the same bar feels less than it used to, and why you find yourself thinking you need the bigger bag now, is that the reward feeling gets a bit crowded and blunted with repetition, so the same hit feels less warm, while at the same time the wrappers and smells before the bite start to glow hotter and call louder. That picture fits what has been seen — it does not prove you are broken or compelled, and it is not a life sentence. It simply names why escalation makes sense inside the trap: the enjoyment goes down while the cue goes up. So you chase more to feel less, and the chase itself keeps the loop alive.

This is why the reward frame is so sticky. Dessert as self-payment for virtue, for getting through, for being good, keeps the loop alive because you never get to learn that the virtue already paid you. The sofa, the company, the release — that was the payment. BAD SUGAR stepped in front of it and said, "I did that." Next time, the craving fires before the bite, not because you need it, but because the brain learned where the gatecrasher used to appear.

Ask yourself. When you were kind to yourself without BAD SUGAR — a bath, a walk with someone you love, an early night, a meal you lingered over — did the kindness feel smaller without the sweet? Or did it feel cleaner, because no one was shouting over it?

The fact is… the treat that takes never leaves you feeling more rewarded. It leaves you feeling more in need of reward, which is how you know it was not a reward at all.

### The Same Evening, Without The Wrapper

I want you to live the same evening again, exactly the same, and see where the pleasure was.

It is eight o'clock. The day has been hard. The flat is quiet. You are on the sofa. In one version, you have the packet. You tear it, the taste is loud for a second, sweet and loud, then a stickiness, then a sag. Shoulders drop. The packet rustles again. You bargain. You tell yourself you will be good tomorrow. You go to bed a little wired, a little tired, a little cross with yourself, without ever deciding to feel cross.

Now play the same evening again, with nothing else changed. Same sofa. Same tiredness. Same people. Same film. Same right to be kind to yourself. Only this time there is no gatecrasher. You make a warm drink, you eat a real piece of food if you are hungry — fruit, plain yoghurt, bread that is food — or you eat nothing at all because you are not hungry and you are allowed to be not hungry. You let the hour be the hour. The film is still funny. The hand you hold is still warm. The relief of stopping still arrives. Only now, when the film ends, the house is still tidy. You feel steady, not edgy. You go to bed clear.

What was different between those two films that made the first feel like love and the second feel like missing something? Be honest. Nothing was different except the bright paper. The love was the sofa, the permission, the company. BAD SUGAR was the noisy guest who made you think his noise was the warmth.

I am not asking you to believe that sweetness itself is forbidden. Good sweetness inside whole fruit, vegetables, plain dairy as part of real food is not BAD SUGAR and is not quit. You are not giving up sweetness. You are evicting the gatecrasher who dulled it.

Feel how that changes the old sentence "I deserve a treat." You do deserve a treat. You deserved it at the school gate, and you deserve it now. A treat that takes is not a treat. A treat that takes is a con that taught you to pay for your own kindness with craving and a little shame, and to call that payment love.

You do not need to be deprived to be kind. You need to be free to be kind without interruption.

So here is the inversion, flat and warm, as one short verdict.

It was never the sweet that was sweet. It was you.

You were the one who made the evening kind, and BAD SUGAR sold you back your own sweetness at a price you paid in edginess and want. Seeing that does not take the treat away. It gives you the evening back, entire, without the gatecrasher eating the end of it.

Hold that. Let the reward story fall where it stood. You have lost no love you need to mourn. You have found where the love really lived, and you can get on with enjoying your life there.

We will leave the lift story for its own light. For now, the treat has told the truth about itself. It takes.

**SUMMARY**
- The treat story was learned early, where sweet was paired with love, and then replayed as kindness to yourself on the sofa
- BAD SUGAR does not add to real food and real moments — it gatecrashes a house that was already sweet and steady and leaves it less so
- Needing more to feel less fits a crowding and blunting of reward with brighter cues before the bite, which explains the family-size chase without meaning you are broken
- The love and relief you felt on good evenings came from the rest, the company, the moment — BAD SUGAR was only sneaking a ride
- When you live the same evening without the gatecrasher, the kindness remains and the craving and shame do not — which is how you know the kindness was never the dose
