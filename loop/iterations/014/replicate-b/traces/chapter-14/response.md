Chapter 14
ORDINARY DAYS

IN THIS CHAPTER — The morning kettle and the quiet tin, the supermarket aisle bright with packets, the evening sofa without the grazing hand, the open cage and the old track in the dust

*You don't have to learn a new life without BAD SUGAR — you only have to live the day you already know and feel how light it is when the con is gone.*

### THE KETTLE IN THE MORNING

You wake up and the house is the same.

Same kettle. Same cool kitchen light. Same tin on the counter where the biscuits lived. The clock says the same time it always said. Nothing has been rearranged, nothing taken away that you needed.

We used to load this hour with a job for BAD SUGAR. Kettle on, tin open, first dose to get going, as if the day needed a hit to start. We told ourselves the sweet made the morning kind. It never did. It only fed the little whine and called the quiet that followed a lift.

This morning you do what you have always done. You fill the kettle. You wait for it to shudder and click. You make the tea, the coffee, the hot water — whatever is yours — and you stand there a moment, hands around the warmth.

The tin is still there, or it is empty in the bin. It does not matter. You are not avoiding it. You are not testing yourself against it. You are inhabiting your own kitchen confidently, because you are out of the Sugar Trap and you know it.

A thought flickers, light and quick: have something with it, just to be sociable with yourself, just to mark the morning. That thought is not you deciding. That was the Big Sweet Lie trying to translate a habit into a need.

Did the biscuit ever boil the kettle? Did it make the light kind? Did it give you the steadiness you feel right now as the steam clears?

No. The warmth is the kettle, the light, you waking up in your own house. The sweetness that matters is already in real food when you are truly hungry, not in that engineered fix that wired you and then called the wire comfort.

Say what is true, in the words you now own:

FANTASTIC! I'M FREE!

Not because you are being brave. Because you are telling the truth. The morning is wonderful without the con sneaking a ride. You feel clear, steady, relief where the negotiation used to be. You put the cup down and get on with enjoying your life.

### THE AISLE IN THE MIDDLE OF THE DAY

You go to the shop. Same doors sliding open, same chill from the fridges, same bright canyon of packets at eye height, at child height, weaponized sweetness shouting treat yourself, reward yourself, you deserve this.

We spent years believing that aisle was pleasure on shelves. We believed those colours were love, that the packet was promising us something we would miss if we walked past. That was the brainwashing talking, not the body.

Today you walk it as a free person. Not hugging the far wall. Not clenching past the sweets. You walk down the middle because you have no reason to avoid anything. The packets are still there. They are not yours. Not because you are being strong, but because you see them plainly now — processed poison in pretty paper, the manufacturer profiting while you would have paid with tired afternoons and restless evenings.

You stop where real food lives. Fruit that smells of itself. Bread that is food, not confection. Vegetables with weight and colour. Plain dairy. You hear your own hunger, when it is there, and you answer it with food you actually taste, instead of a hit that coats the mouth and begs for another.

A voice tries, old script: just a little one, for the trolley, everyone does it, it's sociable to bring something home.

That strain belongs to the Willpower Method, not to freedom. The Willpower Method taught you that the aisle was a test of grit and that passing the sweets was deprivation you had to endure. You are not enduring. You are escaping. You look at the packet and you feel what is true — not envy, but relief. Pity, don't envy, the packet-passers filling their baskets with promises that leave them negotiating at the till. You are not missing their pleasure. They are missing your freedom.

And because I have walked this aisle with thousands, I will tell you what happens in the body so you are not fooled. If that empty, edgy, slightly needy feeling flickers as you pass the till, smile. It is not hunger. It is the old perimeter calling. It is the last whine of a tiny creature that is starving because you stopped feeding it. It peaks in a few days and passes on its own. Each flicker is a cheer that the trap is emptying. Think again, glad and silly:

FANTASTIC! I'M FREE!

Do not preach to anyone in that aisle. Do not argue, do not turn your trolley into a lesson. Let your ease do the talking. Your freedom is marvellous news you carry, not a speech you give.

### THE SOFA IN THE EVENING

Evening comes. Same sofa. Same lamp. Same programme you half-watch.

We used to sanctify this hour. Hard day, deserve it, sofa plus sweet equals comfort. We grazed not because we were hungry, but because the day had left that wired, restless edge and we had been taught that a fix would soften it. It never softened anything. It wired it further, then crashed it, and left us tired, wired and crashing while we blamed the day.

Tonight you sit down with the same lamp and the same tiredness, and you feel the difference because you are not feeding the loop.

The hand that used to go to the packet stays still. Not held still. Just not needed. You have eaten real food today when you were hungry, tasted it, felt the clean satisfaction finish, not the sickly stick and the sag ten minutes later. The evening is not a gap to fill with grazing. It is time returned to you — clear, marvellous, a little exciting because it is yours again.

A thought arrives, soft: just this once, to make tonight nicer, to celebrate, to take the edge off.

You know that script. You will not reopen the decision. Once the Lie is dead you do not bargain with it. You do not suppress the thought either — you cannot not think about cake when you are told not to think about cake. You reframe it. You let the thought come and you attach joy to it. An old enemy died, and you are glad.

You walked past the drawer and felt relief.

That is the proof. The 3pm drawer that used to slide open by itself is still there, and you walked past it and felt relief, not loss. Not pride at having resisted — relief at not needing to. The pleasure was never in the packet. It was in the company, the lamplight, the body settling after real food, the show, the breath out. BAD SUGAR only ever sneaked a ride on those things and then sold them back to you as its gift.

Ask yourself, plainly, and let the answer do the work: when has the evening ever been sweeter because you were wired and guilty on the sofa? When has a hit ever made a bad day better, rather than busier in the mouth and heavier after?

You know the answer. It is the other way around.

### THE OPEN CAGE

I want to show you what that faint unease is, if it comes in these first ordinary days, so you never mistake it for need.

For years you lived in a cage you did not see. Then yesterday you saw it, you named it, you made the vow and you stepped out. The door is standing wide open.

Now you are standing just outside the cage, in your own kitchen, your own shop, your own evening, and your feet still know the old track. You pad a step or two along the bars, not because you need the cage, but because your body remembers pacing there. The lion in the zoo does this when the keeper leaves the gate open. He walks the old perimeter out of habit, sniffs the bars that are no longer a wall, and feels a flicker of oddness. That oddness is not proof he belongs inside. It is habit, not hunger. He is free and his feet have not yet learned the new map.

If you feel that flicker — a moment of shall I, a small restlessness at the old times — do not call it craving that proves you need BAD SUGAR. Call it what it is: cage-habit, converting, each time you notice it, into another hit of freedom.

You are not pacing because the trap still holds you. You are pacing because you left the Sugar Trap and your nervous system is catching up with the truth your mind already knows.

In those moments, rejoice at the dead enemy. Do not mourn a lost friend. There is no friend in that cage. There was a con that robbed you of taste, energy and calm and sold itself as the very thing that gave them. When you hear the old path call, rejoice that the call is fainter, that you can stand in the same kitchen and feel steadier without the fix, that you can walk the bright aisle and feel relief rather than tug. Each time you smile at the whine instead of feeding it, the track grows fainter.

This is what ordinary days are for. Not to test you. To prove you. To let the lion learn the plain. Same kettle, same aisle, same sofa — new feeling. Wonderful freedom, repeated in the places that used to be triggers, until the trigger becomes the celebration itself.

### WHEN THE THOUGHT COMES ANYWAY

Thoughts will still come. That is not failure. That is how a mind works. A packet rustles on television. A smell blooms by the bakery door. Someone offers cake and says go on, one can't hurt.

You have already been handed the scripts for life, and they live now as speech, not as a curriculum to study.

Never reopen the decision. You made it once, solemnly, after seeing the last ordinary sweet plainly. The decision is not opened for debate each time a thought passes. A free person does not re-decide freedom every hour.

Reframe, don't suppress. You do not fight the picture of cake. You let it arrive and you give it its new meaning. Whenever sugar crosses your mind, think: FANTASTIC! I'M FREE! — and mean it — and get on with enjoying your life. The thought that used to be a cue to feed becomes the cue to celebrate.

Rejoice at the whine, pity don't envy, don't evangelize. If that empty, edgy, slightly needy feeling flickers, rejoice — it is dying. If you see others eating BAD SUGAR and telling themselves just a treat, I've been good this week, I don't keep it in the house, pity them warmly, do not envy them, and say nothing. Your ease is eloquent enough.

Forgive slips instantly and refuse to make them deliberate. Your body can cope with an accidental blip — a mistaken ingredient, a mouthful taken before you remembered. Your mind will not cope with a deliberate exception dressed as sociability or self-kindness. If you slip, do not catastrophize. It is a rumble strip, not a crash. Name it — that was the old whine, that was the old lie — think FANTASTIC! I'M FREE! and stay free. You have not blown anything. You have been reminded why you left, and the reminder makes the vow quieter and stronger.

Live the day. Make the tea. Buy real food you love. Sit on the sofa and notice how warm the evening is without the con. You are not counting days. You are not on a journey that never ends. You are free as of now, and each ordinary day is not a test of whether you can stay that way, but a simple, marvellous proof that you already are.

### SUMMARY

* Mornings, shops and evenings are not trials to endure — they are the same places I always lived, now felt as relief and steadiness without BAD SUGAR.
* A flicker of restlessness is not need returning — it is cage-habit, the old track fading while freedom grows.
* A thought about sweets is not a command to feed — it becomes a cue to celebrate that I am free.
* I do not envy or preach to others still in the trap — I pity them gently and let my ease speak.
* An accidental slip is not failure — it is a warning to remember, forgiven instantly, while a deliberate exception is the trap trying to revive the lie.
