Chapter 6
EATING LIKE WE WERE BUILT TO

IN THIS CHAPTER — A quiet kitchen at noon, a market stall of fruit, a plate of roast vegetables and honest bread, the clean quiet after the last bite

*Real eating is already pleasure — your body knows hunger and satisfaction without BAD SUGAR.*

### THE WORRY THAT COMES AFTER THE TRUTH

You have seen it clearly now.

BAD SUGAR does not reward you. It does not lift you. We watched the drawer at 3:07 and the tight shoes, and you felt in your own afternoon that the rescue was the trap.

When that clears, a new worry often arrives, very quietly, and I want to meet it before it grows roots.

It says: "Fine — so BAD SUGAR gives me nothing. But then what is eating? Will it be bland? Will it be control? Will I have to manage myself forever around food?"

I understand that fear. Of course you feel it. We were taught that without the sweet hit, eating is discipline and real food is the worthy, boring bit you get through to earn the fun bit.

The fact is you have it backwards, and your own body is about to show you.

We fell into the trap together — we learned to call grazing and fix-hunting "eating," and to call real eating "eating properly," as if pleasure and food had parted company. That whole picture was the con talking. Real eating was never the dull part. It was the house. BAD SUGAR was the gatecrasher who ate the host and told you the party was his.

I am not going to argue you into trusting your body. I am going to ask you to feel it, in a single ordinary meal, with your own mouth and belly, so the idea of bland freedom dies there and then.

### BEFORE HUNGER ARRIVES

Think of a morning when you have not eaten for a while. Not starving. Not faint. Just empty in the clean sense.

We have been taught to fear that empty. The trap taught us to close it instantly — a biscuit in the car, a sweet at the desk, a sugary drink to "keep going" — so we rarely let true hunger arrive at all.

Let it arrive.

True hunger does not shout or bite. It gathers. First a little lightness. Then a quiet signal, low in the belly, saying "ready." Your mouth waters a fraction. Food becomes interesting — not frantic, not "I-need-to-eat-something-RIGHT-NOW," just interested. The world looks a little sharper. You can picture an apple, a warm bowl, a plate of roasted carrots and potatoes, a piece of honest bread that smells like bread, and you want them for themselves.

That is not a craving. That is appetite, and it is wonderful.

We were built for this. I have sat at tables with children who have never been taught to fear hunger, and I have watched them — they play, they pause, their eyes light up, they eat with relish, they stop. No counting. No bargaining. No crash after. Their bodies lead, and pleasure follows, cleanly. That intelligence is still in you. It did not leave. It was only drowned out by a louder, stickier signal — the Worm's whine that we mistook for hunger.

Let me ask you as plainly as a friend would, and trust your body to answer:

When you feel that quiet, gathering readiness, do you feel frantic or calm?

Do you feel empty in a panicked way, or clear and ready to enjoy food?

You know the answer, because you have felt it on mornings you ate a little later without thinking, or after a walk, or on holiday when you forgot to snack. That clear empty is not the enemy. It is the beginning of satisfaction.

### THE MOUTH KNOWS

Now take real food — not a confection in pretty paper, but food as it grows and as a kitchen makes it.

A peach that gives under your thumb and smells like summer. Roast vegetables with edges crisped, soft inside, salted just enough. A piece of bread that tears with a little chew, that tastes of wheat and warmth. Plain yoghurt, cool and tart, with berries that pop. A bowl of soup that steams your glasses.

Put a bite in your mouth and chew slowly. Not as a discipline — as attention.

What happens?

Taste arrives. Not the shriek of weaponized sweetness that blows the palate out for three seconds and then leaves you flat, but a steady, layered taste that you can follow. Sweetness is there where it belongs — inside the fruit, inside the roast, inside the milk — bright but not blinding. Salt, sour, bitter and sweet move together. Your jaw knows what to do. Your saliva knows what to do. The bite finishes.

Take another bite. Taste is still there. Perhaps more. Real food does not vanish after three bites into "I need another packet." It holds. It says more as you go on, not less.

And notice what your body does while you chew. It says yes. It says "this is food." It says "keep going" while you need it, without jitter, without wire.

We were told real food is less exciting than BAD SUGAR. I want you to feel how wrong that is, with your own tongue.

Which was more pleasant to linger on — the blast that disappeared, or the taste that stayed?

Which felt kinder in your chest after five minutes — the hit and sag, or the steady warmth?

When you let yourself eat real food slowly, you do not need a trick to enjoy eating. The enjoyment is built in. The body was built to like what keeps it steady.

### THE CLEAN FINISH

Keep eating. No hurry. No rule about how much.

Then, at some simple moment, something marvelous happens.

You are done.

Not stuffed. Not sighing with regret. Not negotiating "just one more." Done. A clean, quiet finish that your belly announces without words: enough. The plate can wait. The thought of food recedes the way thirst recedes after water — not by force, but because the need is met.

I love that moment, and I want you to get greedy for it. That "enough" is the pleasure we forgot while we chased fixes that never say enough. A fix says "more." Real food says "done," and the done feels marvellous — clear, light, settled. You can get up and get on with your life. No dip to feed. No drawer calling. No crash to pay for. You are not wired. You are not tired. You are steady, and you are satisfied.

We call that satisfaction a small thing because the trap taught us to measure pleasure only in spikes. But ask your body which it prefers to live in all afternoon:

A spike that shouts and then crashes, or a steady that lets you think, move, laugh, work?

A taste that sneaks a ride for half a minute, or a satisfaction that stays and leaves you free?

You have your answer already, because you have felt both today in your own mouth. The steady does not need to be chased. It is yours by design.

And here is the release that makes it free: your body makes the fuel it needs from real food itself, quietly and steadily, so living without BAD SUGAR is not deprivation, not running on empty. You lose nothing your body actually needs. You simply stop interrupting a system that already knew how to keep you clear and steady, easily, immediately and permanently.

You do not need to learn a new way to eat. You need to let the old way return — the one that was there before the con got between you and your plate. Hunger that gathers, taste that lingers, satisfaction that says "done" and lets you go. That rhythm is not control. It is pleasure doing its proper job.

Let yourself feel the bigness of that.

You were not boring without BAD SUGAR. You were robbed of taste, and of the quiet pride that comes from being satisfied. That sweetness, that pride — it comes back the moment you stop feeding the trap. Not in a month. Not once you have earned it. In the very next ordinary meal you eat with attention.

So eat. When clean hunger comes, take real food you like, chew it, taste it, stop when you are done. No system. No counting. No special way. Just you, and food, as we were built to do it.

You will feel it at once: eating itself is the favourite part. It always was.

SUMMARY

* Real eating is pleasure on its own — hunger gathers calmly, taste lingers, and satisfaction finishes cleanly without a crash.
* The clear empty of true hunger is not panic or need for a fix; it is your body getting ready to enjoy food.
* Real food holds its taste and says "done" when you have had enough; that steady done leaves you clear and free to get on with enjoying your life.
* Your body makes the fuel it needs from real food, so living without BAD SUGAR is not deprivation or a special diet.
