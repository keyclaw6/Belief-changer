Chapter 4
THE TREAT THAT TAKES

IN THIS CHAPTER — The childhood kitchen and the biscuit tin that meant love, the 8pm sofa and the hard day's reward, the house that is real food and the gatecrasher who eats the host

*The thing you call a treat does not give you love — it borrows the love that was already in the room and then asks you to pay for it.*

### THE KINDNESS WE THOUGHT IT WAS

We all learned this very young, and we learned it kindly.

You came in from school, cold hands, hot cheeks. Someone who loved you opened a tin. There were biscuits in there, or sweets from a pocket, or a chocolate bar broken into squares. The kitchen was warm. You were seen. You were praised. You were given something sweet, and the sweetness and the love arrived together.

No child sorts that. The brain does not file two labels — "I was loved" and "I was given BAD SUGAR." It files one warm memory: sweetness is love.

Then we grow up. The tin is no longer in a kitchen. It is in a drawer at work, a shelf at the petrol station, a fridge after the children are finally in bed. The day has scraped you. Someone was rude. Something went wrong. You are tired in a way that sleep will not fix in ten minutes. And the old file slides open by itself. We hear a voice in our own dialect, soft and utterly convincing:

"It's been a hard day. I deserve a treat. It's how I reward myself."

"celebrating, rewarding myself, going to the cinema, etc with chocolate."

You are not weak for thinking that. You are tender, and you are loyal to a kindness you were taught. The voice sounds like self-care. It sounds like you are finally being nice to yourself after being useful to everyone else.

But I want you to look, with me, at what happens in the next twenty minutes when you answer that voice with BAD SUGAR — not as a judge, but as an investigator who likes you very much and has seen this trick a thousand times.

You reach for it. The first bite is sweet. For three or four seconds there is taste — real taste, no one denies taste exists. Then taste fades, as taste always does. What follows is not love. What follows is the hand going back, the packet crinkling, the quick arithmetic — "I'll just have one more" — the faint, familiar tug that you have misread for years as affection but is actually a need to repeat.

Love does not do that. Love settles you. This agitates you and then calls the agitation proof that you needed it.

The fact is… BAD SUGAR sells itself as love but delivers craving and a little shame. The moment was love. BAD SUGAR sneaked a ride.

We were told BAD SUGAR was a genuine pleasure, treat or lift. That is the only name we will give to that claimed benefit from here on, because it keeps the promise precise. And on the promise of love — on the treat — it fails its own test.

### THE TEN SECONDS THAT STEAL THE EVENING

Stay on that 8pm sofa with me, because this is where we live.

You have earned a pause. The dishes are done or they can wait. The house is quiet. You have a show you like, or a person you like, or just the relief of sitting down. You tell yourself, "I'll have a little something sweet and then I'll properly relax."

So you have it. Notice what actually happens, in your body, not in your story about it.

Seconds one to ten: sweet taste on the tongue. Pleasant. Brief. Real.

Seconds ten to sixty: the taste is already gone, but the mouth wants it again. Not because you are still enjoying it, but because the sweetness has fired a signal that says "more." You feel a little more awake and a little less settled at once — that is not comfort, that is a nudge.

Minutes five to twenty: the packet is open on your lap. You are not tasting now. You are feeding a rhythm. You may eat fast, or you may negotiate — "just one more square" — but attention has left the show or the conversation and gone to the packet. If someone speaks to you, you are half elsewhere.

After: the evening you meant to reward has a faint residue. A stickiness on the teeth, a fullness that is not satisfaction, a thought that flickers — "why did I have that?" — which you quickly cover with "well, I deserved it." You were wonderful before you opened the packet. You are a little less wonderful after, and you call that kindness.

If this is kindness, why does kindness need a second helping to prove it was kind?

Let me ask you plainly, as one friend to another, and trust you to answer honestly from your own sofa, not from what you have been told you should feel:

When the taste is gone after those ten seconds, do you feel more loved, or more restless?

If the reward truly rewarded you, why do you need to reassure yourself that you deserved it — and why does the reassurance feel thinner each time?

And if the treat were the source of the comfort, how is it that comfort was already there — in the warmth, the pause, the company — before the first bite?

You know the answers because you have lived them. The comfort was yours. The show was the comfort. The quiet was the comfort. The company, where there was company, was the comfort. BAD SUGAR did not bring them. It stepped in front of them, took the credit, and left you holding the packet instead of the evening.

That is the con of the Sugar Trap. It sells itself as the thing that makes hard days soft, and then it makes the soft part harder to feel. The treat takes more than it gives, and it takes it precisely by pretending to give.

I say this harshly about the trap because I am warm about you. You were not foolish to want self-payment after a hard day. You were not greedy. You were conned out of your own evening by something engineered to look like a kindness. We all were. That is what we mean when we say brainwashing — not that you are silly, but that someone dressed a con in the clothes of love and hung it at eye level for your whole life.

You have guessed the truth already: you never needed BAD SUGAR to make a hard day kinder. You needed rest, warmth, a laugh, a stretch, a breath, a hand held. Real food, where you were truly hungry, is part of that kindness too — and GOOD SUGAR, the natural sweetness already inside whole fruit, vegetables and plain dairy as part of real food, is not BAD SUGAR and is not what we are quitting. There is a whole world of sweetness you keep. The theft we are naming is not sweetness itself. It is the weaponized sweetness that gatecrashes real sweetness and calls itself the treat.

### THE HOUSE AND THE GATECRASHER

Let me show you the same theft with a picture you will not forget, because you will see it every time you walk the aisles.

Think of real eating, real food, as a house. Not a lecture, not a diet sheet — a house you already own. The house has a kitchen, a table, chairs that fit you. The hunger that brings you to that house is honest. It arrives cleanly, it grows gradually, it is answered by chewing, by flavour, by the steady satisfaction that finishes and lets you get on with enjoying your life. A piece of fruit, a bowl of soup, roast vegetables, bread that is food not confection, dairy as it is — the house is solid, it holds you, its sweetness is inside the food, not poured on top.

Carbs, on their own, are inherently lonely — they invite company over. Plain starch loiters. It is there, a little pale, not quite a party by itself. Left alone it would be a quiet guest.

BAD SUGAR is the gatecrasher.

It does not knock. It bursts in with loud music, pretty paper, promises of a better party. "I am the treat! I am the fun! Without me it's bland!" It eats the host. It turns your attention from the meal to itself. The house — your body, your real appetite, your real taste — goes quiet while the gatecrasher shouts. For a few seconds you taste the shout. Then the shout wants another shout.

You know this shape from your own kitchen. A proper meal leaves you clear and steady and done. A dose of BAD SUGAR after that meal — or instead of that meal — leaves you not done. It leaves you looking for the next dose. That is not because real food failed. It is because the gatecrasher took the house and then blamed the house for feeling empty.

I want you to see this so clearly that the next time you hear "it's just a treat," you smile. A treat that takes the steady satisfaction of eating and replaces it with a need to eat again is not a treat in any honest sense of the word. A host who lets a gatecrasher eat the host is not being hospitable.

We have said the behaviour does things TO you and we ask what it does FOR you. Apply that here. The gatecrasher does plenty TO you — it lifts, then it drops, it turns appetite into craving — but what does it do FOR the house? Nothing the house did not already do, only louder for ten seconds and then much worse after.

Once you see the house, you cannot unsee it. The next time you sit at a table, you will notice: the meal is wonderful without the gatecrasher. The evening is wonderful without the gatecrasher. The warmth you felt as a child was the kitchen, the person, the being seen — the gatecrasher was just standing in the doorway holding a packet and taking the bow.

### WHY ONE BECOMES THE FAMILY SIZE

There is an objection that rises at this point, and I want to ventriloquize it fairly, because it feels very personal and very true when you are in it.

"But I can't stop once I start. I buy the small bar and I eat the family size. I tell myself just one biscuit and I eat the box. That proves I love it. That proves it gives me something real, because I chase it."

I know you feel that. We have all stood with the empty packet in our hands and felt the hot flash of "I can't stop once I start."

That feeling is not proof that BAD SUGAR is a genuine pleasure. It is proof that the trap is doing exactly what it was engineered to do.

Look again at the house picture. When the gatecrasher gets in, it does not want to sit down and be part of dinner. It wants the house to itself. One hit triggers the wanting for the next hit, not because you have discovered joy but because the hit has crowded and blunted your reward circuitry for a little while, so the same taste does less and asks for more. That is why "just one" so often becomes the box. That is why the small bar becomes the family size. That is why you find yourself saying the line you never meant to say: "I just crave sweets and when i eat them, i feel like i cant stop."

I must be honest with you about what that mechanism is and is not. What you are feeling — tolerance, needing more to get the same brief sweet note, and the cue that fires before the bite when you see wrappers or smell the bakery — is consistent with what brain scans show in some groups when highly palatable food is repeatedly binged, but those scans do not prove you are compelled or broken, and much of that imaging comes from studies of obesity or binge-eating samples, not everyday grazers, so it suggests a pattern rather than pronounces your fate. The scans show wanting and liking are not the same thing, and that cues can fire hard before taste adds anything after. That is exactly what you feel on the sofa: the wrapper glows before you open it; after you taste, there is little glow left, only the cue for another.

That distinction is freedom. If the glow lives in the wrapper, in the packet, in the picture on the ad — not in the eating — then the pleasure was never in the BAD SUGAR. The pleasure was in the anticipation the trap taught you to feel, and anticipation is the trap's favourite counterfeit for love.

Think of the last time you ate the family size when you meant to have two squares. Were squares two to ten more pleasurable than squares one and two? Honestly? Or were they more frantic, more automatic, more about finishing what you started and quieting the edginess that the first squares created?

BAD SUGAR does not keep its promise as you increase the dose. It keeps its trap. Each dose crowds the reward a little more, so you need a bigger dose to get the same ten seconds. The family size is not proof you love sweetness. It is proof the gatecrasher needs a bigger house each time, and the house is you.

There is relief in that honesty. You are not endlessly greedy. You were being crowded. When you stop feeding the crowding, your taste comes back. Your reward settles. You find — not through willpower, but through the removal of the thing that was crowding you — that a single piece of fruit can taste vividly sweet again, that a meal can satisfy without negotiation, that "enough" returns without effort. That is not deprivation. That is the house returning to you.

And notice what we have not said. We have not said you may never enjoy anything sweet. We have not said sweetness itself is forbidden. GOOD SUGAR — the sweetness already inside real food — stays with you for the rest of your life if you want it, freely and without any of this bargaining. What we are leaving is the engineered dose that sells itself as a treat while steadily taking the capacity to feel treated.

### THE NIGHT WITHOUT THE WRAPPER

We have taken the idea apart. Now we must put the evening back together, so you feel the reclaimed kindliness in your own hands.

Go back to that 8pm sofa. Same day. Same hardness. Same wonderful person you are after a long day — tender, tired, deserving of something kind. Play the evening twice.

First, with BAD SUGAR. You sit, you open, you taste for ten seconds, you chase, you negotiate, you finish the packet a little too fast, you feel a little wired and a little flat, you half-watch the show. You go to bed a little stickier, a little less proud.

Now replay it, beat for beat, without BAD SUGAR — not with a lecture, not with a substitute that feeds the same lie, just without the gatecrasher.

You sit. You breathe. You notice you really are tired. You let the tiredness be true instead of sugaring over it. You make a hot drink if you like. You eat if you are truly hungry — real food, eaten slowly, chewed, tasted, finished — and you feel the clean satisfaction that says "done" instead of "more." The show is the same show but you actually see it. The conversation, if there is one, is a real conversation because your attention is not in a packet. The warmth you give yourself is not a packet either. It is rest, or a bath, or a laugh, or an early night — the adult equivalent of the kitchen that loved you as a child, minus the tin that borrowed the credit.

Ask yourself, which evening was more like love?

Which evening left you steadier, clearer, more relieved?

Which evening did you actually enjoy?

You know. We all know when we let ourselves compare like that, without the slogan in our ears. The warmth, the relief, the sense of being looked after — those belonged to the pause, the rest, the company, the meal that truly satisfied. BAD SUGAR did not bring them. It interrupted them and then asked you to thank it.

This is the inversion at the heart of the treat lie: BAD SUGAR promises to give you what you get anyway when you are free of BAD SUGAR — and then, by taking it, persuades you that you needed BAD SUGAR to get it.

The rescuer is the perpetrator. The kindness that offers to save you from the hard day is the very thing that made the day's end feel needy. Like the friend who pushes you in the river and then dives in to save you so you thank the hand that pushed you — the relief you feel after the hit is relief from a discomfort the hit created.

Once you have seen that, you cannot be bribed with it again. You can still love sweetness. You can still love food. You can still reward yourself — fiercely, tenderly, often. You will. But you will no longer mislabel the gatecrasher as the host.

There is nothing kind about a kindness that needs repeating every twenty minutes to prove it was kind.

There is no love in a treat that leaves you a little less loving toward yourself after.

The moment was love. BAD SUGAR was only ever sneaking a ride.

SUMMARY

* The reward FRAME — "I deserve a treat / it's how I reward myself" — keeps the loop alive, but it does not show BAD SUGAR ever gives love or comfort as a real benefit.
* The ten seconds of taste are real; the promised comfort after them is not — what follows is craving and restlessness, not settlement.
* Real food and real rest are the house; BAD SUGAR is the gatecrasher that eats the host and then blames the house for feeling empty.
* Needing more to get the same brief note — needing the family size — is crowding and cue, not proof of love, and cue fires before the bite.
* The same evening, same warmth, same show without BAD SUGAR is warmer, clearer and freer — the pleasure was the moment, company and body, not the dose.
