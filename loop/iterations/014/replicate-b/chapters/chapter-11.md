Chapter 11
BUT I LOVE FOOD

IN THIS CHAPTER — The family table where everyone claims the same sweet tooth, the mirror that says it's character, the line of myths waiting at the kitchen door, a kitchen that proves freedom on an ordinary afternoon

*You were never a sugar person who then got trapped — being trapped is what made you feel like a sugar person.*

### THE STORY WE TELL AT THE TABLE

We have all said it at that table.

"We've always had a sweet tooth in our family." Mum, dad, sister, you — the same hand reaching for the same tin, the same laugh: "It's just who we are." The story is warm. It explains everything. It also excuses everything, which is why we love it.

I love food. I have always loved sweet things. It's my personality.

I hear you. And I want to be warm to you while we look straight at it, because this is the last excuse standing — and it is the gentlest trap of all.

We fell into BAD SUGAR the same way we all did — not because we were built to crave weaponized sweetness, but because we were fed it early, often, and with love. A child's birthday, the reward after school, the packet at child-eye height, the slogans that said treat yourself. The brainwashing did not ask your blood type. It did not check your genes. It simply told you, a hundred times a year for twenty years, that sweetness from a packet means love, reward, and comfort. Of course you ended up reaching.

That story at the table is not proof you were born this way. It is proof the household was sold the same lie.

This is the Sugar Trap talking, not your nature. And it is the Willpower Method that taught you to blame your nature when the method failed.

Let me ask you plainly, and you answer only with what you have lived:

If that sweet tooth were simply you — your character, your genes — why did you not crave BAD SUGAR before you were fed it? Why could you sit happily for months as a small child on real food — fruit, bread that was food, roast vegetables, milk — without negotiating for a fix?

If it were identity, why does the "I love food" feeling vanish after a proper meal of real food, and roar back only after a dose of BAD SUGAR?

And if it were who you are, why do people who never touch BAD SUGAR still love food — deeply, greedily, joyfully — without that wired, edgy hunt?

You know the answer. You have lived it. It is the other way around.

### THE TABLE PROVES IT BACKWARDS

Look at that family table again, but this time turn the arrow around.

Every person you call a "sugar person" shares the same cluster of traits: the grazing, the evening negotiation — "just a treat," "I've been good this week" — the 3pm slump that begs for sugary somethings to get me through, the "I can't stop once I start" after the first biscuit, the drawer that opens by itself. We call it a sweet tooth. We call it personality.

The trap calls it evidence.

Those shared traits are not the cause of the trap. They are the result of it. The behaviour creates the state it then pretends to explain.

We ate BAD SUGAR on an intermittent, restrict-binge schedule — light breakfast, try to be good, skip, then hit the packet at night. That schedule itself drives escalation. The same hit, repeated, stops giving the lift it once seemed to and starts needing a bigger hit to get the same brief relief — so now it is not one biscuit but the family-size bar. That "need the bigger size now" feeling has been looked at with brain imaging mostly in people with obesity or binge patterns — it is a way to make sense of tolerance and why cues glow brighter before the bite, not proof your brain is damaged or your will is broken. And those cues — the wrapper crackle, the smell by the till, the ad at the bus stop — can fire before you have tasted a crumb. The body learned to expect the hit. The thought arrives pre-labelled as desire, and we misread it as proof we love BAD SUGAR.

As you saw earlier, when large groups answer a validated questionnaire about highly palatable food, many say they recognise this loss-of-control pattern in themselves — it is common, it is self-reported, it does not make anyone diseased or defective, and it means you are not uniquely weak.

There is an honest disagreement you deserve to hear straight: scientists who work on this argue openly about words. Some say the animal binge work reflects the access pattern — intermittent access drives bingeing — not proof that sugar itself is addictive in the human sense. Others read the same behaviour as addiction-like. Both sides agree the bingeing happens and that a dopamine flicker is not the same as pleasure. I tell you because you do not need a label to be free. Whether you call it a trap or a habit or a brainwashing, the move is the same: the Big Sweet Lie fed the little whine and called it love.

So the family resemblance is real — and backwards. You do not binge because you are a sugar person. You feel like a sugar person because you were taught to binge. Remove the brainwashing and starve the little creature that whines, and the "personality" evaporates.

Shall we test it where you live?

Next time you feel true hunger — not the sudden I-need-to-eat-something-RIGHT-NOW feeling, but the slow, empty, honest hunger that arrives after you have been busy and have not eaten — notice what your body wants. It wants food. Real food. Water, fruit, vegetables, plain dairy, a warm plate that steadies you. Feed that hunger with real food and watch what happens: clean satisfaction, then done. No crash, no negotiation, no call for "something sweet." Now compare that to the whine that arrives an hour after a sweet hit — edgy, nagging, slightly needy — that points only at BAD SUGAR and promises energy while delivering tired, wired and crashing. One is hunger. The other is a parasite complaining.

You can love food without feeding the parasite. In fact, that is the only way to love it fully.

### I THOUGHT I WAS THE EXCEPTION — THEN I WAS FREE

I want to give you a life, not a lecture.

For seventeen years I told the sweet-tooth story about myself. At family dinners I was the one who "knew cakes." I baked. I hosted. I reached first. I said it with pride: I have always had a sweet tooth, it's who I am. I also had the 3pm drawer, the hidden wrappers in the office bin, the evening negotiation that always ended with the whole box gone after the "just one," and the morning promise that tomorrow I would be good. I tried every version of moderation — I don't keep it in the house, once a week is fine, dark only — and each rule made BAD SUGAR more precious and me more obsessed.

I did not quit because I suddenly loved health. I quit because I finally saw the con. I had been taught to call the trap my personality.

The first week was not a straight line. I will tell you honest, as you will live it. Days two to four the whine was louder — that empty, edgy, slightly needy feeling that old me called "I need a treat." I expected horror. It was not horror. It was mild and a bit restless, like a radio left on in the next room. I did not fight it. I named it — there is the little creature complaining — and I was glad it was complaining, because complaint means starvation. By day five the volume dropped. By day ten I walked past the supermarket aisle that used to be a canyon of bright packets at child height and felt nothing pull. Not strain. Nothing.

What changed was taste itself. An apple on day twelve was absurdly sweet — sweet enough to make me laugh. I ate it in the sun on the back step, not because I "should," but because I wanted it. A bowl of roast carrots and potatoes in the evening left me steady and clear, and I slept without the wired buzz. In three weeks I noticed my afternoons were not a roller coaster. I did not need sugary somethings to get me through. I was not counting. I was not resisting. I was just eating when hungry, stopping when satisfied, and getting on with enjoying my life. My partner still had biscuits in the tin for a while. I pitied the tin. I did not envy it.

There was a wobble on day eighteen — a birthday, a table of cakes, a well-meaning friend pushing a slice toward me with love in her eyes. For a second the old brainwashing flared: you will hurt her feelings, you will miss the moment. I remembered what you and I have already seen: the moment was the singing, the laughter, the warm room. BAD SUGAR was only sneaking a ride on it. I said no thank you, ate the fruit and cheese I wanted, and the moment stayed beautiful. No lie was needed to make it lovely.

No part of that needed me to stop loving food. I love food more now. GOOD SUGAR — the natural sweetness already inside whole fruit, vegetables, plain dairy eaten as part of real food — was never BAD SUGAR and never needed quitting. Real food is the house. BAD SUGAR was the gatecrasher who ate the host. When the gatecrasher left, the house was sweeter, not less.

If there was a restart button, it was not "try harder." It was understanding. Once I understood that the whine was not me and the treat was not a treat, there was nothing to resist. That is how well you feel when you are able to avoid sweet cravings — not tense, not deprived, but steady and relieved, as if a tight shoe has finally come off.

### THE MYTHS LINED UP

Here they are — the last whispers of the brainwashing, lined up so you can see them plain. You will hear these in your own voice. Let them come, and see.

*"Fruit will spike me too — so I might as well have the cake?"*
No. Fruit is food. It comes with water, fibre, chew, and a meal, and your body knows what to do with it. BAD SUGAR is engineered sweetness stripped from its food and weaponized to hit fast and call for another hit. Your body can make all the glucose it needs without you ever feeding it BAD SUGAR, and that does not mean you must live on any special diet.

*"I need sugar for my brain — it needs glucose to function?"*
Your brain needs glucose, not BAD SUGAR. Your body manufactures glucose from real food without a single sweet packet. The lift you call "brain fuel" is a spike-then-drop that leaves you less clear. You never rise above the steady baseline of someone free of BAD SUGAR.

*"Dark chocolate is healthy, so…?"*
A healthier poison in prettier paper is still the same con. If it is engineered around added or refined sugar and eaten as a fix, a lift, or a treat, it is BAD SUGAR. Health framing is the manufacturer's favourite costume. Food is not healthy because a label says so; you feel it in clear, steady energy, not in wired and crashing.

*"I have an addictive personality — some of us just are?"*
Look at the table. The shared traits you call personality — craving, grazing, loss-of-control after one — appear after the trap is installed and disappear when the trap is dead. They are results, not causes. Even brilliant, fierce people who call themselves addictive have walked out easily once they stopped blaming character and saw the con.

### EVEN IF — AND THIS IS WONDERFUL NEWS

Here is the graceful concession that closes the last door.

Even if — even if you did have a slightly more cue-sensitive, more sweet-responsive nature than the next person — this method still frees you.

Why? Because freedom here does not require you to become a different character. It requires you to stop feeding a belief. The belief — that BAD SUGAR is a genuine pleasure, treat or lift — is what gives the whine its meaning. Kill the belief and the whine starves. The little creature dies whether it was loud or quiet. The Big Sweet Lie dies whether it was shouted or whispered in your family for years.

You do not need to be fixed. You need to be free. And you do not need to argue with your genes to get there.

Would a different character have changed the truth that BAD SUGAR does nothing FOR you? Would it have made the tight shoe comfortable? Would it have made the rescue real when the rescuer is the one who pushed you in?

No. The trap is not your identity. The trap used your identity as its hiding place. Seen once, it cannot hide again.

And that is marvellous, because it means there is no exempt group. There is no "sugar person" who must remain a prisoner. There are only people who were conned — and people who have seen the con.

You have seen it.

Stay with me. In our next chapter we meet the most beautiful lie head-on — the celebration that feels like it would be grey without sweetness — and you will feel with your own eyes and hands that the beauty was never in the packet.

### SUMMARY

* The sweet-tooth identity is not the cause of the trap; it is the result of being trained on BAD SUGAR, and the family resemblance proves the training, not your nature.
* A bigger hit or a bigger bar is not proof you are broken; it is what happens when intermittent hits make each dose feel larger and cues fire before the bite.
* Honest disagreement about labels does not change your lived pattern, and that pattern is common and not a character defect.
* Real hunger wants real food and ends in steady satisfaction; the edgy whine wants only BAD SUGAR and ends in tired, wired and crashing.
* Even if you were more cue-sensitive than others, killing the belief still starves the whine — so no identity exempts you from freedom, and loving food grows once BAD SUGAR is gone.
