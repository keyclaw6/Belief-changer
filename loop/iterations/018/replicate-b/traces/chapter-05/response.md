Chapter 5
THE LIFT THAT STEALS

**IN THIS CHAPTER**
The fluorescent office at 3:07 p.m. — The drawer that opens by itself — Tight shoes worn all day — The friend who pushes you in and dives to save you

*BAD SUGAR never lifted you — it made you low so it could sell you the brief relief of feeling less low.*

### The Drawer That Opens By Itself

It is 3:07 p.m.

You are not deciding anything. The thought arrives before the sentence. The drawer slides — that soft wood rattle you know blindfolded — the packet rustles, the hand is inside. You tell yourself you are choosing. You are not. The packet is choosing you.

First bite. A flash. Shoulders lift half an inch, the fog clears for maybe ninety seconds. You think, there, that is energy, that is what I needed to function.

Watch what happens next with me, without looking away.

Ten minutes later the shoulders sag again, a touch lower than before. The mouth is sweet and dry. The want is back, a little sharper than it was at three. The hand is back in the packet before you have finished chewing the story that this is fuel.

I lived in that drawer for years. We all did. We learned to read that sag as proof we needed another hit. We were proud of how we powered through, and we were afraid of the flatness we imagined waiting if we did not feed the drawer.

The fact the drawer opens by itself is not proof you need what is inside it. It is proof the drawer has been taught to open.

You know that empty, edgy, slightly needy feeling that arrives right there at three o'clock? That faint itch behind the eyes, the inability to settle, the I-need-something-RIGHT-NOW tug? That is not you needing energy. That is the little creature we named earlier starting to whine because its last dose is fading. It is the Sugar Worm stirring. It asks for the one thing that will keep it alive a little longer, and we were taught to hear its whine as our own wise body asking for fuel.

It never was.

### What You Called Fuel Was The Fall

Let me take your own body as the only laboratory we need.

You have said to yourself, many times, “It gives me energy. I need sugar to function. It helps me concentrate.” I said it too. It felt true in the only way that matters — you ate something quick and sweet and you did feel a lift.

And then? In your own words, the pattern you have lived a thousand times:

“I would have something quick and sweet but now know that will only give a short term lift which will inevitably be followed by a fast drop.”

That is not my phrase. That is your lived report. The lift is real and it is short. The drop is inevitable and it is fast. The “fuel” is a spike-then-drop misread as genuine energy.

So let me ask you plainly, with the drawer still open in your mind:

Did you feel higher after the biscuit than you felt at ten in the morning before you had needed anything at all?

If BAD SUGAR truly gave you energy, why did you need another dose an hour later, and why was the second dose less bright than the first?

Would a person who had never fed BAD SUGAR that day have felt that 3 p.m. dip at all, or would they have simply stayed steady?

Only one set of honest answers fits. You never rose above the steady baseline of someone free of BAD SUGAR. You were lifted out of a hole that the last dose had dug, just enough to fall back in. What you called energy was relief. What you called relief was the drug ending the discomfort it had created.

This is the loop the body lets us see if we watch it without the story. BAD SUGAR hits the blood fast. The body, which is marvellously competent, releases insulin to do its job and clear the surge. That clean-up can overshoot and leave you feeling lower, shakier, hungrier than before the bite — a real after-dip mechanism that explains why the need-to-eat-now feeling follows sweetness. And — this matters, so hear it cleanly — this is not low blood sugar as doctors define it. Everyday slumps are not clinical hypoglycemia. That diagnosis is rare and contested in healthy people. The crash you feel after BAD SUGAR is common and it is not a disease. It is the trap’s hangover.

Do you see what the trap did? It created the low, then sold you the cure for the low it created, then called itself energy.

There is a second fact that finishes the trick. You do not need to import BAD SUGAR to make glucose. Your body manufactures the glucose it needs from real food — from protein and the ordinary work of your liver. Your brain does not starve when you stop feeding BAD SUGAR. There is no deprivation here. I am not prescribing any diet, and you do not need a special regimen to keep your energy up. Steady comes back on its own when the spike-and-crash stops.

We were brainwashed to read the crash as a call for more of the same. The crash is the bill. The need for more is the debt talking.

### Tight Shoes

There is an image I want you to wear for a minute so you never lose this.

Imagine you decide, for no reason, to put on a pair of shoes two sizes too small and wear them all morning. By noon your feet throb. By three your whole body is tight with it. Then, at 3:07, you sit at your desk and, with enormous relief, you kick the shoes off.

Would you call that relief a genuine pleasure? Would you tell your friends the shoes give you energy? Would you fear life without the shoes because you would miss that wonderful moment of taking them off?

Of course not. You would see the game instantly: the shoes first create the pain and then sell you the ending of the pain as if it were a gift.

BAD SUGAR is tight shoes for your blood and your nerves. It pinches, then sells you the loosening. The lift you feel at the first bite is not you rising above normal. It is you briefly returning toward normal — toward the clear, steady energy you would have had all day if you had not put the shoes on in the first place — before the pinch starts again.

Once you see it, you cannot unsee it. Every time you thought BAD SUGAR helped you concentrate, it had first scattered you. Every time it helped you function, it had first pulled the floor out from under function.

This is why willpower logic keeps failing. Tight shoes do not need to be managed, cut down, or negotiated with. They need to be taken off. You do not need to be stronger. You need to see that there was never a benefit to keep.

And here is the cost the shoes charge daily, in the words we will use from now on to name the addict’s permanent state. When you live on BAD SUGAR you are not energetic and then tired. You are tired, wired and crashing — all at once. Tired because the steady supply is gone. Wired because the next spike is buzzing your nerves. Crashing because the last spike is already falling out from under you. That triple is not your personality. It is not age. It is the shoes. Take them off and the baseline is not flatness. The baseline is clear.

### The Friend Who Pushes You

Stay with that inversion for one more turn, because this is where the feeling of being fooled lifts and becomes freedom.

You are standing by a river with a friend. Without warning he shoves you in, clothes and all. You sputter, you flail, you go cold with panic. Then the same friend dives in, grabs your arm, and hauls you to the bank. He wraps his jacket round you and you feel immense gratitude. You might even call him your rescuer.

Unless — with a chill — you remember he pushed you.

BAD SUGAR is that friend. It pushes you in — the spike, the insulin surge, the fast drop, that empty, edgy, slightly needy feeling — and then, ten minutes later, it dives in with a biscuit and plays the rescuer. We praised the rescue for years. We said it gave us energy, focus, life. The whole time the rescue was the perpetrator returning to the scene.

Let that land not as fear but as relief.

If energy came from BAD SUGAR, then stopping would mean losing energy. If energy was stolen by BAD SUGAR and only briefly returned as relief, then stopping means gaining energy back and keeping it. Nothing is taken from you when you stop feeding the Worm. The theft stops.

Do you want to keep renting back, for ninety seconds at a time, the steadiness that is already yours for free?

If the lift were real, why would it leave you lower each time?

If the crash proves need, why does the person who never took the first dose not crash at all?

You know the honest answers because you have lived them. The lift steals.

You do not need to fear flatness. Flatness was the condition of the addict, not the condition of the free. The free are not flat. They are steady, clear, and done chasing. When you stop putting on the tight shoes, you do not lose a pleasure. You lose a pinch. When you stop letting the friend push you in, you do not lose a rescuer. You lose a bully.

Stay right there. Notice the inversion sit in your own afternoon body. The 3 p.m. drawer does not prove BAD SUGAR helps you function. It proves BAD SUGAR has been interrupting your function and then briefly relieving the interruption. You were never rising above normal. You were dipping below it and being charged for the ladder back to where you started.

There is nothing to mourn here. There is something to rejoice about: the energy you thought came from BAD SUGAR was always yours. It comes back the moment you stop letting BAD SUGAR steal it.

### SUMMARY

- What I called energy from BAD SUGAR was relief from a low that BAD SUGAR created — a short lift inevitably followed by a fast drop, not genuine fuel.
- The common after-dip in mood and hunger is a real effect of the sugar surge and its clean-up, but everyday slumps are not clinical low blood sugar.
- The body makes the glucose it needs from real food, so stopping BAD SUGAR does not starve the brain or require a special diet.
- Life on BAD SUGAR is tired, wired and crashing — tight shoes that pinch then sell you the relief — and steady, clear energy returns when the theft stops.
