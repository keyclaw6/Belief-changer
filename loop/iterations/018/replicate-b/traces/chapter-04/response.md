Chapter 4
THE TREAT THAT TAKES

**IN THIS CHAPTER**
The kitchen after school — A mother’s hand with sweets — The 8 p.m. sofa after a hard day — The house party where the gatecrasher eats the host

*That treat never loved you back — the love was the moment itself, and BAD SUGAR was only sneaking a ride.*

### The Kindness We Were Taught To Taste

I know how tender this one is.

For most of us, BAD SUGAR did not enter as a chemical. It entered as kindness. A hand on your head after school. A sweet slipped into your palm that said you were seen, you were good, you mattered. The wrapper was love in miniature. You learned the equation before you could spell it: sweetness equals care.

Then we grew up and kept the equation and hired ourselves to carry it out. The adult version is quieter and more tired. Eight p.m. The bag on the floor, the email finally closed, the kitchen cleaned just enough. You are not hungry. You are wrung out and you whisper the sentence we have all whispered to ourselves.

“celebrating, rewarding myself, going to the cinema, etc with chocolate.”

“It’s a treat. I deserve it. It’s how I reward myself.”

I said it too, for years, and I meant it kindly. When life has taken from you all day — boss, traffic, bills, children needing one more thing — of course you want something that gives back. It feels cruel to question it. I am not questioning your need for kindness. I am questioning who actually gave it to you.

We were taught to call BAD SUGAR a genuine pleasure, treat or lift. That phrase is the whole disguise. It tells you the hit on the tongue is the reward, the comfort, the little love affair you are owed. If that were true, taking it away would be deprivation. If it is false, you are not losing kindness when you stop. You are getting it back clean.

Let me ask you as plainly as I can, while you are still holding that 8 p.m. feeling in your mind.

When you ate that treat after the hard day, did you feel loved *because* the sugar loved you — or because you had finally stopped, sat down, and let the day end?

If the same sofa, the same quiet room, the same pause and the same kindness toward yourself had happened with a cup of tea and real rest or real company and no BAD SUGAR at all — would the warmth have been less, or would it have been the same warmth without the sticky aftertaste?

And was the shame or the nagging for more that often followed in the next ten minutes part of a genuine treat at all?

Let the honest answers sit there. The warmth was real. The cause was not.

### The House Party

Let me show you what actually happened in that room, because once you see it you cannot unsee it again.

Picture real food as a house. Not a lecture about nutrition. A house you live in. The kitchen is warm, the table is set. There is fruit that snaps, bread that is food, a soup that steams, roast vegetables that fill the room. When you are truly hungry you enter the house, you eat, you are satisfied, you leave the table and get on with your life. The house does not follow you down the hall whispering for more. It does what a house is meant to do: shelter you, then let you go.

BAD SUGAR is not the house. BAD SUGAR is the gatecrasher.

Plain carbs are a bit lonely — they hang by the door and they invite company over. That company is BAD SUGAR. It comes in loud, in bright paper, smelling sweeter than anything in the pantry. It does not knock. It gatecrashes the party, turns up the music, spills itself over the host, and then eats the host. By the second plate you are no longer tasting food. You are feeding the gatecrasher. The house is still there, but you cannot hear it.

That is why we say we love food and then feel we cannot stop once we start. We are not speaking about food. We are speaking about the gatecrasher who arrived with food and took over its name. The “treat” is not the house being kind. It is the gatecrasher convincing you the noise is the party.

You have felt the proof of this without having the words for it. Think of a meal that was real food — not a packet of engineered sweetness — eaten when you were truly hungry. Remember how it began, how it satisfied, how it finished. There was a natural end. No negotiation. No hand back in the packet. No voice saying one more will finally feel like enough.

Now think of BAD SUGAR on that same hungry evening. The first bite promised the same satisfaction and delivered a different instruction: more. The second bite was less bright than the first. The third less bright again. Soon you were chasing a feeling that was getting weaker while the urge was getting stronger. Many of us know the quiet escalation — “why am I not enjoying it like before, I need the family size now.” The circuit gets crowded and blunt with repetition, so a bigger dose feels like it is needed to reach what a small one once brushed. That pattern is consistent with what is seen in the reward system — it fits the lived experience of tolerance — but pictures of the brain do not prove you are compelled or broken. They only help explain why the treat takes more each time and gives less.

That is the con inside the Sugar Trap. It sells itself as a genuine pleasure, treat or lift and then, hit by hit, it makes pleasure harder to feel. The borrowing never repays.

### What Was Love And What Was The Ride

Stay on that 8 p.m. sofa with me and replay the evening twice, like a film you run back to back.

First run: you, the hard day, the sofa, the quiet at last, the sense that you have survived something, the BAD SUGAR in your hand. Notice what actually warms you. The stopping. The sitting. The exhale. The permission to be done. The sweetness is there for twenty seconds on the tongue, then it is gone, and what lingers is not warmth but a faint edginess, a calculation about whether there is more, a little film on the teeth.

Second run: the same you, the same hard day, the same sofa, the same quiet at last, the same survival — no BAD SUGAR. Maybe a piece of fruit because you like it, maybe just the rest and the quiet, maybe laughter with someone, maybe nothing at all except being done. Notice what remains. The stopping is still there. The sitting is still there. The exhale is still there. The kindness toward yourself is still there. The edginess and the calculation are not.

Did BAD SUGAR make that moment loving — or did the moment make BAD SUGAR seem loving while it took the credit?

This is how the Sugar Trap keeps its reward story alive. It waits until life gives you a real good — rest, company, celebration, relief that something hard is over — and then it slips into the frame at the exact moment the good arrives and says, I did that. We were all conned that way. We. Not you alone. Every advert, every film, every birthday ritual taught us to stand next to love and take the photograph as if we were love.

You do not need to moralize sweetness to see this. GOOD SUGAR — the natural sweetness already inside whole fruit, vegetables, plain dairy as part of real food — was never the thief. It lives in the house. It does not gatecrash. You are not here to quit sweetness. You are here to evict the gatecrasher who convinced you he was sweetness.

If BAD SUGAR were a friend, its friendship would be easy to judge. A friend who arrives only when you are low, who makes the low deeper so his hello feels brighter, who asks for more each time and leaves you more restless than before, is not a treat. A friend who takes the credit for your own courage and your loved ones’ company is not love.

It was never love. It was theft.

You were the kindness in that room. The day was hard and you were kind to stay with it. The sofa was kind to hold you. The people you love were kind to be themselves. BAD SUGAR was only ever sneaking a ride on the warmth you and your life had already made.

Feel what that reassigning does. Nothing is taken from you. Everything is returned to its owner — the moment, the body, the people. BAD SUGAR did not give you comfort; it charged you for the comfort you had already earned and left you needing to pay again.

Keep this one verdict clean and do not re-argue it later. The reward was not in the packet. The packet was in the reward.

### SUMMARY

- What I called a reward was not BAD SUGAR being kind; it was my life being kind — rest, relief, company — and BAD SUGAR sneaking a ride on that kindness.
- Real food satisfies and finishes; BAD SUGAR gatecrashes, makes the same hit less enjoyable next time, and asks for more, which explains why the family size replaces the small bar.
- The warmth after a hard day came from stopping and being done, not from the dose; the same evening without BAD SUGAR keeps the warmth and loses the after-edginess.
- The treat that sold itself as love delivered craving and after-effects, not love, and seeing that leaves the love intact — freer and cleaner without the packet.
