Chapter 6
EATING LIKE WE WERE BUILT TO

**IN THIS CHAPTER**
The kitchen at noon light — A bowl of fruit that smells like itself — True hunger arriving like a tide — The first slow mouthfuls — Clean satisfaction instead of a crash

*Eating without BAD SUGAR is not bland control — it is the return of real hunger, real taste, and a clean finish that feels like enough.*

### The Fear That Freedom Will Be Bland

You have done the hard intellectual work.

You have seen the treat for what it was. You have felt the lift steal in your own 3 p.m. body. Part of you is saying, yes, I see it — BAD SUGAR was not giving me anything.

And another part is whispering the last quiet worry. Not about pleasure. Not about energy. About food itself.

"If I stop eating BAD SUGAR, what is left? Is eating now going to be control? Measuring? Chewing joylessly while everyone else enjoys? Will I spend the rest of my life being careful?"

I know that whisper. We all heard it in the trap.

We were taught that eating is a problem to be managed and BAD SUGAR is the fun part. So when you imagine life without BAD SUGAR, you picture the management without the fun. A plate with the colour taken out. Discipline where delight used to be.

The fact is, that picture is backwards. It is the trap talking again, and it has gotten this completely the wrong way round.

I want to show you something you already know in your bones but have not had the chance to feel cleanly for a long while. Not an argument. A meal.

We are not going to talk about eating. We are going to eat like we were built to eat — once, slowly, with attention — and let your body make the case.

### True Hunger Is Not That Empty, Edgy Tug

We have forgotten what real hunger feels like because BAD SUGAR kept interrupting it.

Real hunger is not sudden. It is not panicky. It does not arrive at 3:07 with an I-need-something-RIGHT-NOW snap of the fingers. It does not make you stare at the drawer.

Real hunger is quiet, and it builds. It starts as a little lightness. An emptiness that is clean, not edgy. A thought: food would be nice. Then, a little later, the mouth waters. The belly gives a soft hollow knock. You notice smells more. You start to imagine eating with pleasure.

That is not the Worm. That is you. That is your body calling you to the table in the voice it was given before anyone ever sold you a packet.

We learned to misread that empty, edgy, slightly needy feeling as hunger. It is not hunger. It is a fading dose. Real hunger does not make you wired. Real hunger does not crash you. Real hunger makes you steady and turns food delicious.

Have you noticed how a child who has not been trained on BAD SUGAR behaves? She plays. She runs. Then she stops and says, "I'm hungry." She eats what is there — bread, soup, an apple, cheese — with total concentration. She chews. She sighs. She stops. She says "I'm full" and runs off again. She does not negotiate. She does not bargain for one more bite to get the right feeling. She does not hang around the empty packet feeling tired, wired and crashing.

We were that child once.

Your body has not forgotten how to do this. It has only been shouted over. Today we are going to let it speak without interruption.

I lived for years thinking I loved food but spending every meal half somewhere else — half counting, half craving, half waiting for the sweet at the end that would tell me the meal was over. That is not loving food. That is being kept from it.

You are not about to learn a new way to eat. You are about to remember the old one.

### The Ordinary Meal

Let me sit you down at an ordinary table. No ceremony. No health-food performance. Just real food that is food, not confection.

It is midday or evening. You have left enough space since the last time you ate that true hunger has had time to arrive. You are not starving. You are ready.

On the table is something simple. Perhaps a bowl of roast vegetables still warm from the oven, glossed with olive oil, edges crisp. A slice of real bread — bread that is bread, not cake that pretends to be bread — with butter that smells like cream. A piece of fruit that smells like itself — a peach, or an orange you have to peel, that sprays sweet oil on your fingers. Nothing engineered around BAD SUGAR. Nothing designed in a lab to make you unable to stop once you start.

Do this with me now, in your imagination, as if your hands were there.

You lift the first mouthful. You have waited, so you are hungry. You notice.

The first taste is brighter than you expected. Real food tastes more when you are truly hungry. The salt, the roast sweetness of the carrot, the sour snap of the fruit — it is not loud like BAD SUGAR. It is clear. You can taste the edges.

You chew slowly. Not because you are being virtuous. Because chewing is where taste lives. BAD SUGAR taught you to not-chew — to pour, to crunch, to swallow. Real food asks to be chewed, and your body answers. The flavour changes as you chew. That is the pleasure. It is not a hit. It is a unfolding.

Notice what is happening in your body while you chew. Your shoulders are down. Your breathing is easy. There is no buzz. There is no sag. There is a gentle, growing warmth that says, this is being received.

Take another mouthful. And another. Let yourself enjoy it without commentary. You do not need to decide if this is enough. Your body is deciding for you, as it always knew how.

A few minutes in, something wonderful happens that BAD SUGAR never allowed. You feel satisfaction begin to arrive, not as a spike but as a tide. It is not excitement. It is deeper than excitement. It is the feeling of enough coming into view. The hunger that called you is being answered. The taste that was bright stays bright — it does not go dead the way the third biscuit always did. Real food does not need a family size to still be liked. It is liked at the first bite and it is liked at the tenth.

And then, without a crash, without a negotiation, it finishes. Cleanly.

You put your fork down because you want to. Not because you are stopping yourself. You are not full in the stuffed sense. You are satisfied in the steady sense. The mouth is clean. The tongue is not coated in that sickly sweet film. The belly is calm. The mind is clear and done thinking about food.

Have you felt that finish before?

When was the last time a sweet left you feeling cleanly finished?

If BAD SUGAR were pleasure, why did it never finish cleanly, and why does this — this ordinary fruit, these ordinary vegetables, this ordinary bread — leave you feeling done and content without a single thought of needing more?

You know the answer because you just lived it.

This is not a better kind of control. This is not control at all. This is appetite working as it was built to work: hunger that arrives, taste that delights, satisfaction that closes the meal. The enjoyment is not added on top of eating. The enjoyment is eating, when nothing is interfering with it.

There is a simple truth to lodge here, spoken plainly. Your body makes the glucose it needs from real food — from protein and from its own quiet work — so there is no deprivation in leaving BAD SUGAR out. I am not prescribing any diet and you need no special plan to keep steady. That steady sweetness after a real meal — the clear taste in the mouth, the even energy in the limbs — is your own. It was always yours.

Real food is the house. BAD SUGAR was the gatecrasher that barged in, ate the host's dinner, and told you the party was his.

You have just been back in the house.

### Enough Is A Feeling, Not A Rule

We were brainwashed to believe that without BAD SUGAR we would need rules to protect us from ourselves. Portions. Points. Permissions. A life of careful arithmetic.

Look at what you just felt.

Did you need a rule to tell you when hunger had arrived? No. You felt it.

Did you need a rule to tell you how much to chew? No. Taste told you.

Did you need a rule to tell you when you had had enough? No. Satisfaction told you, cleanly and early, the way it tells every creature that eats real food.

Enough is not a number. Enough is a feeling. It is the feeling of a meal that satisfies and ends. BAD SUGAR robbed you of that feeling in two ways: it shouted over hunger with false alarm, and it prevented satisfaction from ever arriving because the hit always demanded another hit. We blamed ourselves for having no stopping place. The stopping place had been removed.

Do you see why freedom here is not bland?

Bland is what BAD SUGAR made eating. The same sweet hit, packet after packet, until taste was dulled and you needed more to feel less. Real food is the opposite of bland. Real food is various. It is crunchy and soft, salty and sweet in the natural way fruit is sweet, warm and cool, chewy and melting. It leaves your palate alive, not coated. It leaves your energy steady, not wired then fallen.

I promise you this, not as a sales line but as a settled fact about bodies: when the spike-and-crash stops, appetite normalises of its own accord, and it does so easily, immediately and permanently. Not because you will control it. Because there is nothing left to interfere with it. Your body does this work for you. You do not need to learn to like satisfaction. You need only stop preventing it.

You do not have to do anything else in your life to make this happen. You do not have to change where you shop or whom you dine with or how you live. You change nothing else. You simply stop feeding BAD SUGAR and get on with enjoying your life — including the enormous, ordinary pleasure of being hungry, eating, and feeling done.

Think for a moment of tomorrow morning if you let this stand.

You wake. You are not thinking about BAD SUGAR first thing. You are a little hungry — that clean, light, ready feeling. You eat something real. You taste it. You go about your morning steady. At midday you are hungry again, clearly. You eat again, slowly enough to taste it. You finish feeling clear, not sleepy and not searching. In the shop you pass the bright packets at child-eye height and you see them for what they are — engineered weapons of sweetness in pretty paper — and you feel nothing envious, only relief that you are not carrying them home to do the gatecrasher's work on your table.

That day is not disciplined. It is delightful. It is not controlled. It is natural. It does not need effort because it is what your body was built to find wonderful.

You were never giving up sweetness. You were being robbed of it. The sweetness that belongs to you — the sweetness of a ripe peach, of roast onion caramelised at the edges, of bread that smells like wheat — returns when the chemical shout stops drowning it.

Let that settle as a piece of good news, not a task.

You already know how to eat like this. You did it as a child. You have just done it again in your imagination, and your body recognised it instantly because it never forgot.

Hold that ordinary, marvellous finish in your mind — that clean, steady, enough feeling. That is freedom's flavour. That is what you are returning to. Not a diet. Not a renunciation. A reclaimed delight that was yours before the trap taught you eating was a problem and BAD SUGAR was the fun part.

It was the other way around.

### SUMMARY

- Eating without BAD SUGAR is not bland control; it is the return of true hunger, real taste, and clean satisfaction.
- Real hunger builds quietly and makes food delicious, while that empty, edgy need is not hunger but a fading dose.
- A slow ordinary meal of real food satisfies and finishes cleanly, leaving steady energy and a clear palate instead of a crash.
- The body makes the glucose it needs from real food, so leaving out BAD SUGAR is not deprivation and needs no special diet.
- Enough is a natural feeling of satisfaction that returns on its own when BAD SUGAR stops blocking it.
