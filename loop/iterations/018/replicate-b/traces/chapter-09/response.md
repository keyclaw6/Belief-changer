Chapter 9
THE BODY KEEPS THE SCORE

**IN THIS CHAPTER**
The dentist's mirror — The cuff at three o'clock — The quiet scale in the bathroom — The mood after the treat — Plenty TO you, nothing FOR you

*BAD SUGAR is already doing plenty TO you and nothing FOR you — and seeing that clearly is relief, not fear.*

### You Feel Fine While The Score Adds Up

You are indignant now and rightly so. You have seen the canyon, you have seen who built it, you have seen the schedule that teaches a binge and the cue that glows before the bite. That indignation is dignity returning. It is warm and clean. Keep it.

And now I am going to show you what the trap has been charging you while you were told you were enjoying it.

We like to say we feel fine. We say it sincerely, because we do — in the way the water feels fine when it warms one degree at a time. That is the little token we can borrow from the frog: the heat rises so slowly you never jump until someone points to the thermometer. You did not feel the score adding up because the trap was designed to be slow.

Let me point, gently and flatly, to three ordinary places where the score already shows.

Not in a lab. Not in a headline. In your own bathroom and chair.

Open the bathroom cabinet. The toothbrush sits there. The scale sits there. In the clinic a cuff tightens at three in the afternoon — the same hour the drawer used to slide open. These are not lectures. They are mirrors. And what they reflect is not weak character. It is what an engineered sweetness does TO a body when it is fed every day.

You may hear a small voice as we look: "Steady on — I feel fine, my teeth are okay, this is a bit much."

I hear it. We all said it. Let us answer it together, with what is flatly true, and then let the relief do its work — because I do not want you quitting out of fright. I want you quitting out of relief. The facts make freedom exciting; they are not a sentence.

The fact is… BAD SUGAR never gave you energy, calm or comfort — it only billed you later and sold you the bill as proof you needed it.

Hold that while we look in the mirror.

### The Mirror

Lean back in that chair and open your mouth.

The dentist lifts the mirror. You see a small brown pit, or an old filling, or gums that bleed when pressed. Nothing dramatic. Just ordinary damage in an ordinary mouth in a country where sweets are ordinary.

Here is the plain mechanism, without scare and without hedge: free sugars feed plaque acid that dissolves teeth. The plaque eats the sugar, makes acid, the acid rots the surface. That is what BAD SUGAR does TO teeth. Not in one bite. Not as a guarantee after one sweet. As a linked, population-wide pattern that is so settled it sits in healthy-diet guidance alongside everything else: brush, fluoride, and less free sugar. Other things matter — fluoride, brushing, saliva — but the fuel for the acid is the sugar.

We made this convenient to forget. The packet is pretty paper; the tooth is hidden behind a lip. The con needed that distance. If the packet left a stain on your front teeth the instant you opened it, you would never have called it a treat.

Ask yourself, straight:

If BAD SUGAR were doing something FOR your mouth, would a professional need a drill to undo what it did TO it?

Would you call anything else that quietly rots the only teeth you will ever grow a genuine pleasure, treat or lift?

You know the answer in your own mouth. The pleasure was the film and the company and the moment — the wrapper was only sneaking a ride. The tooth keeps the score the moment forgot.

I say that at full force and then I set fear down. I do not want you brushing in a panic or counting cavities like a sentence. I want you feeling the simple relief that you never need to fund that acid again. The mirror is not a judgment. It is a receipt. And you are free to stop paying.

### The Cuff And The Scale

Now the afternoon cuff.

At three o'clock you used to feel that familiar sag — the shoulders, the eyes, the hand back in the packet for a lift. We called that low energy. We fed it a hit and called the brief lessening energy. That is the whole trick.

Across populations, high added-sugar intake is linked with higher heart and stroke risk — not because one dose stops a heart, but because a high, steady intake loads the liver, nudges blood pressure up, keeps inflammation humming, and quietly bypasses the body's normal appetite control. The lift you felt was not free. It was deferred load. The body took the hit on credit and sent the bill to the vessels, the liver, the pressure gauge.

The scale tells the same story another way. Societal intake sits far above the population guidance that sets free sugars at less than ten percent of energy — about fifty grams — with extra benefit below five percent. We do not live a little above that line. We live far above it, every day, because the canyon made it normal. Your body did not ask for that load. The canyon asked for it on your behalf.

Again, flat and fair: this is an observed link at population level, not a prophecy that one sweet causes heart disease or that stopping guarantees you will never be ill. Bodies are complex, and many things shape the heart. — if you live with diabetes, take medicines that lower blood sugar, or are pregnant, speak to your clinician before changing what you eat — I say that once, plainly, because this is about belief, not a prescription.

Now the questions that close the excuse:

If BAD SUGAR were fuel, would the body need to raise pressure to cope with it?

If it were steady energy, would the scale and the cuff be trending the wrong way while you feel more tired, wired and crashing, not less?

That last phrase is not my invention. It is your own honest report, in your own words, of what the trap feels like day after day when the hits get more frequent and less satisfying. Tired, wired and crashing is not a personality. It is the score.

And here is the cliff-jump token I promised to leave with you, because moderation talk returns right here: "you can jump off the cliff as long as you don't fall more than a few metres." That is what "just a little BAD SUGAR is fine for the heart" means. There is no little safe fall when the pattern itself is the problem. The packet that looks small still teaches the circuit that the hit matters, and the cue glows brighter next time.

Feel the fact, then feel the relief. The fact is heavy; the relief is heavier. You do not need to earn health through fear. You only need to see that the lift was never a lift — it was a loan — and you are free to stop borrowing.

### The Mood After The Treat

This one is tender, so I will be tender and exact.

We told ourselves BAD SUGAR helps mood. A treat when you feel you deserve one. A sweet something to get me through. Celebrating, rewarding myself, going to the cinema with chocolate — and for ten minutes it does feel like kindness, because the needy edge eases when you feed it.

In long-term cohorts that followed men, those who took more sugar from sweets were more likely to show common mental distress later. The treat did not predict comfort later; it predicted lower mood later. That is the direction the data points when people are followed for years.

Hear the limits as clearly as the point, because honesty is what makes belief stay dead. That finding is observational, in men, from sweets as the source, and it does not prove that sugar causes depression in everyone, or that stopping cures anything on its own. Mood has many parents. What it does show, cleanly, is that the story "sugar comforts me, so it is kind to my mind" does not survive follow-up. In the group where it was tested, higher sweet-sugar predicted worse mood, not relief.

We misread that wholly. We fed the Worm to quiet that empty, edgy, slightly needy feeling, the Lie called the quiet "proof it helps," and we therefore credited the rescuer who was also the perpetrator. That is the same inversion you felt at three in the afternoon, only now it is about the heart in another sense.

Ask it plainly:

If BAD SUGAR were comfort, would those who ate more sweets be more comforted months later?

If it were kindness, why does the evening after the treat so often end a little more edgy and empty, not more settled?

The credit belongs elsewhere. Company comforts. Rest comforts. Real food satisfies. The sweet only relieved a low the last sweet created, and then ensured the low would return. You were never being kind to yourself with BAD SUGAR. You were paying the trap to pause the discomfort the trap had given you.

I land that without leaving you in dread. I do not want you quitting because you are frightened of feeling low for the rest of your life. I want you quitting because you see you were made to feel low while you were still consuming it — tired, wired and crashing while you were still buying it — and now you can be free for the rest of your life instead. Two futures use the same words. One is a threat the trap made: trapped for the rest of your life, needing a dose to feel normal. One is the promise that is now true: free for the rest of your life, with nothing to pay to feel normal. Choose the second with relief, not fear. The body keeps the score, yes — and the moment you stop feeding the score, the body begins to keep a different one.

### Plenty TO You, Nothing FOR You

Let us gather the mirrors.

The mouth keeps the score in acid and decay. The vessels keep the score in load and pressure. The mood keeps the score not in comfort later but in less of it. The guidance line — less than ten percent, about fifty grams, extra benefit below five — sits far below where the canyon taught us to live, not because you failed but because the norm was engineered far above it. High intake is linked with harm across populations; no single hit writes your fate, and no single abstention writes a guarantee. That is the honest shape of it.

We have fed BAD SUGAR for years thinking it did something FOR us — lifted, soothed, rewarded. The body, faithfully and without drama, reports what it did TO us. Plenty TO you. Nothing FOR you.

Do you see how the two halves of that old confusion — "is it bad?" and "but is it good for me?" — collapse into one clear verdict?

When something is doing plenty TO you and nothing FOR you, the only honest name for it is a con.

There is no secret benefit hiding in the packet that outweighs acid on teeth, load on the heart, and lower mood later. There never was. The film was lovely, the company was warm, the real food was delicious — BAD SUGAR only sneaked a ride and made you thank it for the ride while it charged you for the ticket.

I say all of that at full Carr force, as you would want me to, and then I tell you what I always tell you after a hard truth: do not quit out of fear.

Fear makes a bad master. It makes you white-knuckle and watch the clock. Freedom makes a good one. You are not escaping because you are frightened of what might happen if you stay. You are escaping because you have seen there is nothing to stay for. The dessert did not love you back. The lift did not lift. The comfort did not comfort later. Once that is seen, there is nothing to miss and nothing to resist.

We fell into this together — we all did — learning to call warning lights weakness. You will walk out differently. When the dentist's mirror glints, you will feel relief, not shame, that you no longer fund that acid. When the cuff tightens, you will feel glad the deferred bill has stopped growing. When the evening tries to sell you kindness in pretty paper, you will smile because you know where kindness really comes from.

You were taught to feel fine while the water warmed. You are not fine in the trap — you are tired, wired and crashing in it — and you are marvellously, steadily fine the moment you step out of it. That is the relief this chapter was built to hand you.

Hold it. Enjoy it. We have one more villain to bury — the method that told you escaping would be hard — and then the way is clear.
