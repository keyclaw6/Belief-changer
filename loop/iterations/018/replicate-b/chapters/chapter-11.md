Chapter 11
BUT I LOVE FOOD

**IN THIS CHAPTER**
The family table where the sweet tooth story gets told — The traits table turned inside out — Three myths lined up on the counter — One kitchen, one drawer, and one ordinary escape

*You were not born a sugar person — the trap taught you to feel like one, and that feeling leaves when the lie does.*

### The Story We Tell At The Table

We are at the family table, and I can hear the voice already because I said it myself for years.

"We've always had a sweet tooth in our family." 
"I'm just a foodie — I love food too much to stop."
"It's who I am. My personality needs something sweet."

It is said warmly, almost proudly, with a smile. The children hear it. Grandmother nods. Someone pushes the biscuits forward as if they were proof of identity, and everyone laughs because it feels true and kind and harmless to believe.

I understand why you want this story to be true. After everything we have already seen together — the con of the treat, the theft of the lift — this last little room feels like a safe place to keep a private exemption. If sugar is about who you are, then you are not trapped. You are just being yourself. No one can argue with who you are.

And if it is in your genes, in your blood, in your nature, then no book and no promise can touch it. You get to stop investigating. The pressure is off.

That is why we must look here, together, without shaming you for wanting shelter. I am warm to you, merciless to the con that sold you this shelter.

The fact is… you were not born inside the Sugar Trap. You were led into it, and the feeling you call "my sweet tooth" arrived after the trap was already feeding you.

Think of the shape of your own life for a moment, honestly.

Did you decide, at some thoughtful age, after comparing lives, "I shall be a person who needs sugar to feel like myself"? Or did you eat BAD SUGAR as a child because it was there, bright and praised and given as love, and only later start to describe yourself as someone who loves it? At what stage did you decide you needed it? The fact is, we never thought it over when we found it.

We did not choose our identity and then find sugar to fit it. We found sugar, often before we could choose anything, and then built an identity around the discomfort it left behind.

That empty, edgy, slightly needy feeling that hums between hits — you know it well now — that is not personality. It is withdrawal re-labelled as character. The Big Sweet Lie does that re-labelling brilliantly. It takes the Sugar Worm's whine and translates it: "This is just how you are."

Is that who you are, or is that what the trap made you feel after it was fed?

You know the honest answer, because you have lived both sides. On days you never fed BAD SUGAR — a long walk, a busy morning, flu — you were still you. You still loved food. You still laughed at the table. You were not flat and joyless without it. The table was still the table.

So what did BAD SUGAR ever give that table except the brief interruption of taste and the longer aftermath of wanting more?

### The Traits Table Turned Inside Out

Lay all the "sugar person" traits out on the table and look at them plainly, like cards.

"I can't stop once I start."
"I think about sweets all day."
"I need something sweet to get me through."
"I hide wrappers."
"I bargain for just a treat."
"I feel tired, wired and crashing."

We spread them out and we are meant to read them as the cause: see, you are this kind of person, therefore you eat sugar like this.

Turn the table over.

The fact is… those traits are the result of the trap, not its cause.

Every single one appears after intermittent use, never before it. No child is born hiding wrappers. No child bargains for just a treat before they have learned that a treat is a hit that comes and goes. The preoccupation, the loss of stop-signal, the secrecy, the bargaining — they arrive together once the schedule of restrict-then-binge has taught the brain that BAD SUGAR matters.

That is the inversion you have already felt in your afternoons. The treat did not soothe a needy person; it made a person feel needy.

Here is how the escalation fools you. Have you noticed you need the family size now where a single biscuit once seemed enough? Have you noticed the craving fires before the bite — at the rustle, at the wrapper's gleam, at the smell from the kitchen — more than after?

There is a simple, human way to understand it without pretending you are broken. When the reward circuit is crowded by repeated hits, it gets blunted. The same bite delivers less. That is why the pleasure thins and the portion grows. At the same time the cue becomes hyper-reactive. The packet, the aisle, the clock at three o'clock glows brighter before you taste anything. One system dulled, one system sensitised. Wanting goes up while liking goes down.

I say this as the way escalation is understood — an interpretation that fits what many heavy users report and what imaging consistent with that pattern suggests, seen mostly in people living with obesity and binge eating — not as proof you are damaged, not as a sentence, and not as something that compels you. A crowded circuit is not a broken brain. It is a tired brain that has learned a false lesson, and it unlearns when the lesson stops.

Does that sound like a personality you were born with? Or does it sound like a trap that teaches exactly these feelings and then sells them back to you as identity?

I want to be honest with you about the science here, because honesty kills the Lie better than certainty ever could. Serious researchers disagree about the words to put on all this. Some labs say intermittent sugar access can produce addiction-like patterns of bingeing and craving where sugar is the reinforcer, and that each binge can trigger the reward circuit again. Other leading labs — Cambridge neuroscientists among them — argue that the bingeing reflects the pattern of access, not the chemistry of sugar itself, and that human evidence for sugar addiction as a diagnosis is thin. The debate is not settled. That is the open, contested state of it.

What both sides grant, without argument, is that the behaviours you recognise — the one-bite-becomes-the-box, the cue-driven wanting — do occur under intermittent access, and that a dopamine signal is not the same as pleasure or happiness.

I tell you that split precisely so you do not need to pick a team to be free. You do not need a label to explain your life. You have lived the pattern. The pattern is enough to see the con. Whether you call it addiction or not, the belief that BAD SUGAR gives you a genuine pleasure, treat or lift is what keeps the wanting alive — and that belief dies in the light.

And as you saw earlier, many report this pattern. Not a rare flaw in you. A recurrent, self-reported trap signature across many ordinary adults — not a formal diagnosis, just many people describing, in their own words, the same roller coaster. Does that sound like a unique defective identity? Or does it sound like many decent people caught by the same engineered schedule?

It was never identity. It was exposure, schedule, and a lie.

And it was the Willpower Method that told you identity was destiny. If quitting were a test of who you are, then every failure would prove who you are is weak. That is the cruelty of the Willpower Method. It makes you measure your worth by your ability to suffer without something that was never nourishing you in the first place.

You are not weak. You are not a sugar person. You are a person the Sugar Trap taught to feel like a sugar person, and that teaching is reversible the moment you stop believing it.

### Even If It Were True

Let me give you the graceful concession that makes this reframe unbreakable.

Even if — even if — you did have a more sensitive palate, a more food-loving temperament, a family with sweeter habits, a nervous system that lights up quicker at the sight of cake — even if every word of "it's genetic, it's my personality" were true in the strongest form you fear — this method still frees you, easily, immediately and permanently.

Why? Because freedom here does not require you to become a different person. It requires only that you see one truth: BAD SUGAR does nothing FOR you.

A sensitive palate is not a problem when the thing it craves gives no genuine benefit. In fact it is a gift — it will enjoy real food more once BAD SUGAR stops crowding it. A food-lover is not doomed; a food-lover is the very person who will feel the gain most, because real hunger and clean satisfaction are deeper pleasures than the hit-and-crash.

Do you need to be someone else to stop drinking poison in pretty paper if you see it is poison? No. You just need to see it clearly. The body you have is enough. The will you have — the same wilful persistence that kept you trying after every Willpower Method collapse — is enough.

So the identity excuse dies both ways. As fact, it is backwards: the traits are the result, not the cause. And as a what-if, it is irrelevant: even if you were exactly the sugar person you fear you are, the escape still works because the escape is not about becoming someone else. It is about stopping feeding something that never fed you.

Is there any version of you that would choose to keep paying, daily, for the rest of your life, for something that does nothing FOR you?

You know the answer. And you feel, even now, the relief of not having to defend a label any longer.

### Three Myths On The Counter

Here is a distinct room we must clear, quickly and cleanly, because these myths will tap on the window the moment you feel bright again. Each is spoken in the voice you have heard around you — sometimes your own. I answer each in its own few sentences. No lecture. Just the inversion.

"Fruit will spike me too?"

No. When this book says sugar, take it to mean BAD SUGAR — added and refined sugar plus the starchy junk carbs engineered to behave like it. The natural sweetness already inside whole fruit, vegetables and plain dairy as part of real food is not BAD SUGAR and is not quit. Your body knows how to meet real food with real hunger and real satisfaction. Fruit does not come in pretty paper designed to make you chase the next hit. It comes with fibre, water, chewing, and an ending. Is that a blip your body can handle? Yes. Is it the same con? Not even close.

"I need sugar for my brain."

The fact is… your body makes the glucose your brain needs from protein and glycerol; you do not need to import BAD SUGAR to think. What you felt as "I need sugar to concentrate" was the quick lift followed by the fast drop — a spike-then-crash misread as fuel — and the relief when the next hit briefly quieted the whine. People who never eat BAD SUGAR concentrate steadily all day. Was it sugar powering you, or was it sugar making you tired, wired and crashing and then selling you the brief loosening of its own pinch?

"Dark chocolate is healthy, so…?"

So the manufacturer found a prettier story for the same trap. "Healthy" on the packet does not change what BAD SUGAR does TO you or what it fails to do FOR you, and it certainly does not make a hit give you a genuine pleasure, treat or lift. If there is no benefit in doing it often, there is no point doing it occasionally either. One special exception keeps the whole belief alive — the Big Sweet Lie fed by a single dose. Do you want to step back from the cliff, or negotiate how far to fall?

Ask them plainly. Each myth collapses the moment you place the two columns side by side: what is this doing TO me, and what is it doing FOR me? When the second column is zero, there is nothing to defend.

### One Ordinary Escape

I promised you long-form proof that ordinary people escape, not perfect people, not saints, and not people with different genes from yours. Here is one, in the main flow, as it was lived. I was not special. That is the point.

I am not a person who loved kale and hated cake. I loved cake. I loved the petrol station chocolate at 4pm that counted as "just a treat" because I had been good all week. I loved the white bread that made lunch feel complete. I am 42 now, two children, a job that lives at a desk, and at my worst I was grazing from the moment the kitchen got busy in the morning until the sofa at night.

If you added it, it was not a lot in anyone's story — a spoon here, a biscuit there — but it was every day, and it was every single day for something I did not even need. On a bad day it was three sugary coffees, a pastry at 11, a sandwich on white bread, a bag of crisps, two biscuits at 3pm, a sweetened yoghurt after dinner because it felt light, and chocolate after the children were in bed because I deserved it. I told myself I did not keep it in the house, which meant I bought it every day.

I tried the Willpower Method seven times in two years. One January I kept a streak app and got to 19 days. I was thinking about BAD SUGAR more on day 19 than on day 1. On day 20 I ate the whole box and called myself weak. I dieted and cut down — once a week is fine — and found I spent six days picturing Sunday. I blamed my sweet tooth. My mother has it, my sister has it, I assumed I had inherited it like eye colour.

Reading this book I refused to argue at first. Keep an open mind — I did. Don't stop until the vow — I didn't, which helped, because I was not defending a sacrifice. I watched my own drawer at 3:07. The packet rustled, the first bite gave a short, bright lift that I would now know will only give a short lift inevitably followed by a fast drop, shoulders sagging twenty minutes later, hand back in the packet. That was not energy. That was relief from the low the previous hit had created, and then the low again.

I reached the vow champing at the bit, which surprised me, because I had expected dread. I ate the last sweet with attention — not enjoyment, attention — on its sickly stick and its wire. I made the solemn vow never to feed the Worm again, and I felt, instantly, not deprived but light. Not heroically strong. Just free. I kept saying the line I had been given whenever sugar crossed my mind — FANTASTIC! I'M FREE! — and it felt absurd and wonderful and true.

The first three days were not nothing, and I want to be honest about that so you do not mistake a flicker for failure. There was that empty, edgy, slightly needy feeling, especially in the evenings at the old sofa time. It was mild. It was like a creature complaining because it was not fed. I learned to rejoice when it whined, because a whine meant it was starving. By day four the evenings were quiet. By day six I shopped and walked past the bright canyon of packets at child-eye height and felt pity, not longing. They looked like bait, not food.

Two weeks in, I ate a mango after a real lunch of roast vegetables and laughed because it tasted overwhelmingly sweet — sweeter than I remembered sweetness being. I had thought quitting would be bland. It was the opposite. My palate came back. My afternoons stopped crashing. I did not change anything else in my life — same table, same family, same job — and that was the proof that freedom did not require a new life, just the removal of the con.

I am nine months free as I write this. I have not counted days heroically; I stopped counting after the first week because there was nothing to count. I still think about BAD SUGAR — the thought appears — and I still answer it the same way: FANTASTIC! I'M FREE! The thought becomes a hit of relief now, not a tug. If a stray ingredient slips in at a restaurant, I do not panic. My body copes with an occasional blip. My mind does not reopen the decision. I guard the belief, not the behaviour, and the belief has stayed dead because I see there was never a genuine pleasure, treat or lift in it at all.

Was I ever a sugar person? No. I was a person in the Sugar Trap who believed she was. And the fact I am free — not through will, but through seeing — is the proof you can be, as you are.

### Nothing About You Needs Fixing

So let us land the verdict for this chapter, flat and warm.

You are not exempt because of who you are. You are not doomed by a sweet tooth you inherited. The sweet tooth was not the cause of the trap. It was the taste the trap left in your mouth.

And even if you were the most food-loving, sensation-seeking, sweet-toothed person in your family, the arithmetic does not change. BAD SUGAR is doing plenty TO you and nothing FOR you. There is nothing to lose and everything to gain by seeing that, even as the person you already are.

Have you ever loved food more on a day you grazed all day on BAD SUGAR — or on a day you were truly hungry and ate a real meal slowly until you were cleanly satisfied? Which day did food feel like love? Which day did you feel well when you were able to avoid sweet cravings?

You know. You have always known which eating made you feel well.

You do not need to become someone else to be free. You need to stop believing that freedom requires it. The Big Sweet Lie wanted you to think the price of stopping was your identity. The price of continuing is your steady energy, your calm, your taste — the very food-love you thought you were protecting.

It is the other way around.

When you feel the family story rise again — "we've always had a sweet tooth" — name it and smile. That story is not a diagnosis. It is brainwashing talking, passed like a favourite recipe, never examined. You can love your family and not keep their trap. You can love food and not feed the Worm that dulls it.

There is nothing about you that needs fixing before you can escape. The escape fixes the feeling you thought was you.

**SUMMARY**

- The "sugar person" label is backwards: the preoccupation, loss of stop-signal and secrecy are the results of the trap, not the cause you were born with.
- Escalation and cue-driven wanting come from a crowded, blunted reward response plus brighter cues — an understood pattern that explains why more is needed for less and why wrappers glow, not a permanent flaw and not a force that compels you.
- Whether sugar is called addictive is honestly contested among scientists, but both sides agree the binge-and-cue pattern you recognise occurs and that a brain signal is not the same as pleasure — your lived pattern is enough to see the con.
- Even if you were the most sweet-toothed temperament, freedom still works because BAD SUGAR does nothing FOR you; a sensitive palate enjoys real food more once freed.
- Three quick myths collapse when asked what BAD SUGAR does TO you versus FOR you: whole fruit is not BAD SUGAR, your brain does not need imported BAD SUGAR to think, and a "healthy" label does not create a genuine benefit.
- Ordinary people with ordinary pasts describe an early mild whine that fades in days, then steady freedom and returned taste — early wobble then stability, not perfect and not identity-dependent.
