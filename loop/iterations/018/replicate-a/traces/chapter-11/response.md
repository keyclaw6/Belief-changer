Chapter 11
BUT I LOVE FOOD

**IN THIS CHAPTER** — the family table with the empty biscuit tin in the middle, the sweet tooth story handed round like dessert, the myths stuck to the fridge on yellow notes, a quiet morning testing hunger against a whine

*Your sweet tooth didn't make the trap — the trap made your sweet tooth.*

### THE STORY WE TELL ABOUT OURSELVES

We all know this line. We have said it ourselves, often with a small laugh that asks not to be argued with.

"I love food. I've always had a sweet tooth. It's just who I am."

It is warm. It is disarming. It feels like an explanation that frees you from having to try again. If it is identity, then the drawer that opens at 3pm, the packet that empties before you meant it to, the bargaining for "just a treat" — none of it is your fault, and none of it can be changed. You were born this way. Your mother had it. Your family has it. At our table we love food.

I hear you. I have sat at that table. I have told that story about myself while reaching for the tin that proved it.

And because I respect you, I will meet that story where it lives, not where it is convenient to attack it.

The fact is, we fall into the trap and then we borrow the trap's story about ourselves.

Let me show you what I mean in the most ordinary picture.

It is Sunday. The table is cleared but the tin is still out. Someone says, "We have always had a sweet tooth in this family," and everyone nods. The children hear it. You hear it. The tin opens. A hand goes in. Is the hand moving because a sweet tooth made the family — or did the family that kept the tin on the table make the sweet tooth?

We were all conned at that table. We learned that love tastes like BAD SUGAR, that celebration needs chocolate, that needing sweets is a character. We did not decide any of it. We inherited it like an old recipe card we never questioned.

So I will ventriloquize the strongest version of your objection, word for word, because you deserve to hear it spoken back with love, not mocked:

"You don't understand — I really do love food. Not everyone is like this. My friends can take it or leave it. I can't. My body needs sugar. My personality needs comfort. You can tell other people to quit, but for me it's different."

If that is true, I want nothing to do with arguing you out of it. The test is simple. We will look at what actually happened to you, step by step, and let your own life answer.

### IT'S THE OTHER WAY AROUND

Look at the traits we share, you and I and everyone else who has lived in this cage.

We say we love food, yet we skip the roast vegetables to get to the pudding. We say we have always had a sweet tooth, yet our earliest memory is plain fruit and milk tasting impossibly sweet before the packets arrived. We say we need sugar for energy, yet we are the ones who are tired, wired and crashing every afternoon. We say we need the family-size now, that one small bar no longer does it, that the taste is not what it was.

Do those traits look like a personality you were born with — or like a result?

It is the other way around.

BAD SUGAR does not find sweet-toothed people. It makes them. The need for the family-size now is not proof that you are that type of person. It is the familiar blunting and crowding that follows repeated hits — your system turning the volume down so you need more to hear anything, while the wrappers and smells turn the volume up and fire before you have even taken a bite. That picture is consistent with what brain imaging has suggested, not a proof stamped on you, seen most clearly in small studies of people who already binge, not a verdict that you are damaged or compelled. The hyper-reactivity to cues explains why the drawer talks before you are hungry. The mild tolerance explains why the square that once felt big now feels like nothing.

You thought you chased sugar because you loved it. The truth is you think you love it because you have been chasing it.

Ask yourself three honest questions, and give the only honest answers:

When you felt that empty, edgy, slightly needy feeling before the biscuit, did that feeling prove you loved the biscuit — or did it prove the last biscuit had left you low?

When you needed the bigger pack to get the same brief quiet, did that prove your personality was extreme — or did it prove the con was escalating its price?

When the packet was empty and you felt flat and a little ashamed, did that flatness prove life without BAD SUGAR would be flat — or did it prove BAD SUGAR had made a flatness for you to live in?

There is only one line that joins those answers without twisting itself.

The traits we call identity are the result of the Sugar Trap. That is the Sugar Trap doing its oldest trick — selling you its damage as your character.

And I want you to feel how much of the argument this single reversal already wins. As you saw earlier, many people report this exact pattern on validated scales — not as a diagnosis, not as a label on you, simply as many honest self-reports that look just like yours. That matters for one reason only: you are not uniquely weak, and you are not uniquely built for this. An experience shared that widely is not a personality. It is a trap with a wide mouth.

I know the next voice that arrives, because it arrived for me: "But even if it is a trap, isn't sugar addictive? Have the scientists not proven it?"

Here I will protect your trust by telling you the plain, unshowy truth. Among the scientists who argue this most fiercely, there is honest disagreement. Some read the animal binge work — where a restrict-then-binge schedule drives the binge — and say it looks like addiction. Leading Cambridge voices look at the same binge and say it reflects the access pattern, not sugar's special chemistry, and that human evidence is still thin and open, not settled. Both camps agree that bingeing happens when the schedule is intermittent. Both agree that dopamine firing when a binge lands is not the same as pleasure or happiness. I tell you this not to revive the excuse. I tell you so you see I am not selling you a slogan when I say the belief is the big gear. Whatever word the labs settle on, the lived loop you know — one bite becomes the box — is real, and it is reversible by killing the belief that feeds it.

Which brings me to the graceful concession that ends the identity war entirely.

Even if you did have the most sugar-sensitive constitution in the street — even if you were, by any name, a person who feels these pulls more sharply — this method still frees you. Why? Because the physical whine is trivial, mild, already dying when unfed, and the Big Sweet Lie is what made it feel like love, need, and self. Break the Lie and the Worm starves. You do not need to be a different person to be free. You need to be a correctly informed one.

You were never a sugar person who then got trapped. You were a person who got trapped and then was told you were a sugar person.

It was not you that made the craving. It was the craving that made the you you think you are.

That is the inversion. It is flat. It is kind. And it leaves nothing to miss.

### THE FRIDGE MYTHS

On the fridge, stuck with magnets, are three little notes we all keep. Let us take them down one by one and answer them as you would to a friend — short, plain, without a lecture.

"Fruit will spike me too?"

Whole fruit is not BAD SUGAR. It comes as food, with water and fibre and chewing and a stop signal, not as weaponized sweetness engineered to bypass them. Your body knows what to do with real food. That note confuses the house with the gatecrasher. Keep the house.

"I need sugar for my brain."

Your body makes the glucose it needs from ordinary food. You do not need to pour BAD SUGAR in to think. The 3pm slump that feels like proof you need sugar is the after-dip of the last spike doing its familiar yo-yo — and in healthy people it is not low blood sugar as doctors define it, just the trap's rebound. The brain was fine before the tin, and it will be steady when the tin is gone.

"Dark chocolate is healthy, so...?"

A healthier poison is still sold as a treat that feeds the same Lie. If it is BAD SUGAR made to be a hit, a fix, a dose that keeps the loop alive, it keeps the lie alive with it. You do not need a better version of the trap. You need to step out of it and enjoy food that does not bargain with you.

You will notice something as each note comes down. GOOD SUGAR — the simple sweetness already inside whole fruit, vegetables, plain dairy as part of real food — stays exactly where it is. This book never asked you to give up food, or flavour, or sweetness. It asked you to stop letting processed poison in pretty paper dine on your appetite. When those myths go, a lot of fear goes with them. You are not stepping into blandness. You are returning to taste that BAD SUGAR had dulled.

And if a flicker of the old story still whispers, "But the Willpower Method might work for someone with my personality," hear it for what it is. That is the Willpower Method talking, not you. Strain is not a personality test. Escape is not a character prize.

### I THOUGHT THAT WAS ME — A LONG WALK HOME

I want to give you a piece of long testimony now, not as a slogan but as a life, because the plan that freed me was warmed by many such lives before me. This one is stitched from them, in one voice, with the numbers left in.

I was thirty-eight, a mother of two, and I had told the sweet-tooth story for twenty years. Biscuits in the car, chocolate in the handbag "for the kids," white bread toasted with sugar before school because I thought the children needed energy. I told myself I loved food. The truth was I loved the ten seconds after the first bite and then I was already chasing the next.

I ate between 80 and 120 grams of BAD SUGAR a day without ever counting it as sugar — a spoon here, a drink there, a "healthy" bar that was pudding in disguise. I weighed 76 kilos on a frame that had been 64 before the second pregnancy, and I carried the weight like proof that I was the type who could not change. My teeth ached when I drank cold water. I joked about it: "sensitive teeth, family trait."

The first week I read this book I did not try to be good. I ate as normal and watched. I watched the 3:07pm drawer. I watched the hand. I watched how the lift never lasted past the shoulders sagging. On Thursday I caught myself saying "I've been good this week, I deserve a treat" and heard the frame for the first time: I was paying myself with the thing that was taking from me.

The first three days after the vow were a wobble, not a crisis. Day two I felt that empty, edgy, slightly needy feeling at 4pm like a radio left on low. I did what the book said and named it. I said, "There you are. You are starving." It passed while I made dinner. Day three I went to the supermarket and passed the canyon at child-eye height and felt the old pull as a kind of nostalgia, not a command. I thought, Fantastic — the door is open and I am not walking back in.

By day six the mornings were different. I woke before the alarm, not dragged. The fruit I had thought was "not a treat" tasted startlingly sweet — a peach that made me close my eyes. My daughter asked why I wasn't hiding the wrappers any more. I hadn't noticed I was.

Three weeks on I stood on the same scale. 73 kilos. I do not tell you that to promise you a number — bodies differ, and this was never a diet. I tell you so you can see the picture: the same kitchen, the same shop, the same family table, and the tin shut. The sweet tooth I thought was me had gone quiet. Not because I became a new person. Because I stopped feeding the thing that had been speaking in my voice.

You will have your own version of those days. It will not be perfect. It will be honest. The wobble comes, the wobble goes. The body steadies. Taste returns.

### EVEN IF IT WERE TRUE

So let us close this last identity door with care.

If you have carried the sweet-tooth story since childhood, I will not argue you out of your history. I will ask you to see it from the other side for a minute. What if the family that shared a sweet tooth simply shared a tin, a habit, a schedule, and a set of slogans — "a treat when you feel you deserve one," "I don't keep it in the house" said while keeping it in the house, "just a treat" said every day? Traits shared around that table are not proof of a trait before the table. They are the most natural result of eating the same con together.

We say these things because they dignify the trap and excuse the sadness it leaves.

And if someone told you that you have an addictive personality, hear this flat and warm: the people who measured tolerance and cue reactivity most closely will be the first to say their pictures are consistent with escalation, not proof that you are broken or compelled, and that most of those pictures come from people already deep in binge patterns. The picture explains why the family-size now feels needed. It does not write your future.

Even if it were true — even if every word of the identity excuse were true — you would still be free the moment the Lie dies. The Worm needs the Lie to live. Starve the Lie, and the whine starves with it. You do not need to become someone else to walk out. You need to see that the costume you thought was your skin was the trap's coat, and you can take it off.

We began this chapter at the family table where the story gets handed round.

Leave the table with a different story, one your own afternoons will prove.

You were never a sugar person. You were a person a trap had convinced you were a sugar person — and the moment you see that, there is nothing to defend, nothing to mourn, and nothing to miss.

### SUMMARY

- The sweet tooth and the need for bigger, sweeter doses are not signs of a fixed identity but the result of living inside a trap that creates tolerance and cue-driven wanting.
- Feeling a mild, empty, edgy need before eating does not prove love for BAD SUGAR; it proves the last hit left you low and the belief misread that low as desire.
- Honest disagreement remains about the addiction label, but the binge pattern under a restrict-then-binge schedule is real and shared widely — and that wide sharing shows this is not a personal defect.
- Even if a sensitive make-up were true, freedom does not require a new personality — it requires the belief that BAD SUGAR gives a genuine pleasure to die, so the physical whine can starve.
- Ordinary lives show an early wobble that passes, then steady mornings, clearer taste, and the quiet return of real hunger and satisfaction once the belief stays dead.
