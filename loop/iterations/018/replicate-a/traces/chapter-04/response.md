Chapter 4
THE TREAT THAT TAKES

**IN THIS CHAPTER** — the kitchen after school, gold foil on the counter, the 8pm sofa after a hard day, the house that is real food, the gatecrasher who eats the host

*The warmth you called a treat was the moment itself — BAD SUGAR just stole the credit and sold it back to you.*

### THE WORD YOU CALL LOVE

You came in hungry to test the stories, and the first one on the shelf is the tenderest. It is the one we defend hardest because it feels like defending kindness itself.

"It's a treat / I deserve it / it's how I reward myself"

We all know that voice. I know it too. Celebrating, rewarding myself, going to the cinema, etc with chocolate — that is how we have described love to ourselves when we were tired. After school, sweets were love in foil. Grown up, the sofa at eight is the after-school kitchen grown larger. The day has bitten you. The children are finally down or the emails have finally stopped. You say, I have been good, I have earned this, I deserve something sweet. And BAD SUGAR arrives right on cue, pretty paper, soft centre, promise on its face.

Let me speak to you warmly here, because this is not blame. You were not greedy. You were being tender to yourself the only way you had been taught.

Look at that evening plainly with me. The room is warm. The lamp is low. There is a blanket, perhaps a hand to hold, a favourite show starting, the long exhale after a hard day. That exhale is real. The relief in your shoulders is real. The company, the quiet, the permission to stop performing — all real.

And then the packet opens. What happens in the next ten seconds?

Can you stay with the actual experience, not the advertisement? The first bite is brief, sweet, slightly sickly at the edges if you really taste it. The second bite is already chasing the first. By the third your attention is not on the film or the person beside you but on the bar itself — how much is left, whether you should have more, whether you should feel guilty for wanting more. The moment that was warm before the packet opened is now a negotiation with foil.

I want you to let that register as fact, not as judgement.

You called it a treat. But did it treat you? Did you feel more soothed and finished, more loved and settled after — or did you feel a little more edgy and slightly needy, already thinking about tomorrow's treat while the wrapper rustled in the bin? Did the love get larger, or did the craving and the small shame get larger while love stayed exactly where it was before you opened anything?

We were taught to read that little lift as proof BAD SUGAR gave us a genuine pleasure, treat or lift. The fact is… it never did. Not once. Not ever. That phrase is not an insult to joy — it is the accurate name for an illusion that borrows its light.

### THE HOUSE-PARTY GATECRASHER

Let me show you how the theft works, because seeing it once is enough.

Real food is the house. Think of a proper house that already knows how to feed you. There is bread that is food, not confection. There is fruit with its own clean sweetness, vegetables roasted until they are sweet, a bowl of soup that actually finishes hunger. The house is quiet, solid, kind. It invites you in, feeds you, lets you leave satisfied without a bargain. Hunger comes, you eat, hunger goes. Taste is bright because it has not been blasted.

Carbs on their own are a little lonely in that house. On their own they loiter by the kitchen door, pleasant enough, nothing special. They invite a little company — a spread, a topping, a friend to sit with. That is normal. That is not the trick.

The trick is who walks in next.

BAD SUGAR gatecrashes. It does not knock. It bursts the door in bright clothes, turns the music too loud, shoves the host against the wall, empties the fridge, and shouts that the party was its idea. It takes the warmth that was already there — the rest after work, the laughter on the sofa, the celebration with people you love — and eats it, then tells you in its loud voice: you had a good time because I came.

We believed it. Of course we did. The house was already warm, so when warmth and sugar arrived together we credited the louder guest.

Now play the evening again without the gatecrasher. Same sofa. Same lamp. Same hard day that deserved kindness. Same person beside you, same show, same blanket, same permission to stop. Only one thing missing: the bright packet. What happens?

The warmth does not vanish. If anything, you notice it more, because no one is shouting over it. You taste your tea properly. You feel the blanket. You laugh with mouth empty and hear the laugh. You finish the evening cleanly, without the rustle, without the bargaining in your head, without the tomorrow-guilt. The rest was yours before. The rest is yours after. BAD SUGAR did not bring it.

That is the surgical move that frees you from this justification forever: reassign the credit to its true source. The situation gave the comfort. Your body, resting, gave the relief. The company and the moment gave the love. BAD SUGAR only sneaked a ride on their back and charged you for the ticket.

Once you see the gatecrasher, you cannot unsee him. And you cannot honestly say he was a genuine pleasure, treat or lift. He was an interruption that called itself the party.

### WHEN ONE BECOMES THE FAMILY SIZE

"But," you say, "it really did feel like pleasure — at least at first — and now I need more to feel anything at all. That must mean it was a pleasure that is fading. That must mean I need it."

I hear you. That feeling is common, and we need to look at it kindly and precisely.

You have lived this sequence: the small bar that once felt like enough now feels like nothing. You buy the family size not because you are greedier but because the small one leaves you flat. You think, why am I not enjoying it like before? Must be me. Must be I need it more.

Here is what is actually happening, stated as plainly as I can without dressing it up as lab talk.

The brain learns what you repeat. When you repeat BAD SUGAR as reward, two things crowd together. First, the enjoyment of the taste itself gets quieter with repetition — the same hit feels less. Second, the cue that comes before the bite — the wrapper crinkle, the smell from the shop, the 8pm clock on the sofa — gets louder and fires earlier. So before you taste anything you feel the loudest wanting, and after you taste it you feel the flattest result. Wanting up, liking down.

That pairing explains why one bite so often wants the whole box and why the dose creeps up to family size without you deciding to be immoderate. It is not proof you are weak. It is the trap making its own signal louder while its own reward gets thinner.

I want to be honest about the limit here too, so you trust me. This crowding-and-blunting picture is consistent with what brain imaging shows in some groups, largely where eating had already become very troubled, but imaging of that sort does not prove a person is compelled or that a reward system is damaged. Think of it as an interpretation that fits what you have already lived — escalation and cue-driven wanting — not a sentence about your brain. The lived truth is enough: you are not losing a true pleasure. You are watching a con get less convincing while shouting louder to be let in again.

Ask yourself, with that in mind:

If BAD SUGAR were truly giving love, would love need a bigger bar each month to feel like love?

If the treat truly satisfied, why does the thought after the bar say not "lovely, finished" but "I need something more"?

You know the honest answer, and the honest answer is the escape. You never lost a real sweetness you must chase with larger doses. You are seeing through a trick that first raised the volume on wanting and then sold you that volume as proof you were being treated.

And this matters: none of this means sweetness itself is forbidden or that you must live without sweet taste. GOOD SUGAR — the natural sweetness already inside whole fruit, vegetables, and plain dairy as part of real food — is not BAD SUGAR and is not quit. The house is still sweet. The house's sweetness is steady and bright and finishes cleanly, without the gatecrasher shouting over it. We are not moralizing against sweet. We are returning it to where it belongs: inside real food, inside real moments, not weaponized in pretty paper.

### LOVE DOES NOT LEAVE YOU WANTING

Let us land this chapter cleanly, without mixing in the next illusion.

BAD SUGAR sold itself as love, comfort, reward, a little kindness when the day had been unkind. That is why this justification was the last to fall for many of us. It felt unkind to question it.

But look at the contract it actually honoured.

Love leaves you fuller, calmer, steadier after it has been there. BAD SUGAR leaves you emptier, edgier, slightly needy, already bargaining for the next dose while you tell yourself you have been treated.

Love does not need a wrapper to be remembered. BAD SUGAR is nothing without the wrapper, the slogan, the bright packet that profits while you pay.

Love is most alive in the exact places we credited to sugar — childhood kitchens, cinemas with chocolate where the film and the hand you held were the whole warmth, evenings on the sofa where rest was the true relief. You have already felt, in the house without the gatecrasher, that all that warmth was there before and remains after.

So the inversion is complete on this point, and we will not re-argue it later.

The evening was love — sugar sneaked a ride.

The treat that was supposed to give took. It took taste while calling itself taste. It took calm while calling itself comfort. It took the moment while calling itself the moment. That is the nature of the Sugar Trap, to sell you the ending of a discomfort it created and call it tenderness.

We close with a verdict you can carry as settled fact, without willpower, without strain, with relief.

BAD SUGAR was never the kindness. You were.

The kindness is the blanket, the laugh, the exhale, the company, the permission to stop and be cared for — all the things that were already yours. When you want to be kind to yourself after a hard day now, you will not reach for the con that borrows that kindness and charges interest. You will reach for the kindness itself. You will lie cleaner in the blanket. You will taste your food properly. You will finish an evening and feel finished, not chased.

That is the belief that changed here. You came in seeing sugar as reward. You leave seeing reward as everything sugar took credit for, restored to you whole.

### SUMMARY

- The reward you called a treat was not love from BAD SUGAR — it was love from the situation, your body at rest, and the people you were with, while BAD SUGAR sneaked a ride and took the credit.
- The house of real food is already sweet and satisfying; BAD SUGAR gatecrashes that house and sells you the warmth that was there before it arrived.
- Needing a larger bar to feel anything is not proof of a true pleasure fading — it is wanting getting louder as liking gets quieter, which is why one bite so often becomes the box.
- GOOD SUGAR inside whole food stays free and sweet; you are not giving up sweetness, you are returning it to the house where it belongs without the con.
