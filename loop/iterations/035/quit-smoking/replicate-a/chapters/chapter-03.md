Chapter 3
WHAT DOES IT ACTUALLY DO FOR YOU

*Stop counting what it costs against what it gives, and ask what it ever gave at all.*

### THE LEDGER WE ALL KEEP

I know the page you have added up for years.

We all kept it. On one side the price at the till, the cough on the stairs, the smell in the coat, the hiding and the mints, the mornings when the chest felt tight. On the other side the reasons we told ourselves balanced it. It calms me. I like the taste. It is my little reward. It gives me a pause. It goes with food, with a drink, with friends. I know it harms me, but at least it does something for me.

I kept that ledger myself. I could recite it without thinking. And while there was anything written on the pleasure side, the argument felt close. Harm against benefit. Risk against reward. Perhaps one day the harm side would grow heavy enough to tip me.

That weighing is the Smokescreen talking. It keeps you inside the nicotine trap while you believe you are thinking clearly.

Let me put it to you as one smoker to another, without a sneer and without a lecture. What if there is nothing on the other side to weigh?

I am not asking whether smoking does more harm than good. Every smoker knows it harms. You do not need me to tell you about yellow fingers and stale rooms and the dull head in the morning. You know the bill. You have paid it every day for years.

I am asking a different question. What good is there at all?

You have begun to see you were conned into needing doses. Your hand moved before you decided. You hid in cold places that choice would never need. Keep that seeing. Now turn it on the ledger itself.

Say your reasons out loud, one by one, the way you say them to yourself when no one is listening. Do not tidy them. Do not make them sound sensible. Say them raw.

We will look at each one together, not to shame you for holding it, but to check what the dose added to the hour it stood beside.

### SAY THEM OUT LOUD

I hear your voice because it was mine.

“I enjoy it.”

“It is my reward for getting through.”

“It helps the pause.”

“It completes the meal.”

“It goes with company, with a drink, with talk.”

“I love the lighting, even if I hate being a smoker.”

I will not mock one of them. I felt each of them. We all did. To deny the feeling would be to lie, and lies do not open minds.

Notice what kind of sentences they are. They are not descriptions of the dose. They are descriptions of moments. Food. Finished work. A break between tasks. Talk among friends. The strike of the match after a gap. The dose is always standing next to something pleasant, never pleasant on its own.

When did you ever sit in an empty room with no food, no drink, no work finished, no friends, no pause needed, no gap endured, and light a dose for the pure taste of hot smoke, and think, this alone is marvellous? You never did. We smoked on stairs we did not taste, in rain we hated, between trains while watching the board, half sick with a cold, half asleep, half angry at ourselves for lighting. Those are not the acts of a man enjoying a taste. Those are habit smokes, lit because the gap came, smoked because the pack was there, stubbed with no memory of why.

So let us be precise about what we mean by enjoy. If you mean the second the match strikes after hours apart, when something in you goes quiet, yes. That second exists. I felt it. You felt it. It lasts for draws, not for hours. And if you mean the party that seems to follow almost everything finished, yes. That party exists as feeling. Teaching a class. Finishing a story. Finishing a paragraph. Driving 500 miles. Driving to the shop. The hand seems to say, now you have earned it.

Hold those two honest admissions while we look. Heaven at lighting, and only at lighting. A party that attaches itself to almost everything finished. If those are true, what did the dose add to the food, to the pause, to the company, to the work itself?

That is the check we will make now. Not in theory. In your week, in your mouth, in your chair, in your kitchen.

### HEAVEN AT THE MATCH

Let us be fair to your objection, because part of it is true.

“The moment I light up I’m in heaven, but feel shitty afterwards. So whilst I love smoking I hate being a smoker.”

I have heard that sentence in a hundred forms. I said it myself. I am not going to call you a liar about your own mouth. That taken-care-of feeling at lighting is real as feeling. For a few draws the chatter drops. The shoulders drop. Something feels cared for.

Ask only what was cared for, and for how long.

It arrives at the strike of the match. At the first draw on empty lungs after the gap. It is brightest when you have waited longest, after sleep, after a long meeting, after time apart from the pack. For seconds the faint unease pauses. That unease is an empty, slightly restless, slightly edgy little tug. It whines between doses. It is the small feeder we call the Nipper asking for its next squirt. Light, and it pauses.

Then what? The dose burns on. The second half tastes hot and stale. The fingers smell. The mouth turns sour. The head that seemed to clear clouds again within minutes. The yellow stays on the fingers no matter how you wash. The jacket keeps the smell into the next morning. The problem that sat on the desk before you lit sits there still after you stub. And an hour later the same faint unease returns and the hand starts hunting again.

Look at the shape, not as a judge but as an investigator. Does that look like a day-long gift? Does it look like genuine pleasure added to your life? Or does it look like seconds of quiet at lighting followed by hours of paying for it?

Put it against the whole day. Twenty doses. Twenty lightings. That is perhaps twenty times sixty seconds when the match strikes and the first draw hits. Twenty minutes of quiet, if we call it quiet. What about the other fifteen hours awake? The counting of when you can slip out. The cost counted at the till. The spraying and the mints. The dull head that only seems to lift for a minute. The listening for footsteps. The planning of journeys around where you can light.

If a friend offered you a pudding and told you the first spoon would be heaven and the rest of the bowl would leave you stale and hungry for another bowl in an hour, would you call that pudding a pleasure? Would you write it on the pleasure side of any honest ledger?

You know the answer. You have always known it.

Heaven belongs to the lighting. The rest of the day belongs to the bill. A pleasure that lives only in the first draws and turns to staleness by the half is not acting like a pleasure. It is acting like a dose. It quiets and then it re-creates the need to quiet again.

You love the lighting and you hate being a smoker because both halves are true. The lighting is the second the need pauses. Being a smoker is the life of needing the pause.

### AFTER THE MEAL

Take the meal. Your meal, last night or last week. No laboratory. Just your life.

The plate cleared. The taste of food still in the mouth, warm and full. Hunger met. Body settled. You sat a moment with that satisfied heaviness that says, that was good.

Then the hand moved and the dose was lit.

Tell me truly, from your own throat. What did that dose add to the food? Did it deepen one flavour you had just enjoyed? Did it make the meat richer, the bread warmer, the sauce deeper? Or did it cover the mouth with heat and ash, sour the taste you had just praised, and leave you needing a mint to get the food taste back?

We tell ourselves the dose completes the meal. Look at what happened. The meal was complete before it. The pleasure was the food, the hunger met, the sitting down, the cooking done well or the café chosen well. The dose did not improve one bite you had already eaten. It only marked the end by spoiling the aftertaste.

Ask the questions that close the trap, and answer from your own mouth.

If the dose truly improved taste, why does it leave you needing to rinse the taste away? If it truly completed the meal, why was the meal already complete in the seconds before you lit? Have you never eaten the same meal in a place where you could not light, on a plane, in a hospital waiting room, in a house where no one lights indoors, and found the food just as good? Did the food turn to ash in your mouth for lack of smoke? Or did you eat, enjoy, and move on, and only later hear the old story say something was missing?

It is you that enjoys the food. Your tongue. Your hunger. Your choice of dish. The dose stood beside the plate and took the credit.

Think of the first doses you ever took with food. As a boy or girl you ate with appetite and needed nothing after but to run out and play. No one taught you then that a meal was unfinished without smoke. You learned it later, dose by dose, because older smokers lit after eating as if the eating were not closed without it. You copied. The Nipper learned to whine at that hour. The Smokescreen named the whine as need for completion. From then on every full belly called the hand.

That is not completion. That is timing. The whine arrives after food because the last dose has faded during the meal, not because food calls for smoke the way salt calls for pepper.

Once you see it, you cannot unsee it. The food pleases on its own. The dose only covers it.

### THE PAUSE ON THE STEP

Take the pause. The step outside, the kettle boiled, five minutes between tasks. You light and you stand and you stare. You say the dose makes the pause restful.

Was it the dose that rested you? Or was it the stopping, the air on the face, the stillness, the five minutes of doing nothing?

Non-smokers take that same five minutes and feel the same loosening in the shoulders. They stand. They look at the yard. They drink water. They do nothing and call it a break. They do not need smoke to pause. The pause was pleasant. The dose only rode along.

Watch what actually happens in those five minutes. You stop the task. You change posture. You breathe slower because you are not typing or lifting or talking. You look at distance. Your mind idles. Any body would settle under those conditions, dose or no dose. But because the hand lit at the start of the stillness, the Smokescreen says the stillness came from the lighting.

It is the other way around. The stillness came from stopping. The lighting only broke the stillness into feedings.

Ask yourself, from your own day. Have you never paused without lighting, when the pack was empty or the rain too hard or the meeting too short to slip out, and felt the same small loosening? Did your shoulders refuse to drop because there was no smoke? Or did they drop because you stopped?

And ask the reverse. Have you never smoked without pausing, the rushed one on the stairs you do not taste, the wet one in the rain while others stay warm inside, the one lit while you keep typing with one hand and answer the phone with the other? Did those doses rest you? Or did they pass like habit smokes, hot and hurried, leaving you just as tight as before, with the added tightness of having fed the little feeder again?

A rest that only rests when you also stop is not a rest given by smoke. It is a rest given by stopping, with smoke taking the bow.

We fell for it because we never separated the two. We lit at the start of every break for years. The brain, honest in its confusion, glued them together. Break means light. Light means break. Pull them apart once, and the glue dissolves.

The break pleases on its own. The dose only stood in the break and claimed it.

### TALK AMONG FRIENDS

Take company. Friends talking, laughing, the night warm around the table. You say the dose makes you belong, makes talk flow, makes the hour glow.

Who made the warmth? The friends, the laughter, the stories, you yourself, quick and funny when you are at ease. Would that talk have been flat without smoke? Have you never laughed hard and long in places where no one lit, in a kitchen where the host hates smoke, on a walk, at a table with children present, and felt the same glow? The company was the pleasure. The dose only stood beside it.

I know the pull of the rail outside, shoulder to shoulder, lights cupped against wind, talk low and close. I stood there for years. It felt like belonging. Look closer at what belonged to what.

You belonged before you lit. You were invited before you lit. No one checked your pack at the door. The laugh that made you double over came from a joke, not from a filter. The story that held the table came from a voice, not from ash. When you stepped to the rail, you did not add to the talk. You paused the talk to feed a hunger, then returned and called the hunger belonging.

Ask the honest questions.

If smoke made company, why do non-smokers at the same table laugh just as hard, stay just as late, remember just as much? Are they missing a layer you have? Or are you stepping away from the layer to tend a need and telling yourself the need is the layer?

If smoke made talk flow, why do your best stories come when you forget the pack entirely, when the night is so good the hand does not stir for an hour? Did the talk dry up for lack of smoke? Or did it flow because you were among friends and at ease, and the Nipper happened to be quiet for once?

I hear you answer, because I answered the same way.

“But I feel closer with a dose in hand. I feel less edgy, more myself.”

Less edgy than what? Than the minutes before lighting, when the tug had grown loud and the Smokescreen translated it as awkwardness. Light, and the tug pauses, and you call the pause confidence. It is not confidence added. It is unease paused. The non-smoker at your elbow never felt the unease to begin with. He is not less himself for lacking a dose. He is himself throughout.

It is you that laughs with friends. Your wit. Your warmth. Your listening. The dose sneaks a ride on each of them and takes the credit.

### LITTLE PARTIES FOR ALMOST EVERYTHING

Now look at the strangest claim of all, because it exposes the whole con in one glance.

Cigarettes were our reward for almost everything. Teaching a class. Finishing a story. Finishing a paragraph. Driving 500 miles. Driving to the shop. Washing the car. Paying the bills. Getting the kids to bed. One small party after another, all day, every day.

I lived that chain. I know how natural it felt. Done well, light up. Got through, light up. Pause earned, light up.

Step back and hear how odd it sounds when spoken plainly. What other reward works like that? What pudding do you take twenty times a day, after triumph and after chore alike, after joy and after anger alike, after a feast and after a missed lunch alike? What medal do you give yourself for everything, which means for nothing in particular?

A reward that follows everything cannot be about the thing it follows. It is about the timing of the hunger.

After any finished act, time has passed since the last dose. The little feeder has begun to whine again. You finish the paragraph, you lean back, you notice the whine, the Smokescreen says, you have earned a party. You light. The whine pauses. You call the pause a prize for good work.

But who did the work? You did. Your focus wrote the paragraph. Your patience taught the class. Your hands drove the miles. Your care got the kids to bed. The work was yours, the skill was yours, the satisfaction of done belongs to you. The dose did not write one word, teach one child, drive one mile. It only arrived at the moment of done and stood in the applause.

Keep the joy of the work intact while we look. I am not asking you to doubt your craft. Your craft is real. I am asking you to give the credit where it belongs. The party was the lighting, not the work. And the lighting was only the pause of a hunger the last lighting had guaranteed.

Ask it cleanly.

If the dose truly rewarded work, why does it reward bad work just as promptly, the paragraph you hate, the drive you cursed, the chore you rushed? If it truly marked done well, why does it also follow done badly, and done indifferently, and nothing done at all but time passing? Does that look like a medal? Or does it look like a feeder fed on schedule, with the story of reward pasted over the schedule?

Have you never finished well and forgotten to light because the moment was so full you did not hear the whine, and felt the full glow of done without smoke? Did the work feel unrewarded? Or did it feel complete, and only later did the story whisper that you had missed your party?

The work pleases on its own. Done pleases on its own. The dose only stands beside done and claims it.

Once you see the chain, the ledger looks different. It is not twenty rewards a day. It is twenty feedings a day, each pasted with a different label from the hour it stood beside. Food label. Pause label. Company label. Work label. Peel the labels off, and every feeding is the same small act. A tube lit, hot smoke drawn, tug paused for minutes, tug guaranteed to return.

### THE HUG THAT WILL NOT LET GO

Let me give you the picture that undid the ledger for me. Keep it with you, because you will need it when the old story talks loud.

You are out of your depth in water. You are frightened. You thrash. Someone swims to you and puts strong arms around you and holds your head up. You gasp with gratitude. You cling to him. You thank him with all your heart.

Then you notice you cannot move. His arms are tight around your chest. When you try to swim toward the shore, he holds you where you are. When you try to breathe fully, his weight presses. He saved you, and now he will not let you go. And worse, you begin to understand what your gratitude would not let you see at first. He is the one who pushed you in.

That is what a dose does.

We threw grateful arms around the rescuer because at lighting the panic quiets. Shoulders drop. The Nipper stops whining for a minute. We say, it takes care of it. We mean the feeling. And the feeling does pause.

But look at the arms. The same dose that quiets the unease is the dose that made the unease certain to return in an hour. It feeds the little feeder so the little feeder lives to ask again. It leaves the fingers smelling, the mouth stale, the chest heavier, the pack lighter, the day cut into feedings. It leaves the life outside you exactly as it was, bills still on the table, words still said, traffic still waiting.

A friend who helps you does not need you to see him twenty times a day. A pleasure that helps you does not leave you more in need after each helping. A rescuer does not tighten his grip each time you thank him.

Think of how we talk when the arms are tight. Bumming cigs. The stick. Just a quick one. Little parties. Closet smoker. Febreeze the hell out of myself. Habit smokes. We talk like beggars and like schoolboys sneaking behind the shed, not like men enjoying a chosen joy. Grown men, foremen and teachers and fathers, whisper about ciggies as if they were sweets stolen from a shop. That is not the language of pleasure. That is the language of arms held tight while we smile and call it holding.

I stood in that water myself. I thanked the arms for years. I mistook the pause for rescue. I told myself the arms were mine, my prop, my reward, my pause, my company. I did not see the push.

You were never weak for clinging. Any drowning man clings. We all clung. The con was not in the clinging. The con was in the push we were taught not to see, the first foul dose endured to look grown, the years of feedings that taught the body to whine on schedule, the wall of stories that named every whine as need for smoke.

See the push once, and gratitude changes shape. You do not hate the man you were in the water. You pity the trick and you loosen the arms.

Ask the only questions that matter here, and answer from your own throat, not from the story.

If the dose truly gave you calm, why are you less calm an hour later than a man who never lit? If it truly gave you pleasure, why does the pleasure live only in the lighting and turn to staleness by the half? If it truly gave you company and rest, why must you leave the table and leave the rest to get it?

You know the honest answer. You have always known it.

It never gave. It only paused the want it had made, and charged you for the pause.

### WHAT IT DOES TO YOU

Put the pictures together and watch the axis turn.

The meal complete before lighting, then covered. The pause pleasant in itself, then broken into feedings. The talk warm on its own, then stepped away from. The work done well by you, then stood beside by smoke. Heaven at the match, then the bill for hours. Grateful arms that tighten each time they hold.

Does any of that describe a friend giving gifts? Or does it describe a con taking while telling you it gives?

I state it flat, as settled fact, because you have just watched it with your own eyes in your own week. There are not two columns to balance. There is one column, and it is all on the wrong side.

It does plenty TO you. It does nothing FOR you.

Say that slowly to yourself, not as a slogan but as what you just saw at the table and on the step. The meal, the pause, the talk were for you. Your tongue, your rest, your friends. The dose did to you what doses do. It covered taste, it cut rest into hungers, it chained the hour to the next whine while letting you call the chaining a break.

That is the switch. Not less harm or more harm. No benefit left to defend. Once credit is returned to its true owners, food and pause and company and work stand clear and bright on their own, and the dose stands beside them with empty hands.

I know this is hard to accept after years of calling the pause a gift. I know the story will whisper that at least it does something, at least the lighting is something. Let the whisper come. Then look at the lighting again. Seconds of quiet bought with hours of want. A loan at cruel interest, with the lender standing beside you calling himself your friend.

You do not need to be miserable to see it. You are not losing a friend. You are seeing arms for what they are, and a man who sees the arms begins to feel great to be slipping out of them.

### THE FEELING TO BRING

You came to this book braced for gloom. Many smokers do. They think seeing clearly must mean misery, that to question pleasure must mean to mourn it.

It is the other way around. Seeing that there was never pleasure to mourn is what lets the heart lift. The ledger does not grow heavier. It vanishes. There is nothing to weigh.

So bring the right feeling to what you have just seen. Do not hang your head over the years of feedings. We all fed on schedule. We were conned, not foolish. Do not stare at the arms in fear. They are already loosening once seen. Bring lightness. Bring curiosity. Bring the lift a man feels when a puzzle clicks and the room looks different.

I say it with total confidence because I have watched thousands feel it at this exact point. The moment the benefit side empties, the mind stops bargaining and starts noticing. Food tastes a fraction brighter because you are tasting it for itself. Pauses feel a fraction wider because no hunger cuts them. Talk feels a fraction closer because you are not half listening for the moment to slip out.

That is not theory. That is what happens when credit goes home.

For now I ask only one thing of you, and I ask it with warmth and with certainty because I know what it protects.

3. BEGIN BY FEELING GREAT TO BE ESCAPING
There is no doom here, only freedom ahead.

SUMMARY
- The weighing of harm against pleasure ends when pleasure itself is checked and found empty
- Quiet at lighting lasts seconds, the rest of the hour belongs to aftertaste and renewed want
- Food tastes good because of hunger and cooking, the dose only covers the aftertaste
- A pause rests because stopping rests, the dose only rides on the stillness
- Company warms because of friends and talk, the dose only stands beside and claims the glow
- Work done well pleases because you did it well, the party that follows is the lighting, not the work
- Arms that tighten each time they hold are not rescue, they are the trap holding on
