### The Stairs That Told The Truth

I want to start not with advice, but with something your body already knows.

A few years ago I was hurrying up two flights of office stairs behind a colleague. He was older than me. He did no sport. He carried the same heavy bag and wore the same winter coat and had slept the same short night.

Halfway up I had to slow my step. At the top I had to stand aside and pretend to look at my phone while I pulled the air in. My chest felt tight. My throat felt raw. There was a sour taste at the back of my mouth from the dose I had finished ten minutes earlier outside the entrance.

He could breathe easy and I could not. He smiled and held the door for me.

We smokers know that moment. We know it in stations and car parks and in our own homes when we run up to fetch something forgotten. The alarm rings and the hand finds the pack before the feet have properly found the floor and the first drag is taken on empty lungs. Then later, on the stairs, the body sends its bill.

I am not telling you this to frighten you. I am telling you because your body does not argue and does not moralize. It simply shows.

Ask yourself quietly, as one smoker to another. Did you decide on the day of your first cigarette that you wanted to pant on stairs? Did you choose the morning cough and the cost and the planning of the day around where you can light? If you could have the same breaks and the same nights out and the same ability to face a hard call, without the cough and without the cost, would you take it?

Of course you would. That is why you picked up this book.

That staircase was my first honest picture. Not a chart. Not a warning on a pack. A plain physical fact. He climbed without thinking about it and I had to think about nothing else. I did not need a lecture to understand it. I only needed to look.

You have had a hundred such pictures. The jacket that smells the morning after. The meeting you sit through counting the minutes until you can slip out. The rain outside the restaurant while others stay warm inside. The yellow on the fingers and the stale room and the dull head that only seems to clear after the first light. We tell ourselves these are small prices. Added together they are our life.

Keep those pictures with you while we talk. Not as reasons to hate yourself. You are not weak and you are not foolish. We all started the same way for the same reasons and we all stayed for the same reason. Your body has been on your side for years. It has been trying to tell you.

There was another stairs moment I remember even more sharply. A station staircase at rush hour and a train to catch. A young mother ahead of me carrying a child and a shopping bag moved steadily upward without pause. I had nothing in my hands but a briefcase and I had to stop on the landing, lungs burning, pretending to study the departure board. She glanced back, not unkindly, and I felt something I had not allowed myself to feel for years. Embarrassment mixed with plain common sense. This was not how a grown man should climb stairs.

That is the value of the body. No non-smoker has to tell himself to breathe well. He breathes well because nothing is in the way. When you notice the difference on the stairs, you are not seeing illness. You are seeing hindrance. Something you do many times a day makes an ordinary climb hard.

Hold that. We will return to what it means.

### I Know Because I Was There

I smoked twenty and then thirty a day. I smoked when I was busy and when I was bored. I smoked after food and with drink and after love and after an argument and while I worked and while I waited. I smoked the ones I said I enjoyed and the ones I said were just habit.

I know what it is to stand outside in winter while others stay inside. I know what it is to plan a journey around where I could light. I know what it is to make a promise to an empty room at midnight that tomorrow will be different, and then to watch the hand move on its own with morning coffee. If you know that promise, you know me. We have sat in the same chair.

I attempted to stop in many ways over many years. I made Monday rules and after-dinner rules and only-with-drink rules. I cut down and watched each remaining dose become more precious. I told myself I lacked will. I told myself next month would be better, when work calmed down and when life was kinder. Each return left me more certain that stopping must be miserable.

Nothing worked for long, and each failure taught me the wrong lesson. I thought the failures proved something about me.

They proved nothing about me. They proved something about the method I had been given.

Then I walked into a small back room one evening, certain I would be told to use more effort, and heard something so simple I almost laughed. There was no lecture. There was no talk of strength. A man who had been where I had been talked me through, step by step, what a cigarette actually does and what it only seems to do. He did not ask me to suffer. He did not ask me to count days. He asked me to look and to question and to follow what he said to the end.

I smoked my last dose that night. Not with tears. With a kind of puzzled lightness, as if someone had shown me a knot I had pulled at for years and it had come undone in my hands. I did not know then that what I wanted was an escape into clear mornings, not another dose.

I tell you this for one reason only. I am not special. I had no unusual power. I had smoked for years and I had failed at least as often as you have and I expected quitting to be miserable. It was not miserable. It was quick and clear, and it has stayed that way.

I was the most suspicious man in that room. I went in to prove it would not work for me. I folded my arms. I waited for the catch. The catch never came. What came was a simple way of looking that made the old dread look silly. If it could happen for a confirmed, frightened, twenty-a-day man like me, it can happen for you.

If I could tell you anything from the other side of that night, it would be this. The dread you feel when you think about stopping is not a preview of what stopping feels like. The dread is part of smoking.

You do not have to believe me yet. You only have to allow that I might be telling the truth.

### Too Good To Be True

You have every right to be suspicious.

“Stop without pain? Stop without a fight? I have attempted so often. I know how hard it is.”

I hear you. I said the same. When someone told me I could stop and find it simple, my first thought was, “Too good to be true.”

Let us look at that thought together, smoker to smoker.

What have you been told all your life? That quitting is one of the hardest things a person can do. That you will crave for weeks. That you will be irritable and empty and unable to enjoy a meal or a drink or a break. That some people manage it by sheer character and most people fail. Every ex-smoker you know who stopped by sheer effort has confirmed it, sighing over the story, telling you how they still want one after years.

So of course “easy” sounds like a lie.

But answer me honestly.

Have those hard ways worked for you? Did counting days teach you anything about why you light at a party? Did being told to be good make a rainy night out any brighter? You followed the instructions you were given, and you still smoke. Is that proof that you are broken — or proof that the instructions were wrong?

Consider the other possibility for a moment. What if stopping feels hard not because cigarettes give you so much, but because you believe they give you so much? What if the struggle you remember was the struggle of feeling you were losing a friend and a prop and a pleasure, while still wanting it? What if someone could show you that there was never anything to lose?

I am not asking you to trust a miracle. I am asking you to do a piece of clear thinking with me. We will take each reason and we will check it the way you would check any other claim. Does it hold up when you look at it without the dose in your hand?

Think of your own voice saying, “I love smoking, I just hate being a smoker.” Think of the tired man who swears he loves his habit smokes while he bums another at every break. Do those sentences sound like a free man describing a pleasure? Or do they sound like a tired man describing a need he cannot defend?

You will see for yourself. Step by step, the credit you gave to the cigarette moves back where it belongs. To the pause itself and to the company and to the food and to you and your own nerves and humour and ability. Nothing is taken from those moments. Something false is taken out of them.

That is why I use three plain words to describe how this happens when the seeing is done. Not hard and not slow and not for a few chosen people. It happens easily, immediately and permanently. I say those words with total confidence because I have watched them come true again and again, starting with myself. I began to feel the first taste of freedom as lightness in my chest on the walk home.

Easily, because there is no fight when there is nothing to fight for. Immediately, because the change is in how you see, and seeing can change in a sentence. Permanently, because once you have seen that a trick was a trick, you do not need to unsee it every morning.

You do not have to feel that yet. You only have to keep reading to find out if it is true for you.

### What This Book Will Not Do

Let me be plain about what I will not ask of you.

I will not ask you to be miserable. Cast aside all pictures of doom and gloom. There is no need to brace yourself as if for an operation. If you feel a tug of fear as you read on — fear that life without cigarettes would be flat or impossible — that is normal. We all felt it. It does not mean the book is failing. It means the old idea is still speaking. We will deal with it together, calmly.

There is a great difference between feeling fear and being in danger. You may feel fear when you think of a wedding and a holiday and a long drive and a hard day with no stick in your pocket. That feeling is not evidence that you need the stick. It is evidence that you believe you need it. Beliefs can be examined. That is our whole work.

I will not lecture you. I know the cough and the cost and the yellow on fingers and the stale room. You know them better than I do. I will not insult you by listing dangers as if you were a child. You are an adult who lights the next dose within the hour while privately wishing you did not have to. You do not need more shame. Shame never freed anyone. Understanding does.

I will not ask you to take anything on trust. Question what you think you know. That is your part. My part is to make the questions so clear that your own experience answers them.

You may have voices around you already. The friend who stopped by effort and tells you it was hell. The article that told you to switch to something milder. The well-meaning voice that says now is not the time, work is too wild and life is too full. Let them wait outside the room while we talk. We will finish our talk first, and then you can judge everything else against what you have seen for yourself.

Why should you give me these few hours? For the simplest reason in the world.

Think of what reading on costs you. An evening or two. Some attention. Nothing else. I thought of the stairs and how good clean air tasted in my nose after a rain. If I am wrong, you will smoke as before, and you will have lost nothing but a little time.

Now think of what is on the other side if I am right. The stairs without panic. The morning mouth that tastes of nothing but sleep. Money that stays in your pocket. No more hiding and no more counting and no more planning the day around where you can light. Not a life with a hole in it, but the same life with a weight taken out of it.

Is there any fairer offer? You risk nothing by listening. You stand to gain everything by seeing.

That is not a slogan. It is arithmetic a child could do. The truth is you have nothing to lose and everything to gain. Keep that arithmetic in front of you whenever the old doubt whispers, “Why bother reading on?” Why bother? Because the cost is nil and the prize is your life back.

I put no pressure on you beyond that. I simply tell you what I know. Give this book a fair hearing and it will do what it did for me.

Read as an investigator, not as a defendant. You are not on trial here. The cigarette is. If a sentence angers you, stay with it. If a sentence sounds impossible, mark it and read on. The book is built like a lock. One piece alone will not open it. All the pieces together turn the key. That is why I ask you to follow the whole argument to the end before you decide.

You are tired of being told you must suffer to be well. You are tired of failure that was blamed on you. I do not blame you. I blame the wrong method and the false teaching that kept you in place. This time the method is different because the seeing is different.

### How We Begin

We begin the way every honest talk begins. With an open mind.

You have a mind full of things you were told about smoking — by adverts and by films and by friends and by the years when everyone lit up without thinking and by your own years of reaching without thinking. Some of those things may be true. Many are not. While we talk, let us lay them on the table and look at them one by one.

Can you do that? Can you allow, just for the length of this book, that what you believe about your pleasure and your calm might be back to front? Not wrong because you are foolish. Wrong because you were taught wrong from the first puff, and then every further puff seemed to prove the teaching.

I know this is hard to accept. A cigarette feels as if it takes care of stress. The hand moves before the thought. I am not mocking that feeling. I felt it a thousand times. We will look at it closely, and you will judge what smoothed what.

For now, one agreement covers all the rest.
