Chapter 10
THE BEST CIGARETTE ON THE PATIO

*The night, the drink and the friends were the pleasure all along — the stick only signed its name on them.*

### THE ONE YOU KEPT

You have held the box in your palm and seen it for what it is. A day counted out in twenties. A theatre of mild that quieted doubt while keeping hunger. A hand behind the smoke that packed your day and called packing pleasure.

And still the mind keeps one locked drawer.

“Mornings I see. Meals I see. Work breaks I see. But you have not stood where I stand on a Friday at midnight with the lads laughing and the wine warm in my chest and someone holding out a light. That one is different. That one is pleasure. Surely you would not take that one from me.”

I hear you. We all kept that one to last. We cleared the mornings and the meals and the work breaks in our heads, and we polished this last one like a photograph and said, this is the real one, this is mine, this proves it gives something.

The fact is it proves the opposite. This is the strongest case for the stick, so it is the weakest place for it to hide. Meet it here and there is nowhere left for it to hide.

We did not keep this one because it was the best. We kept it because it dressed itself in our best minutes — night, drink, friendship, laughter, music low, shoulders loose after a long week — until we could not tell the dress from the body. Take the dress off here, once, and you will never mistake it again.

Look at that kept one straight. Not the idea of it. The thing itself on that patio.

Have you ever asked why this one survived after the others fell? Why the morning dose now reads as hunger and the meal-end dose reads as film on the tongue, while this one still reads as glow? Is it because the dose changed its nature after ten at night, or because the night changed everything around the dose and the dose took the credit?

Did the paper ever give you night? Did the paper ever pour the drink? Did the paper ever bring one friend to the table? Or did night and drink and friends arrive first, and the paper arrive after to sign them?

Think how we learned to sign it. Dawn plus mug meant fire. Food plus fullness meant stepping out. Talk plus night meant lighting. We were shown it a thousand times until showing felt like wanting. The patio only looks like proof because the showing was thickest there — low light, warm chest, ring of faces, no clock. Strip the showing away and the minutes stand up on their own. Sun by day, lights by night, leisure after work, company shoulder to shoulder. Those were the givers long before paper arrived.

### THE PATIO AT MIDNIGHT

Half eleven. The pub doors breathe open and shut. Inside, heat and talk. Outside, a square of cold with a rail, a heater ticking, string lights sagging with rain on them.

You are three drinks in. Coat half on. Cheeks warm. The table inside holds your glass with your mark on it. The talk has that late looseness where everyone finishes sentences and laughs before the end.

Then the movement you know in your bones. Chairs scrape. Someone says, “Out for one?” Hands pat pockets. Lighters click. The little crowd drifts to the cold square. You go with them because the laugh goes with them.

A mate holds out the pack. White tubes tipped up. “Go on. One won’t hurt.”

Smoke rises into the string lights. Laughter pops. Someone tells the story about the taxi and the dog and everyone howls. Coughs cut through. Someone steps closer to the heater. Someone checks a phone with lit fingers. The wine keeps the edges soft. Breath smokes white whether you lit or not. The rail bites cold through the sleeve. The heater ticks and never quite reaches.

Stay in that square for ten minutes and ask what exactly gave you pleasure.

Was it the paper burning between your fingers? Was it the sour dry on your tongue? Was it the sting at the back of the throat and the tight pull under the ribs by the third draw?

Or was it this: night without clocks, drink warming the chest, friends shoulder to shoulder, no boss, no phone, no hurry, laughter coming in waves, cold air on the face after heat, belonging without having to earn it.

We credited the stick with the square. The square never needed the stick.

Think of your own Fridays. Have you ever laughed on a patio without lighting and found the laugh thinner? Or did you laugh just as hard on nights when the rain drove you all back inside and no one lit and no one missed it? Have you ever stayed inside with the non-smoker nursing the same glass and found his evening flat, or was he laughing just as loud with no ash on him?

Watch the square with the paper taken out and left in the pack.

The lights still sag with rain. The heater still ticks. The cold still lifts the face after the heat inside. The story still lands. The shoulders still loosen. The walk home under orange street light still feels wide and quiet. Nothing loved leaves when paper leaves. What leaves is coughing into the laugh, stepping downwind to light, cupping flame while the punchline lands inside, tapping the pocket to be sure, counting how many left for the hill home.

The stick was only ever sneaking a ride on night and drink and friendship.

Ask three plain questions and answer without the pack helping you.

Did the laugh get louder because paper burned, or did you miss the best line while you stood outside tending it? Did the drink taste richer with ash on your tongue, or did the first sip after taste of ash? Did the circle pull closer round the flame, or did it close tighter inside while you were gone?

The night gave. The paper took.

### WHAT THE DRINK DOES TO THE RULE

“You often get offered a cigarette and in that kind of environment it’s hard to turn down. You don’t want to be the only one not smoking and because you’ve been drinking, you don’t think about the risks, you think one won’t hurt.”

That voice tells the truth about the moment. Drink plus patio plus offered hand collapses the rule you held all week. Monday to Thursday you can say no without a tremor. Friday at midnight with three drinks warm in you and the whole ring lighting, the no thins to nothing.

I am not telling you the drink makes the stick give pleasure. I am telling you the drink turns down the voice that says no while it turns up the tug that says now.

Drink loosens. That is what drink does. It loosens tongues and shoulders and careful plans. It does not put taste into ash or company into paper. It only makes you less likely to argue with the hand reaching and more likely to call the reaching pleasure.

Have you ever noticed how wise you are about this trap at noon and how foolish at midnight? At noon with clear head you can list every stale mouth and missed story. At midnight with wine warm you call the same stale mouth heaven. Did the dose change between noon and midnight, or did the witness change? Was the noon witness lying, or was the midnight witness loosened past arguing?

And belonging? Look with cold eyes at that fear of being the only one not smoking.

Did you go out to belong, or did you go out to feed a hunger on a clock while the belonging happened around you? Who belonged more — the lad who stayed by the table holding the talk together, or the two huddled by the rail tending small fires while the punchline landed inside?

We all feared standing empty-handed while the ring lit. We feared the pause where someone would notice and ask and we would have no paper to hide behind. So we lit to look part of it, coughed to stay part of it, stepped out to stay part of it, while the talk ran on without us.

Did paper ever make you more part of the ring, or did it twice pull you out of the ring to tend it? Have you ever watched the non-smoker in that same ring, glass in hand, laughing without stepping out, tasting without souring, staying for the whole story? Did anyone call him outsider? Did the laugh skip him? Or did he hold the centre while you held the rail?

Have you ever counted how many stories you heard to the end inside, and how many you heard in pieces between clicks and cups and steps back in? Is that belonging, or is that commuting between belonging and hunger?

I am not telling you to dodge your friends or turn down the glass. Change nothing about your nights except the paper. Go out. Drink what you drink. Stand on the cold square with the lads. Laugh till the ribs ache. The test is not whether you can hide from the patio. The test is whether the patio needs the stick, and it never did.

The drink did not make the dose true. It only made you less likely to notice it was false.

Watch the offer itself. A hand extends. White tubes tipped. Gold ring on the big finger. “Last one in the pack, have it.” The voice is kind. The kindness is real. The kindness belongs to the mate, not to the tube. Would that hand be less kind if it held no tube? Would that laugh be less warm if no smoke rose through it? Take the tube out of the kind hand and the kindness remains. The hand was the giver. The tube was the rider.

### HEAVEN FOR THREE PUFFS

“the moment I light up I’m in heaven, but feel shitty afterwards. So whilst I love smoking I hate being a smoker”

That is the most honest sentence a smoker ever wrote. Heaven, then filth. Love of the lighting, hatred of the being.

Hold that heaven up to the light and time it, because timing tells the truth.

First draw after two hours without. The hand stops shaking toward the pocket. The tug goes quiet for a minute. The chest drops. The head says, ahh, there. Heaven.

Second draw. Less. Third draw. The edge already creeping back under the ribs. By the butt the mouth is dry paper, the tongue is film, the fingers smell, the heater ticks, the laugh inside has moved on to another story you missed.

Did the stick lift you above the non-smoker laughing beside you, or did it drag you back toward him for three minutes and then drop you below him again? Did it add night to your night, or did it pause a hunger it planted that morning and call the pause heaven?

We called that pause heaven because the fall before it felt like hell. Two hours without, then the tug gnawing, then the pocket tapped, then the click, then quiet. The quiet felt huge because the noise before it was loud. The non-smoker beside you never heard the noise. He never needed the quiet. He stood in the same cold with the same wine and the same laugh and never rose to heaven because he never fell to hell.

Is heaven that needs hell first a heaven, or a pause in hell?

Watch what happens after. You stub and go back in. Coat sour. Hair sour. Mouth sour. You sip wine and the wine tastes of ashtray for the first mouthful. You smile and hope no one leans too close. You laugh a little too loud to prove you are still in it. You step less far next time, cough on the stairs home, wake with a tongue like carpet while the non-smoker wakes with his mouth clean.

You slip back inside stale, breathless and chained while the story has moved on without you.

That is not heaven that costs a little filth after. That is a minute of quiet rented at the price of the night, and the quiet dies before the night does.

Time your own heaven on your own patio and you will find the same clock. Light to butt, seven minutes. Heaven, perhaps ninety seconds at the front. Sour mouth, two hours. Missed middle of the story, gone for good. Cold fingers, wet shoe from ash, throat faintly raw on the hill home. Which part was the gift and which part was the bill?

Have you ever lit that midnight dose and felt cleaner five minutes later? Have you ever lit it and found the next want came later than before, or did it come sooner because drink loosened and night ran long and the meter ticked faster? Have you ever woken after that heaven and thanked the tube, or did you wake, taste carpet, cough, and promise the drawer you would be careful next time?

The heaven lasts three puffs. The being lasts all night.

If you feel ill or frightened in your body, speak to your doctor — I speak here of belief, not medicine.

### MY OWN WINTER SQUARE

I want to put my own night beside yours, because I kept this one longer than all the others.

I smoked twenty-five a day for fourteen years. Closet years at the end. Mints in the top pocket. Coat washed and still sour by noon. Febreeze the hell out of the curtains and still heard my wife pause before she leaned in. I told my wife I had cut to ten. I counted twenty-three stubs in the garage bin on a Tuesday and lied about twelve. Habit smokes at the desk. Habit smokes at the wheel. Habit smokes after food. But the patio smokes I called little parties and told myself they were not counted.

My patio was behind a pub in winter. Low wall. Wet rail. Heater that never reached. Third pint. Half one in the morning. The lads out in a ring, collars up, telling the old story about the wedding and the lost shoe. Someone — big hands, gold ring — shook the pack at me. “Last one in the pack, have it.” Bumming cigs and proud of it, as if cadging proved I was not chained.

I can still feel it. Cold iron through the sleeve. Breath smoking white whether I lit or not. First draw hot at the back. Nose running. Eyes watering from heater and smoke together. Laughter so hard my ribs ached. Ash falling on my shoe. Second cigarette off his light because the story ran long. Fingers numb, then burning where I held too close. Mouth like sandpaper under wine. The stick between talk and teeth. The click. The suck. The sour bloom.

I walked home that night buzzing and sour, coat stinking, chest rattling on the hill, head thumping, telling myself what a great night it had been because of those two.

Two weeks later I stood on that same square without lighting. Same pub. Same lads, one missing, one new. Same wet rail. Same heater ticking. Orange wine in my hand, cold on the fingers. Same story told worse and laughed at harder. No tube. No click. No cupping in wind.

I stayed for the whole story. I heard the middle I had always missed while cupping flame in wind — the bit about the shoe in the taxi footwell that I had laughed at for years without ever hearing. I tasted the drink clean to the bottom, no ashtray first sip. I breathed the cold to the bottom and it did not bite. The string lights swam in rain and looked kind. The rail stayed cold and felt honest under the palm. I talked with both hands round the glass and never once patted the pocket.

I walked home with a clean mouth and remembered who said what. The hill did not pull at my chest the same way. The coat smelled of night and rain, not of old fire. The head was clear enough to replay the laugh on the stairs without coughing through it. I let myself in without airing myself on the step like a man hiding a smell, and the house stayed sweet.

Nothing about the square had lost its warmth. The paper had only been standing in front of it.

I did not stand out there to prove I was strong. I stood out there and watched the proof happen. The laugh ran. The night ran. The stick added nothing I could name once I stopped signing its name on everything.

Ask what changed between those two nights. Not the pub. Not the lads. Not the drink. Not the story. Not the cold. Only the paper. One night with paper left me sour and short-breathed and proud of poison. One night without left me clean-mouthed and full-eared and present for all of it. Which night owned more pleasure? Which night would you choose to live twice?

That winter square taught me what no packet ever taught. The con is thinnest where it looks thickest. Pull the brightest dress off and the body underneath is small.

### LEAVE THE PAPER OUT

Do it now while the square is live in your head. Leave everything. Take only paper out.

Keep the string lights with rain on them. Keep the heater ticking. Keep the cold rail under your palm. Keep the third drink warm in your chest. Keep the lads shoulder to shoulder and the story about the taxi and the dog. Keep the late looseness with no clocks. Keep the walk home under orange street light with the town quiet. Keep sun by day for the mornings you already won, lights by night for this one, leisure after work, company that never needed ash.

Take out the white tube. Take out the click. Take out the first sting. Take out the dry tongue. Take out the missed punchline while you cupped in wind. Take out the ashtray sip after. Take out the step inside hoping no one smells. Take out the tapping the pocket on the hill. Take out the cough on the stairs. Take out the counting for morning.

What left with the paper? Cough. Stain. Clock-watching. Standing downwind. Tapping the pocket to be sure. Is that pleasure, or is that service?

Ask three plain questions and answer without the pack helping you.

Did the laugh get louder because paper burned, or did you stand outside tending it while the laugh ran inside? Did the drink taste richer with ash on your tongue, or did the first sip after taste of ash? Did the night feel wider with smoke in it, or did the widest minute come when you breathed cold clean to the bottom and heard the whole story?

The night gave. The paper took.

That is the whole patio in one line. The sun by day and the lights by night, leisure after work, company shoulder to shoulder — those were the givers. The dose never gave. It does plenty TO you. It does nothing FOR you.

See it once and the kept one stops being the best one. It becomes the clearest one, the one where the borrowing showed. You stand with both hands warm round the glass and feel at home in the talk because nothing pulls you to the door.

**SUMMARY**
- The patio pleasure was night, drink and company, not paper burning
- Drink and the offered hand collapse the daytime rule without putting pleasure into the dose
- The heaven lasts three puffs at lighting, then leaves stale mouth and missed talk
- My own winter square ran just as warm with no paper in it, and warmer for hearing all of it
- Strip paper out and nothing loved leaves with it, only cough and clock-watching
