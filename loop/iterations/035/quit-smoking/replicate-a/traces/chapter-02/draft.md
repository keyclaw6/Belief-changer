Chapter 2
ARE YOU REALLY CHOOSING THIS

IN THIS CHAPTER
The garage doorway after midnight, the Febreeze bottle and the mint tin, the kitchen drawer with the spare lighter, the hand that moves on its own.

*You did not choose to need this — you were conned into a trap that now moves your hand.*

### THE GARAGE DOOR AT MIDNIGHT

I want you to stay with a picture you know too well.

It is late. The house is quiet. Your partner is already in bed. The kids are asleep. You tell yourself you will just step out for a minute. You take the pack and the lighter and you slip through the kitchen so the hallway light does not catch you.

Outside the air bites. You pull the garage door half down behind you so the light does not spill onto the drive and wake anyone. You light a dose and you smoke it quickly, cupped in your palm against the wind. You listen for a floorboard inside. You do the arithmetic every hider does. How long will the smell stay on the jacket. Whether the mint will cover it. Whether one spray is enough or whether two will itself give you away.

Then you Febreeze the hell out of yourself. Coat, hair, hands. You chew the mint. You wave the door to clear the smoke. You stand a moment in the cold to let your lungs settle. You come back in and you brush your teeth though you brushed them an hour ago. You smile at nothing and hope no one asks where you were.

I describe it in detail because I have lived it. I stood in that cold myself. I know the shame that is not quite shame and the irritation that is not quite irritation. I know the small, practised lie ready on the tongue. Oh, I just needed some air.

Let me ask you plainly, smoker to smoker, with warmth and without a single sneer. Does that look like choice to you?

A man who is freely choosing a pleasure does not hide in a garage to take it. He does not spray his own coat like a crime scene. He does not time his joy between the sleep of others. He does not divide himself in two, one half lighting, the other half listening for footsteps.

We did that dividing for years. We all did. The closet smoker with the mints. The bathroom smoker with the fan on. The driver with the window cracked an inch in January. The office smoker who volunteers for the dull errand because it passes the smoking corner. None of us called it hiding at the time. We called it being considerate. Being discreet. Not making a fuss.

Call it what it is when you see it from the outside. If a friend told you he loved hot chocolate but he drank it only in a cold garage while his family slept, and he sprayed his clothes afterwards and lied about where he had been, would you say he was describing love? Would you say he was describing choice?

You know the answer. You have always known it.

Choice does not need Febreeze.

That garage moment is not proof you are weak. It is proof something has taken hold that choice alone cannot explain. A chooser does not behave like that. A trapped man does.

You came to this book with an open mind. Keep it open here. Look at that midnight picture without defending it. If smoking were simply your pleasure and your habit that you enjoy, why would pleasure need an alibi?

### WHO MOVED YOUR HAND?

There is a second picture, even more telling than the garage.

The alarm rings. Your feet have not touched the floor. Your eyes are barely open. And your hand has already found the pack on the bedside table.

No debate. No decision. No weighing of pros and cons. The hand moves before the thought arrives. Later the same hand moves after food. After the phone call. After the argument. After the coffee is poured. After the meal is paid. In the pause between one task and the next, the hand is already in the pocket.

I hear your objection because it was mine for years.

"Nobody forces me to smoke. I pick up each dose because I want it. It is my choice."

I say it back to you with respect, because I do not think you are lying. I think you are mistaken about who is speaking when that sentence forms.

Answer me honestly, and answer from your own life, not from theory.

If each dose is a free and happy choice, why does the hand move before you have chosen? Why do you light doses you said you did not want, the stale ones on the stairs, the rushed ones in the rain, the ones you do not even taste? Why do you make midnight promises that tomorrow will be different, and then watch the same hand break the promise by seven in the morning?

A chooser keeps his promises to himself. A chooser remembers pleasure. Can you honestly say you taste and prize each of the twenty you smoke? Or do most of them pass like habit smokes, lit because the gap came, smoked because it was there, stubbed with no memory of why?

Think of the phrase we use without thinking. Bumming cigs. The stick. Just a quick one. Little parties. We talk like beggars and like schoolboys sneaking behind the bike shed, not like connoisseurs selecting a joy.

I smoked thirty a day at my worst. If you had asked me then, I would have told you I chose each one. Yet I could not tell you why I had smoked the last three. I could not tell you what they had added to the hour. I only knew the hand had moved and the pack was lighter and the day had been cut into twenty thin slices by lighting and stubbing.

That is not choosing. That is being moved.

There is a simple test any of us can run. If you wanted to stop eating apples because you truly loved apples but had decided they bored you, would you need a book to show you how? Would you need midnight vows and Monday rules and only-after-dinner rules? The very fact you hold this book in your hand answers the question your mouth is still asking.

You were conned.

I use that word carefully. Conned does not mean stupid. We were all conned in the same way at the same age for the same reasons. We tried the first dose to look grown or to join in or to see what the fuss was about. It tasted foul. It made us cough. No child ever lit that first dose and thought, this is delicious, I will do this twenty times a day for thirty years. We persisted through the foulness because everyone around us seemed to get something from it. Then the trap closed, and from that day forward we mistook the closing for choosing.

That closing has a name, and you will need the name because we will use it until the last page. All those garage moments and moving hands and broken promises belong to one device. That device is the nicotine trap.

Say it to yourself without fear. The nicotine trap. It is not you. It is the place you were put.

### HEAVEN FOR A SECOND, THEN THE BILL

Now let us be fair to your objection, because part of it is true.

"The moment I light up I'm in heaven."

I have heard that sentence in a hundred forms. Whilst I love smoking I hate being a smoker. I love the lighting even though I feel shitty afterwards. I know the aftertaste and the tight chest and the stale room, but that first draw still feels like heaven.

I am not going to mock that heaven. I felt it. We all felt it. To deny it would be to lie, and lies do not free anyone.

Look closer at when that heaven arrives and how long it stays.

It arrives at the strike of the match. At the first draw on empty lungs after the gap. It is brightest when you have waited longest, after sleep, after a long meeting, after a flight, after the argument that kept you apart from the pack. For a few seconds the chatter quiets. The shoulders drop. Something feels taken care of.

Then what? The dose burns on. The second half tastes hot and stale. The fingers smell. The mouth turns sour. The head that seemed to clear clouds again within minutes. The problem that was on the desk before you lit is still on the desk after you stub. And an hour later the same faint unease returns and the hand starts hunting again.

I ask you to notice the shape of that, not as a judge but as an investigator. Does that look like a day-long gift? Does it look like genuine pleasure added to your life? Or does it look like a second of heaven at lighting followed by hours of paying for it?

If anything stressful happens in my life, a cigarette takes care of it. I know that sentence by heart. I said it myself. I am not asking you to pretend you never felt that taken-care-of feeling. You felt it. I felt it. The feeling is real.

The question we will keep asking together is a different one. What did the dose take care of? The bills on the table? The words said in anger? The traffic? Or did it briefly quiet something in you while leaving everything outside you exactly as it was?

Hold that question lightly for now. I am not asking you to answer it fully tonight. I am only asking you to see the limit written into your own heaven. Heaven lasts for the lighting. The rest of the day belongs to the bill.

A pleasure that needs hiding, that moves your hand before you decide, that gives heaven for seconds and takes payment for hours, is not acting like a pleasure. It is acting like a dose.

It does what doses do. It quiets and then it re-creates the need to quiet again. You love the lighting and you hate being a smoker because both halves are true. The lighting is the second the need pauses. Being a smoker is the life of needing the pause.

Once you see that split, the old sentence changes shape. It is not that you are weak-willed or ungrateful. It is that you were taught to call the pause a gift.

It was never a gift. It was a loan at cruel interest.

### TWO QUIET PARTNERS

You may be wondering what actually holds you if it is not love of taste and not lack of character.

There are two partners in this, and they are not equal. Once you see them you will never unsee them, and you will not need a lecture to remember them. They will simply be vocabulary, the way lock and key are vocabulary once you have seen a door.

The first is tiny. It lives in the body and it is almost too small to notice on its own. After each dose fades, it leaves behind a faint emptiness, a physical flutter that asks for another dose. Not pain. Not illness. Not torment. What you feel is an empty, slightly restless, slightly edgy little tug. That is all.

I give that tug a name so you can see it from the outside instead of mistaking it for yourself. That small feeder that whines for its next squirt I call the Nipper. It is not mighty. It is not clever. It is a starving little creature that can only survive if you keep feeding it. Miss its feed and it complains for a little while and then it dies. Feed it and it lives to ask again in an hour.

Every smoker knows the Nipper without knowing its name. It is the fidget between doses. The inability to sit through the film without slipping out. The hand in the empty pocket. The sense that something is missing though nothing in the room has changed.

On its own the Nipper could never hold you. You have slept through it. You have flown through it. You have sat through long weddings and funerals and operations without feeding it, and it did not kill you. It faded. What brings you back is not the tug itself.

What brings you back is the second partner, and it is much larger.

It is the wall of stories we were all taught from childhood. That smoking calms nerves. That it helps thought. That it belongs with drink and with friends and with breaks and with food. That it is your reward and your prop and your identity as a smoker. That life without it would be flat or impossible. That wall of learned stories that keeps telling the tug that it means something grand I call the Smokescreen.

The Smokescreen is the real holder. It takes that tiny tug and translates it. It says, you are stressed, light up. It says, you have finished well, pay yourself. It says, you cannot enjoy the patio without it. It says, one won't hurt. It turns a faint physical whisper into a loud life sentence.

See the partnership clearly. The Nipper creates a little emptiness. The Smokescreen tells you only a dose can fill it. You light. The emptiness pauses for seconds. Both partners are fed. An hour later the emptiness returns, and the story is waiting to explain it again.

We fell for it because we never separated the two. We thought the tug was need for pleasure. We thought the story was truth about ourselves. In fact one was a trivial feeder and the other was brainwashing, pure and simple, installed by adverts and films and older boys and tired parents and then polished by years of our own dosing.

I am not teaching you pharmacology. I am giving you names for what you already feel, so you can point. Next time the hand stirs between tasks, you will not say, I need my pleasure. You will say, ah, there is the tug again. Next time the old story starts, you cannot concentrate without it, you will not argue. You will say, there is the story again.

Names make the invisible visible. That is their only job here.

We were never weak. We were conned by a small hunger and a large lie working together.

### THE CON THAT STOLE CHOICE

Put the pictures together and the verdict writes itself.

The garage hider. The hand that moves before decision. The heaven that lasts for lighting and the bill that lasts for hours. The tiny tugger fed by a wall of stories.

Does any of that describe a man enjoying a free pleasure he has weighed and chosen?

Or does it describe slavery made to look like choice?

I use the hard word on purpose, but I aim it where it belongs. Not at you. Never at you. At the con that stole choice while telling you that you were choosing. At the slavery that chained the day into twenty feedings and called the feedings breaks. At the brainwashing that taught a child who coughed on his first puff that he would one day need those puffs to be a man among friends.

Think how we all started. None of us chose the cough. None of us chose the cost. None of us chose the midnight garage or the yellow on the fingers or the planning of journeys around where we could light. We chose to try one foul dose on phoney information, that grown people got something marvellous from it. Then the nicotine trap did the rest. It removed choice dose by dose while leaving the language of choice in our mouths.

That is why arguing with yourself has never worked. You cannot think your way out with the same story that put you in. You cannot count your way out when each counted dose becomes more precious. You cannot frighten your way out when fear itself is part of the story.

You can only see. See the hiding that choice would not need. See the hand that moves before you. See the heaven confined to lighting. See the two partners, the small feeder and the large lie. See them once, clearly, and the word choice loses its power to keep you.

If this were choice, you could take it or leave it without a second thought. Can you leave it without a second thought today? If not, what does that tell you about the word you have been using?

It tells you what it told me on the night the knot came undone. The choice was taken long ago. What remains is a trap that talks in your voice.

There is a way out that is an escape, not a loss, and it begins by refusing to call the trap a friend.

For now I ask only one thing of you, and I ask it with total confidence because I know what it protects. While we do this seeing together, do not change your smoking. Do not test yourself. Do not try to prove anything to yourself by smoking less.

2. DON'T STOP OR CUT DOWN YET
Smoke as normal until your last cigarette.

SUMMARY
- Hiding to smoke with spray and mints does not look like pleasure chosen, it looks like a divided life that choice would not need
- The hand moving before thought, and doses lit without taste or memory, show being moved rather than choosing
- Heaven belongs to the second of lighting, the rest of the day belongs to aftertaste and renewed want
- The taken-care-of feeling after a dose is real as feeling, it leaves outside problems untouched while briefly quieting inside want
- A tiny physical tug and a large wall of learned stories work together, one creates emptiness and the other names it as need for a dose
