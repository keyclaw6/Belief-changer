Chapter 8
THE DAY WAS ALREADY FULL

**IN THIS CHAPTER** — cold lino under feet, kettle steam, a breakfast plate, office steps in wind, ash in the doorway

*Morning, food and a pause never needed smoke — smoke only signed its name on them.*

### THE FEET FIND THE FLOOR

You know this floor.

The alarm tears the dark. Your feet have not touched the cold lino before your hand has found the pack on the chair. You sit on the edge of the bed, light on empty lungs, draw deep while the head swims and the stomach turns. You cough once, sip cold water, tell yourself you are waking up.

We did that for years. We called it starting the day.

I want you to live that same ten minutes with clear eyes, because the morning is the first place the con hides.

Stand it through without the dose and watch what actually wakes you. Feet on cold lino wake you. Cold water on the face wakes you. Piss wakes you. The kettle hissing, daylight at the blind, children shouting for shoes, tea burning the tongue — that wakes you. Your body has woken this way since you were small, long before you forced down the first foul draws to look grown.

"I am not human till I have had one," you say. "I cannot face people without it. The first one gets me moving."

I hear you. I said it with my hand on my heart. Let me ask you to test it against your own hands.

What moved yesterday — the dose, or getting upright, washing, drinking something hot, stepping into light? Did you need a dose to wake as a boy on a summer pavement, when you ran out before breakfast shouting? Has any dose ever left your mouth cleaner than water, your head clearer than washing, your chest fuller than a yawn at the window? Has a non-smoker at your sink ever failed to wake for lack of ash?

You stand with both hands free around the hot mug and taste the tea all the way down.

Look at that mug. The dose never warmed your hands the way the mug does. It never put light in your eyes. It laid a sour film over the tongue and stole the first true taste of tea, then claimed the tea needed it. You drank tea despite ash, not because of it. The lifting you credited to the first draw was blood moving, lungs stretching, day arriving — while the dose quietly paused the night's hunger and signed its name on waking.

Waking did the waking.

Watch tomorrow with the clock and you will catch it. Alarm. Feet to floor. Kettle on. Wash while it sings. Tea. The head clears by the second cup whether a dose burns or not. The only difference is this: without the dose the mouth stays clean for toast, the chest does not tighten before the bus, the head does not swim for a second and call swimming pleasure. The morning stands complete on its own legs.

### THE PLATE STAYS WARM

Now the meal.

We ate fast to reach the after-meal dose, or we ate slow while the want sharpened through the pudding. Either way the meal worked around the coin. Plate pushed back. Chair tipped. Hand to pocket. Step to door. Light. Eyes closed. Sigh. We told ourselves food tastes better sealed with smoke.

Did it?

Taste your mouth five minutes after that dose. Stale. Dull. The roast lingers less, not more. The sauce sits under ash. A smoker's tongue is a tongue with a coat on. You know the short breath on the stairs you blamed on age — that same coat sits on taste. Food did its rich work while smoke laid grey over it.

"The cigarette finishes the meal," you say. "It seals it. Without it something is missing."

I believed that at every table for twenty years. Let me put three questions on that plate, because only honest answers belong there.

Did the meal please you before you learned to smoke — birthday cake at eight, chips after swimming, the first curry with friends when you had no pack? Does the non-smoker at your table close his eyes at the same bite, sit back at the same full minute, laugh the same laugh, without stubbing anything? Have you ever finished a dose after food and tasted food more sharply than before, or did you always taste smoke and call smoke taste?

The pleasure was the closing itself. The sitting back. The company loosening. The belly full and warm. The pause where no one must perform. The dose only sneaked a ride on a minute that was already full.

It never fixed the itch. It caused it.

See it plain. Through the meal hour without a dose the little hunger builds. You feel it as restlessness under the ribs while pudding comes. You light, the hunger pauses for three minutes, you sigh and thank the dose for the meal. The meal deserved the thanks. The dose deserved the blame for the hunger that gnawed through it. The warm relief that closes a good meal belongs to food and company, not to ash.

Eat tonight and stay at the table. Do not hurry the plate to hurry the door. Linger. Hold the tea. Talk. Let the taste stay. Notice how the flavour lasts longer when no ash covers it, how the mouth stays clean for the sweet, how the chest does not heave when you laugh. That lingering is what you called missing. It was never missing. It was buried.

Food closed the meal.

### THE TEN MINUTES ON THE STEPS

And the break.

Eleven o'clock. Shoulders tight. Eyes sore. Paragraph done, or call done, or till humming. Door. Steps. Light. Stare at traffic. Return. We told ourselves we needed the pause and the dose gave it.

The pause gave it.

Stand those same ten minutes with nothing burning and watch what restores you. Standing restores you. Looking far restores you after close work. Cold wind on the face restores you. Stretching the neck, rolling shoulders, yawning wide, drinking water, laughing with the lad from accounts — that restores you. Your body asks for movement and distance and breath, and for years we answered with a tube of paper and called the answer pleasure.

"I need that little pellet after the paragraph," you say. "Teaching a class, finishing a story, driving to the shops — I pay myself with a light. I earn it."

We all paid ourselves that way. Finished class, light. Paragraph done, light. Five hundred miles, light. Trip to the shops, light. Each finished act got its pellet. Let me ask you who did the work.

Did the dose teach the class? Did it write the paragraph? Did it drive the miles while you slept? You taught, you wrote, you drove. The lift in the chest when a job closes is yours — pride, loosening, done-ness. The dose only arrived at the same second and stole the credit, the way a man shouts his name at a wedding that was never his.

Have you ever taken the break without lighting and felt the neck loosen anyway? Have you ever stood in that cold clean air on the office steps and felt it sharpen you more than smoke ever did? Have you ever returned to the desk clearer because you looked at sky, not because you sucked at a filter?

Try it today as a witness, not as a test. Finish the job. Stand. Leave the pack where it is. Walk to the door with empty hands. Feel the wind find the face. Look far down the street. Stretch till the shoulders crack. Drink water slow. Talk if talk comes. Stay the full ten minutes. Notice the eyes clear without sting, the fingers warm without tremor, the mind turn back to work on its own because rest did its work.

The break broke the strain.

No door must be hunted. No lighter must be begged. No rain must be stood in while laughter stays inside. No butt must be hidden from the kids. The ten minutes are yours from first second to last, not rented in three-minute coins.

### WHAT THE TUG REALLY IS

"But the tug will spoil it," you say. "Morning, meals, breaks — the tug will sit in the middle of them and nag till I feed it."

Then look at the tug without its story, because the story is what makes it mighty.

It is small. If you do not name it smoke, where is it? A little emptiness under the ribs. A little edge behind the eyes. Hands a touch restless. If you did not know what paused it, you would call it hunger before lunch, or tiredness, or need to stretch, and it would pass while the kettle boiled. Watch it for a minute and it is only an empty, slightly restless, slightly edgy little tug that thins while you drink water and look out of the window.

That is not you. That is not your true nerves showing without a prop. That is a withdrawing drug grumbling as it leaves. If you feel truly ill, speak to your doctor — I speak of belief here, not medicine.

Have you ever sat two hours in a cinema, a flight, a hospital visit, without lighting and without tearing the seat? Did you die? Did you claw? You fidgeted, you thought of the door, you lived. Was that hour torture? Or was it a small tug made large by denial? Have you ever slept seven hours without a dose and woken alive, yet feared seven hours awake? If the need were iron law, sleep would not suspend it. The body merely grumbles. The mind translates grumble into order because we were taught to.

Parents lit. Films lit. Every workplace door had its huddle. Every hard minute ended with flame. We did not decide the tug meant smoke. We were taught. Unteach it and the tug has no hands.

You will feel it between moments for a little while. Let it sit beside the mug without obeying it. Drink. Stretch. Work. Talk. It rises like a tide, slaps a few times, falls back. Each wave a little lower when unfed by belief. What feeds it is not time without smoke. What feeds it is the thought that the tug means you lost something worth having. Take the meaning away and the sensation cannot hold a morning, a meal, or a break.

You watch that tide fall and know this is escape felt in the body, not in a slogan.

Morning wakes. Food satisfies. Pause restores. None of them ever needed a dose to complete them. The dose only interrupted each, planted a want inside it, paused the want for three minutes, and called the pause pleasure.

8. TRUST YOUR BODY TO BREATHE AND TASTE
Your body knows freedom without being taught.

**SUMMARY**
- Mornings wake through water, light and movement, not smoke
- Meals please through taste, company and pause, not ash
- Breaks restore through air, distance and stretch, not a dose
- The tug is small and brief and not you, and it thins while you live the day
