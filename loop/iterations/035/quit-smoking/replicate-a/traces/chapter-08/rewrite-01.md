Chapter 8
THE DAY WAS ALREADY FULL

*Morning, food and a pause never needed smoke — smoke only signed its name on them.*

### THE FEET FIND THE FLOOR

You know this floor.

The alarm tears the dark. Your feet have not touched the cold lino before your hand has found the pack on the chair. You sit on the edge of the bed, light on empty lungs, draw deep while the head swims and the stomach turns. You cough once, sip cold water, tell yourself you are waking up.

We did that for years. We called it starting the day.

I want you to live that same ten minutes with clear eyes, because the morning is the first place the con hides.

Leave the pack where it is tomorrow and walk the floor as it is. Feet meet cold. Legs take weight. Spine straightens after night bent. You stand, you piss, you put the kettle on and hear it begin to hiss. You splash cold water on the face and feel the skin jump awake. You pull the blind and daylight hits the eyes. Children shout for shoes. The house creaks alive.

What woke you in that chain?

Your body has woken this way since you were small, long before you forced down the first foul draws to look grown. Blood moves because you stood. Lungs stretch because you yawned at the window. Nerves brighten because light and cold and noise and movement tell them day has begun. No paper tube taught your heart to beat faster when feet hit cold. No ash taught your eyes to open.

"I am not human till I have had one," you say. "I cannot face people without it. The first one gets me moving."

I hear you. I said it with my hand on my heart for twenty years. Let me put it against your own hands.

What moved yesterday — the dose, or getting upright, washing, drinking something hot, stepping into light? Did you wake as a boy on a summer pavement because smoke touched you, when you ran out before breakfast shouting? Has any dose ever left your mouth cleaner than water, your head clearer than washing, your chest fuller than that first yawn at the open window? Has the non-smoker at your sink ever failed to wake for lack of ash?

Watch tomorrow with the clock and you will catch it. Alarm. Feet to floor. Kettle on. Wash while it sings. Tea. The head clears by the second cup whether a dose burns or not. The only difference is this: without the dose the mouth stays clean for toast, the chest does not tighten before the bus, the head does not swim for a second and call swimming pleasure. The morning stands complete on its own legs.

That first draw on empty lungs never woke anything. It paused the hunger that built all night and made the pause feel like waking. All night without doses the body withdrew in sleep, tide rising in the dark, so morning arrived edgy and seemed to prove you needed the light. You lit, the edginess paused for a breath, you thanked the dose for the day. The day deserved the thanks. The dose deserved the blame for the edge it stilled for three minutes.

Waking did the waking.

Stand it through and notice what you gain in plain detail. The tongue is not coated before tea. The first sip lands true. The cough that used to scrape the throat on the stairs does not come. You know the short breath on the stairs you blamed on age — climb them with empty lungs and clear mouth and feel how much of that heaviness was smoke sitting overnight. The breath comes deeper without thinking. The shoulders do not hunch around a flame in the doorway while the kettle boils itself dry inside.

You walk back into the kitchen with both hands warm around the mug. The steam wets the face. The tea burns the tongue awake. The window is cracked and the morning air finds the skin. That is not theory. That is Tuesday lived without a veil between you and it.

Ask the only question that matters about that doorway minute.

Did the dose add one drop to the kettle, the light, the water, the tea? Or did it steal ten minutes from them, sour the mouth that was meant to taste, tighten the chest that was meant to stretch, and hurry you out into the cold to serve it while the house woke without you?

It added nothing. It interrupted everything and signed its name on what remained.

### THE MUG IN BOTH HANDS

Stay with the mug, because the hands tell the whole story.

A smoker never holds a morning with both hands. One hand holds the mug, one holds the stick. One hand warms, one burns. You juggle. You set the mug down to flick ash. You pick it up to sip through sourness. You open the door with an elbow. You find the lighter with cold fingers. Half your attention tends the dose so it does not drop, gutter, or stain.

Put nothing between the palms and feel the difference.

Both hands around heat. Fingers spreading to take the warmth. Thumbs resting. No juggling, no ash hunting, no butt to shield from wind. You lift, you sip, you lower, you look out. The hands are not servants in that minute. They are yours.

We were taught to read that empty-handed minute as missing something. It is the other way around. The dose made the hands full and the morning thin. Empty hands make the morning full.

Drink the tea slow and taste it from first scald to last lukewarm mouthful. The first cup clears the night mouth. The second cup tastes of tea itself, bright and plain. Without smoke laying grey over the tongue, the flavour stays on the tongue instead of turning to stale film by the bus stop. You do not need to Febreeze the kitchen or chew a mint to hide from your own children before school. You kiss the top of a head without turning away. You breathe out and smell rain, toast, soap — not an ashtray standing in a doorway.

Have you ever finished that first dose and felt cleaner than water left you? Have you ever lit and seen the day more sharply than washing showed it? Have you ever held the mug with both hands and thought, this lacks something, or did the lack only arrive when the mind named the tug and the storyteller said, light?

The storyteller was trained, not born. Parents lit before speaking. Films lit before kissing. Every workplace door had its huddle before work. Every hard minute ended with flame before thought. We did not decide that mug plus dawn meant smoke. We were shown it a thousand times until showing felt like wanting.

Unshow it with one lived morning and the showing loses its hands.

I smoked that doorway morning through fog and funerals and births, through cough that shook the bed and promises made with tears. I know the hand moving before thought. I know the pack counted at midnight to survive dawn. I am not speaking from a clean hill. I am speaking from inside your kitchen, where the kettle sings now.

Live the kettle through. Fill it. Wait for it. Listen. Pour. Hold. Sip. Stand at the window and watch the street wake — bins out, coats up, dogs pulling. Yawn till the jaw cracks. Stretch till the shoulders pop. Wash the cup. Put on shoes. The body knows how to climb out of night. It did it for years before the first foul draw made you cough and water, and it does it still when the night's debt is not renewed.

Morning wakes.

### THE PLATE STAYS WARM

Now the meal.

We ate fast to reach the after-meal dose, or we ate slow while the want sharpened through the pudding. Either way the meal worked around the coin. Plate pushed back. Chair tipped. Hand to pocket. Step to door. Light. Eyes closed. Sigh. We told ourselves food tastes better sealed with smoke.

Did it?

Taste your mouth five minutes after that dose. Stale. Dull. The roast lingers less, not more. The sauce sits under ash. The sweet comes through a coat. A smoker's tongue is a tongue with a film on, nose half-closed, throat faintly raw. Food did its rich work while smoke laid grey over it, then the dose claimed the richness as its own.

"The cigarette finishes the meal," you say. "It seals it. Without it something is missing."

I believed that at every table for twenty years. Let me put three questions on that plate, because only honest answers belong there.

Did the meal please you before you learned to smoke — birthday cake at eight, chips after swimming, the first curry with friends when you had no pack and laughed till you hurt? Does the non-smoker at your table close his eyes at the same bite, sit back at the same full minute, loosen at the same talk, without stubbing anything? Have you ever finished a dose after food and tasted food more sharply than before, or did you always taste smoke and call smoke taste?

The pleasure was the closing itself. The sitting back. The belly full and warm. The company loosening. The pause where no one must perform. The dose only sneaked a ride on a minute that was already full.

Eat tonight and stay at the table. Do not hurry the plate to hurry the door. Linger. Push back a little. Hold the tea. Talk. Let the taste stay instead of covering it. Notice how the flavour lasts longer when no ash covers it, how the mouth stays clean for the sweet, how the chest does not heave when you laugh, how you do not have to leave the people you love to stand alone outside while the plates cool.

That lingering is what you called missing. It was never missing. It was buried.

See the hunger plain. Through the meal hour without a dose the little hunger builds. You feel it as restlessness under the ribs while pudding comes. You light, the hunger pauses for three minutes, you sigh and thank the dose for the meal. The meal deserved the thanks. The dose deserved the blame for the hunger that gnawed through it.

It never fixed the itch. It caused it.

Through that hour the drug withdrew while you ate, so the end of eating coincided with the sharpest tug. Pausing the tug felt like sealing the meal. Sealing was sitting back, blood turning to digestion, talk turning soft. The warm heaviness that closes a good meal belongs to food and company, not to ash. The dose only arrived at the same second and shouted its name over theirs.

Food closed the meal.

Stay till the plates are cleared. Help clear them with free hands. Carry, stack, rinse. Smell the soap. Taste the last tea without a film. Talk without watching the clock for the door. When you rise, rise because the evening moves on, not because a whine orders you to the step. The table holds you longer when nothing pulls you from it, and the holding is the pleasure you thought you would lose.

### THE TASTE THAT LINGERS

Linger a little longer on taste, because taste is where the lie is thinnest.

A smoker tastes through smoke. Morning tea through last night's throat. Lunch through the doorway dose. Evening meal through the afternoon chain. Each draw lays heat and tar and sourness over buds that were built to read butter, salt, sweet, lemon, char, fresh bread crust. Then we wonder why food seems flat unless sealed with flame.

It seems flat because flame flattened it.

Take one ordinary breakfast and eat it like a witness. Toast. Butter melting into holes. Marmalade bitter-sweet. Tea. Eat without hurrying for the door. Chew. Pause. Sip. Notice the crunch, then soft, then warm. Notice the tongue clean between bites instead of filmed. Notice the nose open, so smell lifts flavour the way it does for the child who has never lit and finds toast wonderful without help.

Do the same at supper. Soup. Bread. Roast. Green bite. Sweet. Let each sit. Talk between. Drink water. You will find the flavours last minutes longer when they are not chased by ash. You will find the belly settles sooner because you did not bolt to feed. You will find the mouth willing to laugh without coughing, to kiss goodnight without turning, to sleep without a glass of water for a throat that burns.

"But I love the taste of smoke after food," you say. "That first draw with coffee is the best of the day."

I loved that minute too, until I watched it. What loves in that minute? The coffee loves. The sitting loves. The done-ness loves. The three minutes of doing nothing after doing much loves. The dose loves only the pause of its own want, which grew sharpest while you ate and therefore feels sweetest when paused. Because the day's lows stack highest by evening, the brief climb back toward normal feels steepest with night drink and leisure and company sweet on their own. The steepness is withdrawal stacked, not pleasure deepened.

Have you ever sealed a meal and tasted food more, not less, five minutes later? Have you ever kept the taste of roast on the tongue past the doorstep, or did ash always replace it within two draws? Have you ever watched the non-smoker finish the same plate, close his eyes, sigh, and stay sighing without lighting — and thought him deprived? He kept what you chased. He stayed where you left.

Taste does not need a seal of smoke. Taste needs a clean tongue and time.

Give it both and the meal grows longer, not shorter. The plate stays warm in memory because you stayed with it. The sweet is sweeter because no bitter film fights it. The tea after is brighter because the mouth did not go stale. What you feared as an empty end becomes a full one, minute by minute, bite by bite, breath by breath at a table you no longer have to leave.

### THE TEN MINUTES ON THE STEPS

And the break.

Eleven o'clock. Shoulders tight. Eyes sore. Paragraph done, or call done, or till humming. Door. Steps. Light. Stare at traffic. Return. We told ourselves we needed the pause and the dose gave it.

The pause gave it.

Stand those same ten minutes with nothing burning and watch what restores you. Standing restores you after sitting. Looking far restores you after close work. Cold wind on the face restores you. Stretching the neck, rolling shoulders, yawning wide, drinking water slow, laughing with the lad from accounts — that restores you. Your body asks for movement and distance and breath, and for years we answered with a tube of paper and called the answer pleasure.

"I need that little pellet after the paragraph," you say. "Teaching a class, finishing a story, driving to the shops — I pay myself with a light. I earn it."

We all paid ourselves that way. Finished class, light. Paragraph done, light. Five hundred miles, light. Trip to the shops, light. Each finished act got its pellet. Let me ask you who did the work.

Did the dose teach the class? Did it write the paragraph? Did it drive the miles while you slept? You taught, you wrote, you drove. The lift in the chest when a job closes is yours — pride, loosening, done-ness. The dose only arrived at the same second and stole the credit, the way a man shouts his name at a wedding that was never his.

Try it today as a witness, not as a test. Finish the job. Stand. Leave the pack where it is. Walk to the door with empty hands. Feel the wind find the face. Look far down the street past roofs to sky. Stretch till the shoulders crack. Drink water slow and feel it go down. Talk if talk comes. Stay the full ten minutes. Notice the eyes clear without sting, the fingers warm without tremor, the mind turn back to work on its own because rest did its work.

The break broke the strain.

No door must be hunted. No lighter must be begged. No rain must be stood in while laughter stays inside. No butt must be hidden from the kids in the garden. No coin must be dropped for three minutes of quiet that quiet gives for nothing. The ten minutes are yours from first second to last, not rented.

Watch the steps themselves with clear eyes. The lad sucking hard near the filter to squeeze the last quiet out is not taking a pause. He is serving a meter. He checks the time. He coughs, smiles as if the cough proved something, stubs with a small frown and is restless within minutes. The woman cupping flame in wind talks about cutting down while lighting from the last stub. They stare at traffic because there is nowhere else to stare while feeding. You stare at traffic and see the street — bus hissing, pigeon on the wire, girl running with coat flying, light changing on windows.

Have you ever taken the break without lighting and felt the neck loosen anyway? Have you ever stood in that cold air on the office steps and felt it sharpen you more than smoke ever did? Have you ever returned to the desk clearer because you looked at sky, not because you sucked at a filter?

That sharpening is the body doing its work when the con does not sign it.

Take the afternoon slump the same way. Three o'clock. Eyes heavy. Mouth dry. Walk. Water. Window. Stairs climbed slow to move blood. Talk standing. Wash the face. The heaviness lifts because movement and light and water lift it, as they lift it for every creature that was never taught to light. The dose never lifted it. It paused the extra heaviness of withdrawal on top of ordinary tiredness and called the pause energy.

Rest restored.

### WHAT THE TUG REALLY IS

"But the tug will spoil it," you say. "Morning, meals, breaks — the tug will sit in the middle of them and nag till I feed it."

Then look at the tug without its story, because the story is what makes it mighty.

It is small. If you do not name it smoke, where is it? A little emptiness under the ribs. A little edge behind the eyes. Hands a touch restless. If you did not know what paused it, you would call it hunger before lunch, or tiredness, or need to stretch, and it would pass while the kettle boiled. Watch it for a minute and it is only an empty, slightly restless, slightly edgy little tug that thins while you drink water and look out of the window.

That is not you. That is not your true nerves showing without a prop. That is a withdrawing drug grumbling as it leaves. If you feel truly ill, speak to your doctor — I speak of belief here, not medicine.

Have you ever sat two hours in a cinema, a flight, a hospital visit, without lighting and without tearing the seat? Did you die? Did you claw? You fidgeted, you thought of the door, you lived. Was that hour torture? Or was it a small tug made large by denial? Have you ever slept seven hours without a dose and woken alive, yet feared seven hours awake? If the need were iron law, sleep would not suspend it. The body merely grumbles. The mind translates grumble into order because we were taught to.

Parents lit. Films lit. Every workplace door had its huddle. Every hard minute ended with flame. We did not decide the tug meant smoke. We were taught. Unteach it and the tug has no hands.

You will feel it between moments for a little while. Let it sit beside the mug without obeying it. Drink. Stretch. Work. Talk. It rises like a tide, slaps a few times, falls back. Each wave a little lower when unfed by belief. What feeds it is not time without smoke. What feeds it is the thought that the tug means you lost something worth having. Take the meaning away and the sensation cannot hold a morning, a meal, or a break.

We smoked to feel briefly like non-smokers feel all the time, and paid for the loan with the next low. Morning wakes without it. Food satisfies without it. Pause restores without it. None of them ever needed a dose to complete them. The dose only interrupted each, planted a want inside it, paused the want for three minutes, and called the pause pleasure.

Live one full day that way — feet to floor, mug in both hands, plate stayed with, steps stood on with nothing burning — and the seeing becomes bone-deep. Not a slogan. A day lived.

8. TRUST YOUR BODY TO BREATHE AND TASTE
Your body knows freedom without being taught.

**SUMMARY**
- Mornings wake through water, light and movement, not smoke
- Meals please through taste, company and pause, not ash
- Breaks restore through air, distance and stretch, not a dose
- The tug is small and brief and not you, and it thins while you live the day
- A day lived without signing it to smoke proves the day was already full
