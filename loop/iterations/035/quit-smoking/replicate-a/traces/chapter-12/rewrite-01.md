Chapter 12
THE FAREWELL TABLE

IN THIS CHAPTER
The ordinary kitchen table, the yellowed filter, the lengthening ash, the stubbed end and the window air after

*This last dose takes nothing from you — it only shows you there was never anything to keep.*

### ARE YOU CHAMPING AT THE BIT

You have closed the drawer with the numbers in it. You have taken the brass lamp off the shelf. You have crossed out the Monday written in pencil on the calendar. What is left to bargain with once hunger has no number and no date and no special coat to wear?

We all arrived at this gate with the same two feelings at once. A lightness because the proof has piled so high we can no longer pretend paper gave, and a flutter because the hand still moves toward the pocket on stairs and after meals and when the phone rings sharp. That flutter is not evidence that something true remains. It is the echo of hours trained to move together.

So I ask you plain, as I ask every smoker before this table, do you feel ready? You should be champing at the bit. There should be an impatience in you to be done, a wish to stub the last end and turn the face to the window. Not dread. Not a heavy heart. An eagerness to have the whole business finished.

Have you ever waited for a train that would take you home after long weeks away, and paced the platform checking the clock? Was the pacing fear that home would be worse, or was it hunger to be there? Is your impatience now of that kind, or is it grief for a friend?

If the answer is grief, do not force the hour. Go back and look again at the patio and the morning and the meal-end and the diary and the lamp until the grief thins. All you have to do is follow all the instructions.

“What if I feel a wobble even while I feel ready?”

I welcome that question because it is honest. A wobble before the table is not a verdict that you still need paper. Did you ever stand at a door you had long wanted opened and feel the hand shake on the handle? Did the shaking mean you loved the corridor, or did it mean years of being told the corridor was all you had? Would you call that shake love, or would you call it old training knocking once more?

We were conned into believing that smoking was choice and pleasure and prop. We lived years lighting on waking and after food and on breaks and on nights out, washing curtains and airing coats and stepping into cold squares to tend small fires. That living leaves a tremor. The tremor passes when the belief behind it has gone.

Ask and answer without paper helping.

Is there one pleasure left standing that you can name without borrowing hunger’s voice? Is there one hour of your day that paper coloured without first greying it? If three a day gave, why did twenty give more? If one at Christmas gave, why book it months ahead like a train you fear to miss?

You know because you have timed it on your own skin. The lighting ninety seconds bright at the front, the sour mouth two hours, the middle of talk and taste missed for good. This is how you step out of the nicotine trap.

You do not step with dread. You step with impatience. The gate is here. The table is set.

### THE QUIET TABLE

Make it ordinary. That matters more than you know.

Not a birthday. Not a holiday. Not a last wild night with friends and drinks to remember it by. An ordinary hour on an ordinary day, with the ordinary pack you carry and the ordinary ashtray you empty. The kettle still warm. The window cracked to the street noise. The chair you always sit in. No ceremony borrowed from films. No extra doses smoked to say goodbye properly. This farewell is ceremonial in meaning, not in smoke.

We say goodbye best when we look straight. Put the pack on the table and look at it as a day counted out in twenties. A box built to time your hours. Paper tubes lined up to carry one drug in puffs, factory counted. Notice the theatre of filter and mild promise and the clean lettering. Who packed the day so neatly, and for whose pocket?

Put one dose aside for the watching. Push the rest out of reach, to be thrown when the watching is done. You will not need them. It is true now as it was on the first page: you have nothing to lose and everything to gain.

“I want to make it special, to honour twenty years.”

Would you honour a chain by polishing one link? Would you honour years of cough on stairs and sour meal-ends and missed middles by giving paper one more sweet hour? Honour the twenty years by seeing them plain. Let the last one be the plainest of all, watched without kindness and without nostalgia, so memory keeps no perfume to call you back with.

Sit down. Breathe the room air first. Note the mouth as it is before lighting — taste still there from food or drink, tongue clean enough to know coffee or toast, fingers smelling of soap or work, not of yesterday’s fire. Note the hands steady when they are not timing a wait. This is your baseline, the non-smoker mouth and nose you were lent before the first con. Paper never improved it. It only borrowed it and returned it dulled.

Have you ever noticed how the room smells before the first light, and how it smells after? Which smell would you choose to live in for the rest of your life if no hunger voted? Would you pay coins every day to turn the first smell into the second, or would you call that payment strange unless hunger had taught you to call it pleasure?

We paid it because brainwashing called hunger pleasure and called the payment love. We lit on waking before feet found the floor. We lit at meal-end and soured the taste we claimed to crown. We lit on breaks and called stepping out freedom while talk ran inside without us. We hid in garages and fanned coats and chewed mints and called the hiding choice.

Look longer at the pack before you touch it. See the cellophane crackle, the foil torn, the row of white tips all exactly the same, none chosen, none special. Pick one and feel how light it is, how little there is to it — a pinch of leaf rolled in paper with a plug to keep the leaf from the lips. Is this the prop that steadied arguments and carried nights? Does the object match the weight we gave it, or does the mismatch show where the weight really lived?

Look at the table now and call it what it is. A small fire to be tended for minutes, leaving ash and stain. Nothing to keep. Nothing to mourn.

### LIGHT IT AND WATCH

Now light it. Watch it the way you have never watched it, because need always hurried you past the looking.

Hold the filter and see the yellow stain already forming where lips meet. That stain was on your fingers for years and on your breath in lifts. Put the dose to the lips and notice the first touch of paper on dry lip, the sulfur puff from the match or the click of the lighter you have clicked ten thousand times without hearing. Draw and feel the first heat, then the dry paper mouth by the third puff. Taste the stale end as it shortens — not flavour, but hot dust and chemical bite at the back of the throat. Watch the ash lengthen and the grey curl thin. Smell the sleeve as smoke settles into cloth.

Do not hurry. Do not puff to get the worth. Smoke it as you always smoked it, neither punishing nor savouring, and let the senses report without the old story over them.

What does the tongue report after five puffs? Is it livelier, or filmed? What does the throat report — opened, or stung? What do the fingers report — pleased, or smelling? What does the chest report on the next stair — lighter, or tighter? What does the mind report — clearer on the problem that waits on the desk, or merely quieter for minutes about the pocket while the problem sits untouched?

We credited that brief quiet with calm. We said paper steadied the argument and the deadline and the night drive. Did the papers move while we puffed? Did the argument soften? Or did the hand stop reaching for a minute and the head call the pause a gift while the need that caused the reaching was fed again to ensure the next reaching?

Watch to the middle of the dose, where we never looked before. The ash now a full knuckle long, threatening the cuff. Tap it and see how readily it breaks — grey, powdery, dead. Nothing in it. The ember creeps closer. Each draw hotter than the last, each taste staler. The filter warms under the fingers. A brown bloom spreads from the mouth end. Is this pleasure deepening, or is this a fire consuming itself and marking what it touches?

Tell me plain, whose calm was it? If the dose calmed, why did the tight chest return within the hour louder than before? If the dose pleased, why did the mouth turn sour and the talk move on while you tended ash? Does a pleasure end in needing to wash, or does a hunger end in needing to feed again?

Feed it no more and the Nipper starves.

That small creature is no mighty enemy. It is a trivial physical hunger, a hollow unease that complains when unfed and quiets for minutes when fed, only to complain again. We mistook its complaining for proof that paper gave energy and company and courage. The belief fed it. The belief is gone. Without belief it has no voice, only a faint tug that sinks unfed.

Look to the end of the dose now. The filter browned. The taste turned. The ash grey. The fingers marked. Is this the friend you feared to lose, or is this the evidence you needed to lose fear? Would you book months ahead for dry tongue? Would you cross town for sting? Or would you only cross town because hunger had dressed them as memory?

It gave nothing. It took the hour and left the stain.

### WHAT YOUR BODY WILL DO AFTER

Stub it. Push the ashtray a little away. Sit with the stale taste on purpose for a minute and let it teach.

Press the ember out with a firm twist and hear the faint hiss as it dies. Leave the crushed end where you can see it — bent, damp, browned, with a last thin thread rising and thinning to nothing. That thread is the whole story in smoke. A minute ago it looked like something. Now it is litter. Would you fish it out and relight it? Would you put that damp end to clean lips for one more draw? Why not, if it was joy? Is the reluctance proof you have lost something, or proof you have seen it?

That taste is the truth without story. No ninety-second brightness now, only film. The body already wants to rinse it. Let that want stand as witness. The body was never on paper’s side. It coughed on stairs. It soured at meal-end. It woke grey. It knew.

In the hours after this stubbing a faint tug may visit. Hollow. Restless. Edgy at the edges. It may begin within hours. Typically it peaks in the first few days and fades within a few weeks, sharpest around day two or three, then gone. Your hours may run a little early or late. That tug is not you and not proof that paper was your self. It is the dying complain of hunger unfed, already sinking because no belief feeds it.

Have you ever pulled a weed and felt the root shiver in the soil? Did the shiver mean the garden needed the weed, or did it mean the weed was leaving? Is a faint tug evidence of loss, or evidence of exit? If the tug were pleasure leaving, why does the mouth feel cleaner without, and the chest lighter on stairs, and food louder in taste?

You leave it behind easily, immediately and permanently.

Easily because there is nothing to endure once nothing is missed. Immediately because freedom is conferred by the stubbing, not earned by weeks of counting. Permanently because one belief seen plain cannot be unseen unless you invite the old story back and call the invitation proof.

If you feel ill or take prescribed medication, speak to your clinician — I speak of belief here, not medicine.

I smoked through every excuse of body and mood and told myself the body could not manage without. I tell you now from the other side of the table and from watching thousands stub and breathe and taste and sleep and wake: the body manages on its own. It returns to breathing and tasting without being taught. No other glass for the same drink. No counting. No hiding. Go out. Drink what you drink. Stand the cold square. Hear the whole story. Taste to the bottom. Change nothing except paper.

Think of the parking meter we fed all day. Each puff dropped a coin, the needle lifted for minutes, then sank. We stood feeding all day to keep the needle off empty. Did the coins buy parking, or did they buy the right to keep feeding? Who owned the car, and who owned the meter? Now the meter is capped. No more coins. The street is yours to walk.

### THE TWO THOUGHTS THAT WILL KNOCK

Two thoughts will knock in the days ahead. They will arrive in your voice. Hear each now as script and it loses its knock later.

The first knock comes on the bad day. Phone down after sharp words. Papers untouched. Hand half lifting before decision. The old voice says, “Just this once, the day is cracking, one will steady me.”

We all heard it. We all reached on that desk and credited the first puff with calm while the problem sat untouched. Did the puff move one paper? Did it soften one word said? Or did it quiet the pocket for minutes and add sour mouth to worry and call the pause calm? Is the day cracking for lack of paper, or is the pocket cracking for lack of feeding?

Ask without paper helping. If the dose steadied bad days, why did bad days multiply across the years of smoking? If the dose solved, why did the same desk need the same dose daily? Would you ask a man who pushed you into the river to pull you out daily and call him rescuer, or would you see the push at last?

The second knock comes on the bright night. Drink in hand. Laughter. Cold air on the square. A hand extended, kind, “One won’t hurt, you’ve been so good.”

We all stood on that patio and watched lit hands move and feared to be the only one without fire. Did the night give because of fire, or did sun and leisure and company give while paper sneaked a ride? Taste the night air now in memory with and without. Which air carried the laugh to the middle — the air breathed, or the ash tended while talk moved inside and coats took yesterday to wash?

One after months does not stay one. It reteaches the hours. First wall wakes and calls itself memory. Second wall retrains the hands to tap pockets on stairs. Third wall retimes morning and meal-end and break to ask for fire. Fourth wall re-signs night plus drink to mean lighting. Within days the shop queue looks different and the curtains hold yesterday again. Not because you are weak. Because one dose rebuilds the room wall by wall until you stand inside counting again.

Have you tested hundreds already? Did any single lighting stay single because you ordered it to? Or did each single only borrow the next until the row was rebuilt? What would one more test teach that hundreds did not, except how fast the clock winds again?

Hear them now and you will not obey them later. The bad day thought is hunger wearing rescue clothes. The bright night thought is hunger wearing friendship clothes. Both keep the trap alive. You have seen the faces under the coats.

### THE THOUGHT YOU KEEP

You cannot stop a thought arriving by ordering it away. Order the pink animal from the mind and it parades. The way is not to suppress but to answer, so each visit becomes a hit of joy rather than a pang of lack.

In the days ahead a cigarette will cross the mind. At break. At meal-end. On stairs. In the shop queue. On the patio with drink. It will arrive lightly, almost fondly, “Ah, a dose would be nice.” Do not argue. Do not weigh. Do not mourn a friend and do not scold a weak self. Answer with the truth you watched at this table and let joy do the work.

Picture it now so it is ready. Morning feet to cold floor, kettle loud, hands with nothing to pat, mouth clean enough to taste toast to the bottom. Break with air on the face, whole story heard, no door watched. Night with drink laughed to the middle, coat smelling of rain and night, not of fire. The thought crosses like a postcard from a street you no longer live on. You smile because you do not live there.

Then say it inside, loud and glad, as the lifelong script for every such visit:

YIPPEE! I'M FREE!

Say it the first time now, at this table, with the stubbed end before you. Feel how different it feels from dread. No loss in it. No effort in it. Only relief that the feeding has stopped and the hours are yours. Each time the thought returns, it returns pre-labelled. Each answer makes the next answer quicker, until the thought itself means joy.

Does that read as a new chain, or as chains named one by one till the hands are empty? Would a mourner shout with joy, or would a walker out of a locked room into clean morning? Which are you in this minute — the mourner at a grave, or the walker out?

I congratulate you before you light because the seeing is already done. The stubbing only signs it.

12. SMOKE YOUR FINAL CIGARETTE AND KNOW YOU ARE FREE
Close the trap with joy, not sadness.

**SUMMARY**
- The drawer with numbers and shelves and Mondays holds no pleasure, only hunger with new labels
- Readiness is impatience to be done, a wobble before the table means old training, not need
- Watched to the end without story, the last dose shows stain and ash and sour mouth, not a gift
- The faint tug after stubbing peaks early and fades, it proves hunger is leaving, not that paper was self
- One on a bad day leaves problems untouched while quieting the pocket for minutes
- One on a bright night borrows night and drink and company while taking the middle of talk and taste
- A fond thought crossing the mind later gets a glad answer, turning each visit into joy
