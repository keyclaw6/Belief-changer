Chapter 1
THE INVITATION

**IN THIS CHAPTER** — Night kitchen shelf, boxed line on the table, my drawer years, the reading agreement

*You are not about to be lectured about food, you are about to see a con for what it is.*

Have You Been Here Before?

Oh no. Not another book about sugar.

I hear you. That was my thought too when someone first put this method into my hands. Another list of forbidden foods. Another photograph of somebody else's stomach. Another voice telling me I had been bad and must now be good.

Is that why you picked up this book? To be scolded?

Of course not. That is why you are still reading this page. You have been scolded enough.

Let me ask you plainly, reader to reader. Have we all stood in a bright supermarket aisle with a basket over the arm and done that careful arithmetic at the till? “I've managed to be good this week, so a little treat when you feel you deserve one is only fair.” And then the small bar bought for Friday is gone by Thursday, and there is a second wrapper in the bin that we do not quite remember opening?

We have. All of us who lived in the sweet loop have lived that exact evening.

Did you enjoy being told to count every gram, to swap one craving for another craving with a different label, to write down points and sins? Did any of that make you admire food more? Did it make mornings lighter?

No. It made eating feel like school detention.

So let me set your mind at rest on the first page. This is not a diet book. I am not going to tell you to eat less and want it less. I am not going to weigh you, measure you, photograph you, or hand you a meal plan. I will not ask you to punish yourself. I will not ask you to be hard on yourself.

The fact is, you do not need to be hard on yourself to see through a trick. You only need to look clearly for a moment.

Do you want to leave BAD SUGAR behind and still adore eating? Of course you do — that is why you are holding this book. And do you doubt that both can be true at once? Of course you do — every sweet lover doubts it at first. I doubted it. Every person I have helped doubted it.

Keep that doubt beside you. Do not throw it away yet. I am not asking for belief. I am asking for eyes.

This book points to an escape that leaves eating intact. That sentence will sound strange until the picture builds. Let it sound strange for now and keep reading.

What I Mean When I Say Sugar

We cannot talk honestly until we agree what we are talking about. The word sugar has been stretched until it means everything and nothing. One expert means the white powder in tea. Another means all carbohydrate. Another means a piece of fruit. No wonder you feel confused. Confusion keeps you buying.

So I am going to draw one clear line, here, at the start, and I will keep to it for the whole book.

> WHEN I SAY SUGAR IN THIS BOOK I MEAN BAD SUGAR.
>
> BAD SUGAR means refined sugar and added free sugars and junk processed carbs eaten as sweet hits — sugary drinks, confectionery, biscuits, cakes, desserts, sweetened cereals, sweet pastries, ice cream, chocolate bars, and handfuls of starchy snack-carbs grazed in the same way for the same lift.
>
> What I do NOT mean is the natural sweetness inside whole fruit, vegetables, plain milk, and ordinary meals cooked from real ingredients. That food stays. That food is yours. That food was never the problem.

Read that box twice. It matters.

Why draw the line there? Because that is where the loop lives. No one sits at midnight unable to stop after a third apple. No one hides carrot wrappers in the office drawer. No one says, “I feel like i cant stop” about a bowl of plain soup and bread eaten when hungry. The loop lives in the packet, the bottle, the box, the bar — the dose that was built to be nibbled without hunger and to call for another dose an hour later.

Am I saying an apple and a chocolate bar are the same because both contain sweetness? Am I asking you to fear fruit? Am I handing you a chemistry lesson?

No, no, and no. I am saying the opposite. Real food eaten when hungry satisfies hunger. BAD SUGAR eaten when not hungry creates the feeling that another one is needed. One belongs to appetite. The other belongs to the loop.

When this book says sugar from now on, take it to mean BAD SUGAR. That one decision clears away ninety percent of the fog that diets love to sell you. Fruit is not the issue. A home-cooked dinner is not the issue. Your body knows what to do with a meal. What it never asked for was a daily rain of sweet hits between meals, after meals, in place of meals.

Is that distinction clear? Hold it. Everything that follows hangs on it.

Let me add one quiet aside while we are defining terms, spoken plainly and left there. If you live with diabetes or pre-diabetes, take medication that affects blood sugar or appetite, are pregnant, live with an eating disorder past or present, or have any medical condition where changing what you eat could carry risk, talk to your clinician before changing anything and follow their advice first, and use this book for the belief side only. If you ever feel faint, ill, or distressed around food, seek care promptly and put this book aside until you are cleared to continue.

That is not part of the argument. It is simple care. The argument itself needs no white coat. It needs only your own observation.

Notice how much calmer the subject becomes once the line is drawn. We are not talking about food. We are not talking about meals, cooking, hunger, markets, kitchens, grandmothers, harvests. We are talking about packets engineered to be eaten quickly, to dissolve quickly, to leave little trace except the wish for more. Call them by their right name and they lose half their camouflage. Leave real food out of the dock entirely. Real food was never accused.

Do you hear the objection already forming? “Fruit is sugar too so nothing matters.” We will meet that voice where it lives later in the book, with respect, and trace where it learned its lines. For now hold the boxed line. BAD SUGAR is the packet loop. Everything else on your plate is innocent until your own hunger says otherwise.

Why You Are Still Reading

Let me ask you three simple questions. Answer them honestly in your own head. No one is watching.

First: if BAD SUGAR truly made life sweeter, why would a happy sweet lover need a book about leaving it?

Second: if we were freely choosing each dose, why do our hands sometimes move toward the cupboard before we have decided anything?

Third: if moderation worked, why has every rule — weekdays only, weekends only, one square only, the small pack only — ended the same way, with the rule quietly bent and then quietly buried?

You know the answer because you have lived it. We told ourselves we chose. We told ourselves a little fraud on the label was our friend. A tired adult at 10pm told that one biscuit is the free act of an adult mind, while the hand is already reaching and the packet is already half-empty. No blame in that picture. Only a trick that knows our tired hours better than we do.

I am not calling you weak. If every plan you tried collapsed, that says nothing against you.

So why are you holding this book? Not because you love being lectured. You are holding it because some quiet part of you already senses that something has taken hold of you and that the normal explanations do not fit. “I want to get off the roller coaster.” I have heard that sentence in different mouths a thousand times. Different kitchens, same late hour, same low voice.

That sensing is correct. Trust it a little longer.

And here is the heart of this opening: the fact you are still here on page ten, hoping this time is different, means the decision you fear has already been made in the quietest way. You would not be reading if you were content to continue as before. A contented user needs no method, no definition, no invitation. The invitation matters only to someone already looking toward the door. The fact you are holding this book means some part of you already smells freedom and wants a clear view of it.

Do you feel how different that is from a diet? A diet says you must force yourself to want less. This book says you already want a way out and have only lacked a clear picture of what you are walking out from. One starts with your jaw. The other starts with your eyes.

Will you let me show you the picture before you decide anything about your behaviour? Will you allow the possibility that the grey life you fear on the other side is itself part of the fraud? Keep those questions open. You need answer none of them tonight.

My Drawer Years

I need to tell you who is talking to you, or you have no reason to trust a word.

I was not a nutritionist with a perfect kitchen. I was a nightly grazer with a drawer. Top drawer on the right in my kitchen, the one with the spare batteries and the takeaway menus. That is where the biscuits lived. Or rather, where the empty packets lived. I bought with good intentions on Monday. Family pack, because it was better value. By Wednesday the pack breathed out its last crumb. By Thursday I was buying a top-up “for the children,” though my children knew exactly whose hand went in after nine.

We all have our version of that drawer. Ours was not about hunger. Dinner had been eaten. Plates cleared. House quiet. And then came that short term lift then fast drop routine I knew by heart: the bright ten minutes with tea and something sweet, the fog an hour later, the second reach to lift the fog that the first reach had laid down. I called it “sugary somethings to get me through.” Through what? Through an evening that had been perfectly fine until the first dose arrived.

I tried what you tried. Weekday rules. No biscuits in the house — which lasted until someone visited with a tin. Smaller bars. Darker chocolate, as if colour changed the loop. I managed three good weeks once and told everyone how well I felt, while thinking about sweet food every forty minutes. That is not peace. That is a loud argument held behind clenched teeth.

What changed was not my character. I did not find a new hardness. I found an explanation that fitted the facts better than “I lack control.”

I met a man who had walked out of the same loop without a fight and without a diet, who ate with obvious pleasure and never talked about nutrients. He did not lecture me. He asked me to notice two things: what I actually felt in the hour after a dose, and who profited from my belief that I needed it. I watched. I kept eating my meals while I watched. And within days the belief cracked. Not my appetite. My belief.

I ate dinner that week and stopped when I was satisfied without thinking about stopping. I left biscuits on a plate at a party without a speech in my head. I walked past my drawer and felt nothing pulling. Not pride. Not strain. Nothing. The loop had been a noise I mistook for hunger, and when the noise stopped, hunger was still there, plain and friendly as ever.

I have spent the years since helping other sweet lovers see the same thing. Not by coaching their jaws, but by clearing their view. That is my authority here. Not a white coat. A shared drawer, a shared 10pm, and a way out that did not hurt.

Do you believe me yet? You do not need to. I only ask you to keep reading as an investigator, not as a defendant. You are not on trial in this book.

Think of the difference between a defendant and an investigator. A defendant listens for accusation and prepares a defence. An investigator listens for pattern and checks it against the evidence of their own evenings. The defendant asks, “What will they forbid me?” The investigator asks, “Is this true in my drawer, at my desk at three o’clock, in my ‘I-need-to-eat-something-RIGHT-NOW feeling’ that arrives out of nowhere an hour after a sweet lunch?” Be the second person. The truth does not mind being tested. The con minds.

How To Read This Book

Every method has a contract. Diets hide theirs in small print. Mine is on this page in large print, because I want you to hold me to it.

First, read in order. Not because I love rules, but because the picture builds like a safe lock. One tumbler at a time. Skip ahead and you will meet a sentence that sounds strange without the picture beneath it. Stay with me page by page and each step will feel obvious when it arrives.

Second, bring your normal life with you to the pages. Eat your meals. Shop where you shop. Keep the cupboard as it is. Behaviour can wait. Belief comes first. A reader who tries to prove the book by acting early only adds noise. Let the argument do the heavy lifting.

Third, expect to feel two things at once. Expect scoffing — “Too good to be true” — and expect a small lift of hope you are almost embarrassed to feel. Both are normal. Welcome the scoff. I would distrust a reader who swallowed all this whole on page ten. Question me. Test me against your own evenings.

Now the promises, stated plainly so you can hold me to them.

You came here braced for loss. What if the whole movement is gain? I am not offering a smaller life with fewer colours. I am pointing to a larger appetite for real meals, clearer afternoons, money unspent on packets, time unspent in that drawer. Weigh what I ask — a few hours of honest reading — against what you stand to receive, and you will see you have absolutely nothing to lose and everything to gain. That is not sales talk. That is arithmetic. The worst that happens is you close the book unchanged. The best is you close it and never think about BAD SUGAR the same way again.

And the manner of it? You have been told leaving anything must be slow and painful and measured in days survived. I tell you the change in view can happen quietly while you sit in your chair, and the outward change can follow easily, immediately and permanently, because there was never anything real to taper. Does that sound impossible? Of course it does. Every person I helped thought so on page ten and laughed about it on the last page. Hold the impossibility lightly. Let the evidence of your own hours answer it.

All you have to do is follow all the instructions. That sentence is the whole contract in one line. I will not hand you fifty rules about food. I will hand you a handful of simple readings — how to hold your mind while you read — and if you honour them all, the rest assembles itself. Miss one, like a combination missed by one number, and the lock stays shut. Honour them all, and it clicks.

One of those readings concerns eating itself, so let me place it now and remove the fear that this is starvation with a smile. Eat what you want, when you are hungry, stop when you are satisfied, and never punish yourself again. Read it again. That is not a diet. Diets tell you what to want and when to stop wanting it. I tell you to trust hunger and satisfaction, those two old friends you knew before packets taught you to graze by the clock. Eat your dinner. Enjoy bread, soup, vegetables, meat or beans, fruit, whatever real meal you call favourite. Stop when your body says enough. No points. No guilt. No earning your food by being good.

Do you see how different that is from every plan that weighed you? Those plans made food the enemy and left the belief intact. We leave food as friend and examine the belief that a sweet hit adds anything to it. That examination is the escape this book offers, quiet and complete.

Tonight With A Full Cupboard

Picture tonight, because tonight will be ordinary.

The house settles. Plates in the rack. Television murmuring. The shelf above the kettle holds what it always holds — the packet with the bright edge, the tin with the loose lid. You sit with this book. No new shopping list. No bin bag. No announcement to the family. Just you, reading, while life goes on around you exactly as before.

Notice how calm that feels compared with every Monday restart. No dread hovering over tomorrow. No last supper binge because from tomorrow it is forbidden. The con loves last suppers. They make the next dose glow like a farewell lover. We will have none of that here.

In its place we will do something kinder and stranger. We will examine what a dose actually does in the hour after it, not what the wrapper promises before it. We will trace who taught us to call it a treat, a fuel, a reward, a comfort. We will observe why “just one” behaves like a lit match near petrol. We will revisit the bright moments — cinema lights low, birthday candles lit — and ask where the warmth really lived. Each examination is small. Together they change the view.

You did not pick up this book to be punished; you picked it up to find a way out that leaves eating intact. There. I have said it. Eating stays. The packet goes. Not by force, but by sight.

Will you argue with me as we go? Please do — in your head, in the margins, out loud to the empty kitchen. Say, “Rubbish, it helps my concentration.” Say, “You don’t know my afternoons.” Say, “Celebrating with chocolate is love, not a loop.” Bring me your strongest case. I want it. A method that only handles weak cases is no method. Ours handles the strongest and is still standing.

Will you need to memorise anything? Nothing. You will not be examined. If a sentence is true, you will recognise it from your own drawer, your own desk at three o’clock, your own “I-need-to-eat-something-RIGHT-NOW feeling” that arrived out of nowhere an hour after a sweet lunch. Recognition is the only memory this book needs.

And will you need to believe I am clever? I am not clever. I am persistent. I stayed with sweet lovers long enough to hear every justification and to watch each one dissolve when the light hit it. “It gets me through stress.” “Everyone eats it.” “Fruit is sugar too, so nothing matters.” We will meet each of those voices where they live. Not to shame the voice — the voice is yours and it deserves respect — but to trace where it learned its lines.

For now, tonight, only this: keep reading. Keep eating your meals when hungry. Keep noticing. Let the cupboard be. Notice what a relief that calm is compared with the old Monday panic. The decision you fear has already been made in the quietest way — by the very fact you are still here, hoping this time is different. It is different, because this time we do not start with your jaw. We start with your eyes.
