Chapter 8
WHO BUILT THE WANT

**IN THIS CHAPTER** — The bright aisle at child eye-level, the checkout ambush, the mirror that says personality, the open cell door

*Your want was built for you, and your fear was built with it — neither proves you need BAD SUGAR.*

The Aisle That Wanted For You

Walk with me down the aisle where you do your ordinary shop.

You came in for bread and milk. The light is bright. The shelves rise higher than your head. At your trolley handle, small hands would reach first, because the bright packs sit low on purpose. Cartoon tigers grin. Purple monsters dance. A bar the size of your thumb wears a cape and promises power. Further on, biscuits in family armour line up in twenty shades of gold. Bottles sweat. Every pack shouts crunch, goo, melt, new.

You tell yourself you chose. I know that story because we all lived it.

Stand still and watch what chose for you. Food chemists in white coats spent months tuning crunch against melt, salt against sweet, till the bite hits a peak your mouth cannot argue with. Designers tuned the rustle, the colour, the animal eyes that follow a child down the row. Managers paid for the end of the row, for the tower by the till, for the small bars at your elbow while you queue tired with a basket on your arm. Nothing there is accident. Nothing there is hunger answering hunger.

When did a peach ever hire a tiger to beg for you? When did soup ever pay to ambush you at the till? When did plain bread ever need a cartoon to be wanted?

You know the honest answers. The packs that need the loudest shouting are the ones your body never asked for.

We walked that aisle together for years, you and I. We went in steady and came out with a bag that rattled. We told ourselves everyone eats it so it must be fine. We called the extra pack a little reward for strain. We carried it home and called the carrying choice.

The fact is, the want arrived before you did. You were taught it before you could read, then engineered at every turn after you could. Parents slipped a sweet for quiet and called it kindness. Schools gave stars and sugar for marks. Screens tied chocolate to laughter and love and funny evenings. Then the makers tuned the dose to hold you — a lift sharp enough to notice, a dip fast enough to bring you back within the hour, leaving you flat, foggy and never satisfied and pointing you to the next pack.

That last line is not poetry. It is the state you logged at the desk and at seven in the evening. Bright for ten minutes. Coated for hours. Hunting again before the wrapper settles.

I place no blame on you for wanting in that aisle. You were conned in that aisle. I speak harshly of the makers who tuned the con, never of the shopper they aimed it at. A lone shopper was set against armies of tuning, testing, colour, placement. No wonder the hand moved. The hand was led.

You were never weak in that aisle. You were caught in the Sugar Trap.

“Mine Is Different”

“But mine is different,” you say. “My family all have a sweet tooth. I have always been the addictive type. Other people can take one. I feel like i cant stop.”

Let that voice speak fully. I welcome it, because it kept me dosing long after joy left.

Look at it as an investigator, not as a prisoner.

If your nature caused the binge, why did the binge only start after regular dosing began? Did you empty boxes at six years old before sweet became daily? Did you hide wrappers before allow-then-forbid taught you to? If personality drove it, why do you take one slice of bread without chasing the loaf, one handful of peanuts without emptying the jar, yet one biscuit carries the box? Same person. Same character. Opposite behaviour. The variable was never your soul. The variable was the on-off pattern plus the dose.

The pattern itself drives the binge. Allow, forbid, crave, permit — under that wheel, binges follow the way night follows a switched-off lamp. Feed the wheel daily and the wheel turns daily. Starve the belief that turns it and the wheel has no reason to turn.

And you are not odd in this. On surveys that use addiction-like checklists for rich food, around 14 in 100 adults and 12 in 100 children tick enough boxes — around the same level as for drink and cigarettes. That is people answering questions, not doctors giving a diagnosis, and I cite it for one reason only: you are not uniquely broken. The trap is common because the building of it was common.

Watch how common shows in your own mouth. The first bar charmed. Then two were needed for the same note. Then the family pack, eaten standing, half turned away. That crowding — needing more for the same effect — is not proof of a flawed self. It is the learned trap doing what tuned traps do: blunt the note while sharpening the cue. Smell fires before the bite. Wrapper fires before the bite. Three o’clock fires before the bite. The bell rings louder as the pleasure thins. Bell plus low underneath plus the old reading, and feet turn to the machine.

Ask the bell its plain questions.

If smell compelled use, why do walkers fresh from a hungry meal pass the bakery and keep walking?

If three o’clock compelled dosing, why did the hungry morning pass noon without urgency?

If tiredness needed sweet, why does sleep answer tiredness while sweet only postpones it an hour and charges fog?

You know. Cues fire from habit. They do not compel. Hands move on belief.

Your traits did not build the want. The want was built, then your traits were blamed for it. It is the other way around.

The Treat That Predicts The Low

There is a harder fact to set beside the aisle, and I will set it flat because you deserve plain speech.

Men who ate more sweet food day after day were more likely to feel low years later, even when strain and habit were accounted for. The daily treat did not fix mood. The daily treat predicted later low mood.

I am not telling you this will happen to you.

Hear how I use it. I do not want you changing because you are frightened. Fear never freed anyone; fear only tightens the grip it claims to loosen. I tell you because it completes the inversion you already lived: the rescuer was the perpetrator. The lift you bought for gloom laid down the next gloom. The comfort you bought for strain charged you strain with interest.

Test it against afternoons you have lived.

When did a dosed afternoon ever close clear? You dosed at three, buzzed at three ten, fogged at four, reached again at four thirty. When did an undosed hungry meal ever behave that way? You ate hungry at noon, closed satisfied, worked level till six. Which one steadied mood? Which one scheduled the dip?

You know. The dose never steadied. It borrowed from tomorrow to paint today, then sent the bill.

The body you carry is a precision machine, not a dull bucket to fill. Its warning feelings are dials, not faults. True hunger rises slow, welcomes many foods, holds quiet for hours when met. The sweet low arrives sharp, wants sweet only, vanishes ten minutes after a square and returns sharper within the hour. One is information. The other is debt coming due. Unscrewing a dial does not fix the engine. Reading the dial does.

Do not change from fear of a dark year. Change from seeing that the bright packet never gave what it claimed. The gloom you feared without it was gloom you only felt with it.

The Open Door You Fear

Now we come to the fear that holds even convinced readers, because it wears wisdom’s clothes.

“I might fail. And if I succeed, who will I be at birthdays, at late tables, on tired evenings? Life without sweet will be grey.”

I know both ropes because I pulled both. One rope says you will crawl back. The other says you will stand outside the warmth. Pull either and the middle burns your hands. That burning has a name you will now carry in ordinary speech. That burning is the tug-of-war of craving and fear.

See the shape first in a picture you cannot unsee.

A cell door stands open. Light falls across the floor. Food, work, talk wait outside. The prisoner sits on the bunk, hands round knees, begging to stay. Outside is danger, he says. Outside I will fail. Outside I will be no one. He calls his fear wisdom. He calls the cell safety.

In the next cage, the door also stands open. The lion in it was freed that morning. He paces out two steps, turns, paces back, lies down by the bars from habit, not need. No chain holds him. Only the groove of years moves his feet.

We were both prisoners. We were both lions.

The prisoner’s dread does not prove the cell feeds him. The lion’s pacing does not prove the cage nourishes him. Both prove habit plus a story, nothing more.

Ask the door its questions.

If the cell protected you, why did you need this book in your hand to defend staying in it?

If the cage fed you, why did fed days without BAD SUGAR leave you clearer than dosed days with it?

If outside were grey, why were the brightest hours you lately lived — market bag heavy, mouth clean, hunger met, full stop reached — bright without a wrapper?

You know. Both ropes belong to the same hands. Both fears were installed by the same builder who installed the want. Fear of failing says the grumble will grow forever. It will not; it peaks in days and thins. Fear of succeeding says joy lived in the packet. It did not; joy lived in the scene, the company, the hunger met, while the packet only sneaked a ride.

Picture the two rests of life laid side by side. Keep dosing to hold fear off, and you buy fear for the rest of your life — daily bargaining, nightly rustle, mornings coated. See the con and let the door stand open, and you walk level mornings for the rest of your life — shops passed without debate, meals closed without hunting, evenings held without pacing.

No new fear is needed here. The old fears collapse the moment you see whose hands hold the ropes.

A small guard belongs here, spoken plain. Your body can cope with an accidental crumb the way a seatbelt can take a sudden jolt. The belt is there for the jolt, never for steering into walls. Talk to your clinician first if you live with diabetes, take medication that affects blood sugar or appetite, are pregnant, live with an eating disorder past or present, or carry medical risk around food. Guard the belief, and the odd jolt touches nothing.

Do not test the door by peeking back into the cell to prove you are strong. Strength was never the question. Seeing is. Walk out, keep walking, let the pacing thin the way the bakery smell thinned when unfollowed. Within days the feet learn the new groove: past the tower without slowing, past the till without reaching, home with bread and milk and nothing rattling.

The cell never fed. The open air was never grey.

SUMMARY
- The want was tuned, placed, and advertised long before your hand moved in the aisle.
- Emptying the box after one does not prove a flawed character; the on-off wheel drives the binge.
- Many ordinary shoppers answer the same way on checklists, which means common building, not personal weakness.
- Needing bigger packs for the same note sits beside sharper cues, and cues fire from habit without compelling the hand.
- A daily sweet habit goes with later low mood rather than fixing mood, as your own lift-then-dip afternoons show.
- Hunger welcomes many foods and holds quiet for hours, while debt wants sweet only and returns within the hour.
- Both fears are ropes held by the same builder, and an open door needs no courage once seen as open.
