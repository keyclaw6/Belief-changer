Chapter 10
NO SAFE SWEET

IN THIS CHAPTER
The Friday pudding saved all week. The cliff with a promised short fall. The box that empties after one. The clean cupboard. The sweetener packet. The seatbelt in the family car.

*There is no small sweet that leaves the Trap unfed.*

THE FRIDAY PUDDING

You know this week by heart because we all lived it.

Monday morning you lay down the rule. No BAD SUGAR in the house. Clean eating all week. Friday night you may have the pudding. Just Friday. Just one. A deserved treat at the end of a good week.

Monday feels almost noble. Tuesday drags a little. By Wednesday the pudding has a glow it never had when you ate daily. You picture it. You talk about it. You tell a friend, “I’ve managed to be good this week,” and the friend nods and the pudding grows brighter in your head. Thursday night you open the fridge for milk and you see the space where it will sit tomorrow and your mouth waters.

Friday arrives. You buy the pudding with ceremony. You carry it home like treasure. You eat it slowly at first, then fast. You close your eyes. For ten bright minutes the mouth floods. Then the coat settles. Then the tug for more starts under the ribs before the spoon is dry.

So you tell yourself Thursday was the price and Saturday will be the clean start again. Yet Saturday morning the mouth remembers the flood. Saturday afternoon the hand drifts toward the shop. “Well, yesterday was Friday,” the voice says, “and today is almost Friday if you count the week as a whole.” By Saturday night there is a second pudding, eaten standing up, no ceremony. By Sunday there is chocolate “to get through Sunday.” By Monday the rule is laid down again, louder, with a new Friday circled.

We called that control. We called that balance.

Look at what the week actually did. Did Friday taste better because you waited? Or did waiting paint Friday brighter while making Monday to Thursday duller, flatter, harder to meet? Did the rule shrink the want? Or did the rule feed the want for six days so the seventh day could barely hold it?

Ask the week its plain questions.

If Friday was truly a small joy, why did six sweet-free days feel grey beside it rather than clear on their own?

If you truly owned the pudding, why did the pudding own your thoughts from Tuesday onward?

If cut-down truly loosened the knot, why did the knot pull tighter every Monday you tied it?

You know the honest answers because you lived that week more times than you can count. The glow around Friday was not proof that Friday sweets are precious. The glow was proof that starving a want for six days makes the seventh dose shout. That is how the Sugar Trap keeps its grip.

A caged hunger always dreams of the feast. An uncaged body never thinks of it. The Friday rule builds the cage and calls the dreaming proof that the feast is love. It is the other way around. The rule makes the dose precious. The precious makes the rule fail. The fail makes you blame yourself. The blame makes you lay down a stricter rule. Round and round we went, year after year, always certain next Friday would be different.

There is no Friday that stays Friday. There is only a six-day advertisement for a ten-minute flood that calls again within the hour.

JUMP A LITTLE WAY OFF A CLIFF

A friend walks you to the edge of a cliff on a bright morning. White chalk. Gulls. A long drop to black water. He points down and says, with a kind smile, “Jump. You will only fall a few metres. I promise you will stop in mid-air. Just a short fall. A safe fall. A Friday fall.”

You would stare at him. You would check his face for drink or fever. You would say, “What do you mean, a short fall? Falling is falling. You cannot arrange to fall a little way off a cliff.”

He shrugs. “Other people manage it. Be sensible. Use willpower. Just enjoy the rush of the first metres.”

You would walk away. No one you love would jump on those terms. The request is absurd on its face. Falling does not do short by arrangement.

Yet we all accepted the same arrangement with BAD SUGAR and called it sensible. “I will have dessert on Fridays.” “I will keep it to birthdays.” “I will allow just one square.” A short fall, arranged in advance, off a cliff we had already fallen off a hundred times.

I am warm toward you and harsh toward the con that taught you to talk like that. No one taught you physics that way. No one told you fire burns only on Fridays. With BAD SUGAR alone we learned to speak of cliffs as if they had shelves halfway down put there for polite users.

There are no shelves. There is the edge and the drop.

You say, “But I did manage three weeks once.” Yes. And where did those three weeks live? In thought about the fourth week. In dreams of the saved-up dose. In a mouth that rehearsed Friday while the eyes tried to work. That is not standing safely on the edge. That is leaning over it for three weeks and calling leaning balance.

You say, “But some people manage one.” Look closely at those people. Watch the eyes during the meal. Watch the hand after. One permitted dose either sits dull and faintly disappointing — sweet, cloying, oddly narrow after all the dreaming — or it floods and calls for its brother within the hour. If it sits dull, why keep paying six days of thought for dull? If it floods, what was safe about it? Either way the cliff took its payment, in disappointment or in craving.

The fact is, cut-down never means less sweet in your life. Cut-down means more sweet in your mind. The dose you skip lives in your head all day. The dose you allow lives in your mouth for minutes and in your want for hours. You pay twice for every crumb: once in waiting, once in wanting more.

Would you jump off that cliff provided you fell only a few metres? Then why sign the same bargain with a dose that has never once kept to a few metres?

JUST ONE

Let us look at the smallest bargain of all, because it sounds the most reasonable.

“I will have just one biscuit.”

We all said it at 10pm with the kettle on. One biscuit on a saucer. Grown-up. Civilized. The rest of the box closed, the lid pressed, the box pushed to the back.

You eat the one. It is sweet. It is soft. For a minute the mouth floods. Then the flood thins. A coat stays. Under the ribs a small hollow opens that was not there before the biscuit. The hand knows before the head decides. Wrapper rustle and the box empty before the swallow ends. Then the quiet standing in the kitchen with crumbs on the front and the old line running through the head: “I just crave sweets and when i eat them, i feel like i cant stop.”

I do not quote that line to label you. I quote it because we all lived it and it tells the truth about the dose, not about your character. The loss of control is the signature of the Trap, not a verdict on you. One lights the next because the first laid the hollow the second is hired to close.

Ask the saucer its questions.

If one truly satisfied, why did one ever lead to the box empty?

If you truly lacked will, why did you show iron will all week at work, with bills, with children, with night shifts, yet one biscuit moved a hand you had sworn was yours?

If the first biscuit truly gave pleasure, why did pleasure thin with every biscuit after, while the urge thickened?

You know. The first dose does not close a need from the day. The first dose opens a need for the hour. Each square quiets the coat laid by the square before, then lays a fresh coat for the next. Ten minutes of flood, bought with an hour of call.

I lived that saucer for years. I put one biscuit out to prove I was in charge. I ate the one to prove the book wrong. I stood at the cupboard at 10:40pm with the lid off and sugar on my cuff and proved only that the dose, once in, talks louder than the promise made before it. That hollow is the Nibbler dying, and it passes — yet feed it one and you have hired it for another night.

One is never one. One is the order for the box, placed in sweet coating.

THE EMPTY HOUSE TRICK

“But I don’t keep it in the house,” you say. “Surely that proves I am free. No biscuits in the cupboard. No bars in the drawer. Clean shelves.”

I hear the pride in that voice because I used that voice. I emptied the cupboard on Mondays and stood back like a general. No BAD SUGAR under my roof. Problem solved.

Then watch what a clean cupboard actually did in our lives.

Clean cupboard Monday. Clean cupboard Tuesday. Wednesday at 3pm the desk drawer calls. Wednesday night the garage shop glows on the drive home. Thursday the office birthday cake sits under plastic and the hand that was so stern at home shakes over cake among colleagues. Friday the child’s leftover party bag sits on the hall table and the fingers dip “to tidy.” Saturday the “no house” rule buys a large bar “for guests” that no guest ever sees whole.

The house was clean. The mind was crowded. We thought of sweet in the shop, at work, in the car, at the till, on the high street, in other people’s kitchens. We congratulated ourselves on empty shelves while queuing at midnight garages in the rain.

Does an empty cupboard prove the want is gone? Or does it prove the want moved outdoors? If the house were truly the problem, why did the hand find sweet everywhere the house was not?

And look at the other side. A house with BAD SUGAR in it never troubled a body that saw through the con. Bread in the bin, fruit in the bowl, cheese, eggs, chicken, beans, rice — and a bright box on the shelf for others — can sit untouched for weeks when no belief calls it treasure. Not from grit. From lack of wish. The box is loud only while the belief says it holds joy. Kill the belief and the box goes quiet, in the house or out of it.

I am not asking you to stock or to clear. Clearing cupboards never freed anyone, and stocking them never trapped anyone by itself. The Trap lives in the belief, not in the postcode of the packet. Move the packets and you move the scenery. Leave the belief and the belief brings the packets back through rain, through night shops, through other people’s drawers.

Ask the clean shelves their question. If empty shelves meant freedom, why did the evenings out feel more hunted than the evenings in ever did?

You were never unfree because packets sat near you. You felt hunted because a voice said packets held something you needed. That Friday voice is the Sweet Con talking, not you.

TOMORROW AND AFTER THE BIRTHDAY

There is a softer bargain, kindly spoken, that kept me dosing longer than Fridays ever did.

“I will quit tomorrow. After the birthday. After the holidays. After this hard week.”

Tomorrow sounds gentle. It sounds wise. It lets you read on without dread. It lets you enjoy tonight’s dose as a farewell. It feels like self-kindness.

Watch tomorrow at work across a real year.

Tomorrow after the birthday becomes tomorrow after the office party. Tomorrow after the office party becomes tomorrow after Christmas. Tomorrow after Christmas becomes “January is bleak, start February.” February brings a bad week, March brings a wedding, April brings Easter eggs “for the children,” May brings holidays “when routine returns.” The calendar is always full of doors that promise a better start beyond them. We walked through twelve months of doors and never stepped through the one marked now.

And what does tomorrow do to today? Tonight’s dose, billed as the last, gets extra glow. You eat slower, sadder, more attentively, memorizing a flood you claim to be leaving. The farewell makes the dose precious. The precious makes tomorrow harder. Tomorrow, frightened by tonight’s farewell, asks for one more farewell. Round it goes, farewell after farewell, each more tender than the last.

Ask tomorrow its questions.

If tomorrow were truly easier, why has every tomorrow you ever set arrived harder, with more ritual around the dose, not less?

If the birthday truly needed sweet, why did birthdays you remember clearest hold their warmth in faces, songs, candles, talk — while the coated birthdays blur into foil and fog?

If tonight’s farewell truly closed the story, why did the story always need another farewell the next night?

You know. Tomorrow never comes because tomorrow is the Trap speaking in your own voice. The Trap loves tomorrow. Tomorrow keeps today dosing while keeping hope alive enough to stop you looking closely today.

I speak plainly because I wasted two years on tomorrow. Two Christmases of “after this.” Three birthdays of “just this table.” Count your own tomorrows and add their price in money, in hours, in midnight mouths, in mornings started heavy. Not to frighten you. To show that delay, not the dose, was the dearest item on the bill.

There will never be a perfect week to see clearly. There is only this week, with its ordinary shops, its ordinary meals, its ordinary evenings. Seeing does not need a quiet calendar. Seeing needs open eyes in a noisy one.

THE SWAP THAT KEEPS YOU HOOKED

When Fridays fail and tomorrows fade, the mind reaches for a cleverer door.

“I will wean. I will taper. I will swap to the light version, the diet drink, the sweetener packet, the ‘healthy’ bar. Same sweetness, less harm.”

Wean sounds scientific. Swap sounds canny. Both keep the same belief alive under new paper.

Watch weaning at work. Half a spoon rather than two. One square rather than four. A smaller bar, a thinner slice. What fills the gap between small doses? Thought of the next small dose. The day becomes a row of tiny waits with tiny floods between. The mouth never clears. The hollow never closes. Each miniature flood lays its miniature coat and calls for its brother by mid-afternoon. You think more about sweet while eating less of it, which the Trap loves, because thought is its true food.

And watch swapping. Diet drink for cola. Sweetener in tea for sugar in tea. “No-sugar” bar for chocolate bar. The packet changes. The act stays: sweet on the tongue when the hollow bites, sweet as payment for strain, sweet as signal that the hour can continue. The tongue still learns that low means seek sweet. The hand still learns to reach. The mind still says sweet fixes afternoons. You have changed the uniform and kept the army.

Ask the swap its questions.

If the sweetener truly broke the loop, why does the hand still reach at the same hour, with the same urgency, for the same relief?

If the light version truly satisfied, why does the evening still end with grazing, with hunting, with “something sweet to close the day”?

If weaning truly loosened the knot, why did smaller doses make the want louder rather than quieter?

You know. The loop never lived in calories or grams. The loop lived in the belief that sweet answers low. Keep sweet answering low and you keep the loop, whatever the label says. Swap the product and you remain a user, still hooked, still paying thought all day for minutes of flood.

I do not ask you to judge packets by harm. Harm talk never freed anyone; it only starts arguments you cannot win in the shop aisle. Judge by use. It does plenty TO you. It does nothing FOR you. If it does nothing for you often, it does nothing for you occasionally either. If it does nothing for you as sugar, it does nothing for you as sweetener wearing a thinner coat. The pincer closes. There is nowhere left for the “safe one” to stand.

Some voices will call this extreme. “Surely one small diet drink is nothing. Surely you fanatics see poison everywhere.” Hear that voice as information about method, not about you. The voice defends the belief that sweet holds joy. It must call totality extreme, because totality leaves the belief nowhere to hide. Totality is not harshness. Totality is clarity: no dose carries joy, so no dose needs a smaller cousin to carry joy more safely.

THIS IS FANATICISM, SURELY?

Let that sharpest whisper speak fully, because it speaks against this book, not against sweet, and it deserves a plain answer.

“You tell me one biscuit keeps the whole Trap alive. You tell me Fridays fail, tomorrows lie, swaps hook. You leave me no corner. That is all-or-nothing tyranny. Normal people moderate. You brainwash the other way.”

I welcome the charge because I made it myself with a pencil in my teeth.

Look at the difference between tyranny and sight.

Tyranny arrives before evidence and forbids checking. It says trust me, do not test, obey the rule while wanting the thing. That was the Friday rule. That was the clean-cupboard rule. That was every diet that told you to hold your breath while keeping the belief that the dose was treasure. Of course those felt like tyranny. They were.

What I invite is the opposite. Run your own weeks as evidence. Run the Friday week and log Tuesday thought, Thursday mouth, Friday flood, Saturday hunt. Run the weaning week and log hourly thought. Run the swap week and log whether the hand still reaches at 3pm. Then run a week where hunger is met with hungry meals, mouth clean, full stop reached without counting, and log thought, mouth, sleep, morning head. Compare patterns, not promises.

A trick needs you not to check. This method needs you to check with your own mouth, your own shop, your own kitchen. Installed belief told you sweet means love, means through, means deserved, before you could spell. Observed fact shows flood, then coat, then call, whatever the day, whatever the packet. One voice says do not test. The other says test tonight and see. The difference is not subtle once you live it.

And “normal people moderate” — look at normal. Look at the aisle at 6pm. Look at the till towers, the family packs, the midnight garages. Look at the roller coaster you named yourself when the low bit: short term lift then fast drop, then reach again. If that is moderation working, why do moderators think of sweet all day, pay all week, hunt all evening? Normal, in a conned culture, only means commonly trapped. Common never meant free.

No corner is left because no corner ever held joy. That is not tyranny. That is arithmetic.

THE SEATBELT

“I understand totality,” you say, “but what if I slip? What if a crumb lands by accident? A sauce sweetened without asking. A bite taken before the head catches up. Does one crumb throw me back to the start?”

No. And understanding why will guard you better than any vow to be perfect.

Think of the seatbelt in your family car. You click it every trip, not because you plan to crash, but because roads jolt. The belt sits quiet across the chest, unnoticed for miles. When a van brakes hard ahead and you brake hard behind, the belt locks, holds, then releases. You do not get out and drive into the next lamppost to test it. You do not say, “The belt held, so erratic driving is safe.” You thank the belt silently and drive straight on, more attentive, not less.

Your body works the same way with an accidental crumb. A sweetened sauce at a friend’s table. A mouthful taken in the dark before you taste properly. The body absorbs the jolt, steadies, moves on. The crumb lays a brief coat, a brief stir, then passes, unless the mind grabs the wheel and steers into the ditch.

The danger was never the crumb. The danger is the sentence the mind builds on the crumb: “I’ve blown it, so I may as well carry on.” “One means the week is lost.” “That tasted good, so Fridays must be real after all.” Those sentences revive nothing in the body; they revive the belief. Guard the belief and the crumb stays a jolt. Let the belief back and the crumb becomes a steering wheel turned hard toward the ditch.

So hear the margin plain. Your body can ride over an accidental blip. Your mind cannot afford a deliberate one. Deliberate means the belief is back in charge: sweet holds something, this time is special, one will prove control. Accidental means life jolted and you drive on. If you live with diabetes, take medication that affects blood sugar or appetite, are pregnant, live with an eating disorder past or present, or carry medical risk around food, talk to your clinician first and follow their advice.

A slip, if it ever comes, is a rumble strip, not a crash. It says slow down, look, remember what the dose does. Feel the coat, notice the call within the hour, smile that you saw it, drive straight on with a clean mouth and a hungry meal at the next hunger. Never use the belt as permission to drive wildly. Never let one jolt license a second taken on purpose.

Totality lives in the mind, not in panic about crumbs. Keep no special sweet on purpose, and accidents lose all power to move you.
