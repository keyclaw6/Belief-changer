Chapter 3
WHAT DOES IT DO FOR YOU

*Forget what it costs you for a moment — name me one true thing it ever gave you.*

The Wrong Courtroom

We have spent years arguing the wrong case in our own heads.

We say, I know it harms me, but I enjoy it. We say, it rots teeth and thickens waists, but it gets me through. We say, too much is bad, but a little is my pleasure. Harm on one side, benefit on the other, scales held up trembling while we add a stone to each side and hope the pleasure side stays down.

I know that courtroom well. I sat in it for years. I could list the charges against BAD SUGAR the way a tired clerk reads a file. Money gone. Afternoons fogged. Teeth drilled. Trousers tight. Evenings lost to a packet. And still I voted for the prisoner. Still I said, yes, but it helps. Yes, but I love it. Yes, but life would be grey without it.

Do you hear how strange that verdict is? A thing may harm yet still be loved. That is possible. A fire may warm and burn. The question this chapter puts to you is simpler and far more dangerous to the trap. Not, does it harm more than it helps. But, what help is there at all?

The fact is, harm was never the issue. If a thing gave a real gift, we could talk about price. If a thing gave nothing, price talk is a trick to keep us from seeing the emptiness.

We were conned into weighing. Weighing assumes two weights. Take away one weight and the scales fall over. That is what we will do together, quietly, with your own day as witness.

You came out of the last room seeing you were caught, not weak. Keep that seeing beside you now. A caught person does not ask, is the pit deep or shallow. A caught person asks, what did the pit ever do for me that the open path did not do better.

And let me ask you plain, investigator to investigator. Do you want to pay out money and mood and teeth for the rest of your life for a thing that hands you nothing back? Hold that threat beside its opposite. Picture mornings that start clear and stay clear for the rest of your life because nothing was taken and nothing is missed. Same phrase, two futures. Which one describes a gift, and which one describes a bill?

The weighing habit runs deep. We learned it from every diet sheet and every dentist poster. Harm here, pleasure there, be sensible, balance it out. The habit feels honest. It feels adult. It feels like facing facts. Yet notice what the habit smuggles in before argument starts. It grants the prisoner a virtue no one has proved. It places benefit on the scales as if benefit were certain, and then invites you to argue about size. Once you accept that placement, you have already lost, because you will spend your life adjusting the dose to manage harm while never questioning the gift.

I want you to refuse that placement. Not with anger. With curiosity. With the calm of a shopkeeper who asks for a receipt. Show me the goods, you say. Not the packet. Not the promise on the packet. The goods in my hands on Tuesday. What did I hold that I did not already hold before I opened it?

That question changes the air in the room. Harm talk makes us defensive. We brace. We list excuses. We prepare to be scolded. Benefit talk makes us observant. We look. We remember. We check. A scolded person hides the drawer. An investigator opens it.

So open it. Bring your ordinary Tuesday, not your worst Christmas, not your best holiday. Bring the day you call normal, moderate, under control. That is the day the con must defend, because if the con cannot defend Tuesday, it cannot defend anything.

The Day With Two Columns

Take an ordinary day. Not a bad day. Not Christmas. Tuesday.

Morning tea with two sugars. Biscuit with it because the tea is hot and the packet is there. Mid-morning bar from the drawer because work thickens. Lunch with a sweet drink because the meal deal makes it cheap. Afternoon biscuits, three, because the eyes blur at the screen. Chocolate on the way home because the train drags. Dessert after dinner because the day deserved a close. A handful late because the box is open and the house is quiet.

We have all lived that Tuesday. I lived a thousand of them. I could tell you the rustle of each hour without looking at a clock.

Now lay that Tuesday out the way a shopkeeper lays out accounts. Left column what it did TO you. Right column what it did FOR you.

Left column fills fast. Money out, coin by coin, till receipt long as an arm by Friday. Time out, minutes standing and chewing and clearing and regretting. Mouth sticky, teeth filmed, breath sweet-sour. Belly full yet oddly empty an hour later. Mood up for ten minutes, then flat, then foggy, then snapping for more. Evening gone to grazing while the television talks to itself. Sleep heavy and waking groggy.

Follow the money for a minute, because money does not lie to spare feelings. One bar here, one bottle there, one family pack that was meant to last the week and lasts till Thursday. Small coins. Nothing dramatic. Add them across Tuesday, then across the month, then across the year. The sum embarrasses the word treat. A treat is a small brightness. This is a quiet tax paid daily for the right to feel flat by four. Keep the receipt from the till and look at its length on Friday. That length is not decoration. That length is hours worked to buy fog.

Follow the minutes too. The walk to the shop. The queue. The unwrapping. The chewing while standing. The clearing. The second walk because the first dose did not hold. Ten minutes here, fifteen there. Across Tuesday the minutes gather into an hour you never chose to spend. Across a year they gather into weeks. No one told you the price included time. The packet told you the price was coins.

Follow the mouth. Morning film after tea. Midday stick after drink. Afternoon coat after biscuits. Evening ache after chocolate. Teeth that feel furred by nine. Breath you cover with your hand. A mouth washed again and again by sweet, never left clean long enough to rest. You know that mouth because you have lived in it.

Follow the mood hour by hour, without theories, only notes. Nine bright. Ten busy. Eleven thinning. Twelve reaching. One fed and brief lift. Two sinking. Three blurred. Four reaching again. Five short lift. Six drag home. Seven sweet close. Eight grazing. Nine fog. Ten hollow rattle in the box and puzzlement. That is not a day helped. That is a day ridden up and down by doses.

You know that list because you have paid it. Add it honestly. Do not soften it and do not swell it. Just write what Tuesday cost in cash, in minutes, in mouth-feel, in mood.

Now the right column. The benefit column. The column where the gift should sit.

Write there, in plain words, what BAD SUGAR did FOR you on Tuesday that food and drink and rest and company and the day itself did not do.

“I would have something quick and sweet but now know that will only give a short term lift which will inevitably be followed by a fast drop.” I have heard that sentence from recovered sweet eaters in different kitchens, same tired honesty. They thought they were describing fuel. They were describing a loan with instant interest.

“treat when you feel you deserve one.” Write that in the right column if you like. “sugary somethings to get me through.” Write that too. “celebrating with chocolate.” Write that. Fill the column with your own phrases, your own voice, no editing.

Then look at the column the way you would look at a drawer.

Does the afternoon dose sit there as energy, or as ten bright minutes followed by two dull hours and a second reach? Does the evening box sit there as comfort, or as standing up to eat without tasting while half turned away? Does the birthday chocolate sit there as love, or as love with a wrapper held in front of it? Does the cinema sweet sit there as fun, or as fun with a bill stapled to it?

Bring me your strongest item. Say, “Rubbish, mine really does help me in the afternoon.” Good. Keep that item on the table. Say, “You do not know my hard days, mine is a reward.” Good. Keep that one too. Say, “Mine is love, mine is family, mine is the one bright thing.” Good. The truth does not mind being tested. Only a con minds being looked at.

Ask yourself three small questions, and answer only from Tuesdays you have lived.

First, if each dose is a gift, why does the gift need repeating every two hours to keep its paper on?

Second, if each dose is tasted and loved, why do we so often eat the second half without tasting, already regretting while still chewing?

Third, if Tuesday was helped, why does Wednesday start tired, flat, and calling for the same help again?

You know the answer because Tuesday taught it to you. A gift that leaves you emptier after unwrapping is not a gift. A help that must be helped again by noon is not help.

Notice how the audit shifts the burden. Before, you had to prove harm outweighed pleasure. That burden is heavy, and the trap loves heavy burdens, because heavy burdens make you quit the inquiry. Now the trap must prove benefit exists at all. That burden is light for truth and impossible for a con. Watch which side sweats.

And notice what Tuesday proves about moderation. The Tuesday we audited was not a binge day. It was a managed day. Counted doses. Small packs. Sensible rules. Yet the left column still crowded and the right column still stood empty. That emptiness was not caused by excess. Excess only enlarged a pattern already present in the managed dose. Take the smallest Tuesday you can build, one biscuit, one tea, nothing late, and run the same two columns. The sums shrink. The shape holds. Cost shrinks but stays real. Gift shrinks to nothing and stays nothing. A smaller nothing is still nothing.

What Teeth And Guidance Tell You

Let me speak plain about the body stakes, because soft talk would insult you, and then we will set fear aside where it belongs.

Frequent free-sugar doses feed the plaque acid that rots teeth. That acid sits on enamel after each sweet wash, and cavity is the price paid in chairs with bright lights. Brushing matters. Fluoride matters. Meal pattern matters. A mouth is not a single equation. And still, dose after dose through the day keeps that acid fed. That is a real stake, lived by millions, paid tooth by tooth.

Think of the dentist mirror held up on its stick. The small tap. The pause. The quiet catch at the edge of a molar where the probe holds. No lecture. Just a soft spot where hard enamel should be. That soft spot did not arrive from one birthday cake. It arrived from Tuesday repeated — wash after wash, acid after acid, film never long cleared before the next coat. Teeth cannot grow back their cover once that cover thins. A drilled tooth stays drilled.

The public guidance draws a line for free sugars below a tenth of daily energy, near fifty grams, with a lower mark near half that where further benefit lies. Ordinary eating now sails far past both marks without noticing. Feeling fine does not mean intake is low. Sweet has crept into bread and drink and sauce and snack, so a person can pass the ceiling by lunch and call the day moderate.

I speak here of common risk, not a sentence on you — and if you live with any medical risk around food, your clinician’s word comes first.

Do you feel the chill in those facts? Good. Feel it fully for a moment. Teeth that cannot grow back. A daily load carried year after year. Money and mood taxed every Tuesday.

Now set the chill down, because I do not want you to move from fear. Fear makes a slave stare at the chain. I want you to look at the hand that holds nothing.

The fact is, even if teeth were iron and guidance never written, our question would stand untouched. Harm could be zero tomorrow and the right column would still be empty. We are not here to scare you off a pleasure. We are here to show you there was never a pleasure to be scared off from.

Many methods try to frighten people off a pleasure. They paint the hospital wall and shout, stop. Then the reader obeys while still believing in the gift, and belief drags him back the first night fear sleeps. That is the old wrong road, and it kept us trapped long after we knew every harm by heart.

I want the opposite road. See the emptiness clearly, and fear becomes spare. A person who sees an empty box does not need to be threatened to put it down.

Hold both pictures together for one breath. The chill picture: acid fed all day, ceiling passed by lunch, teeth paying. The clear picture: right column empty even if none of that were true. The first picture says the price is high. The second picture says there was never goods. High price for nothing is the definition of a con. Low price for nothing would still be a con. Remove price entirely and the con still stands, because the con was never about price. The con was about a gift that cannot be produced on demand.

The Man Who Helps You Up

What do you call a man who trips you in the lane each morning and then takes your thanks for lifting you out of the ditch?

Do not answer fast. Stay with the picture. Each dawn you walk clean into the day. Each dawn unseen hands pull your feet from under you. You land hard in mud. By mid-morning the same hands reach down, lift you to your feet, brush your coat, and you thank him with real warmth. Kind man, you say. Helped me. Got me through.

Would you call him kind if you saw the trip? Would you call the lift help? Would you pay him from your wallet for the rest of your Tuesdays and call the payment a treat?

We laugh because the trick is bare in daylight. No adult thanks a tripper. Yet we have all thanked doses that behaved exactly so. The lift we praise arrives only where a low was dug first. The comfort we praise arrives only where unrest was fed first. The rescue is real as a sensation. The kindness is false as a claim.

Be harsh to that tripper, never to yourself for trusting him. Trusting an offered hand is normal. Seeing the foot behind the ankle is freedom. You were not foolish to thank the lift. You were kept from seeing the trip.

Notice how small the trip is. Not hunger. Hunger is plain and builds slowly and leaves cleanly after a meal. This is thinner. A restlessness an hour after sweet lunch. A flatness at dusk. A call at ten that says sweet, now. Tiny. Gnat-like. Easy to miss until taught to answer it with a packet.

And notice how large the talk is that reads that gnat as a command. Treat. Reward. Fuel. Love. Comfort. Fun. A lifetime of bright words pasted over a tiny echo, until the echo sounds like need and the words sound like truth.

Both halves live in you right now. The tiny echo in the body. The large reading in the mind. Name them both here, side by side. Enough for today is this: an echo is not a gift, and a reading is not a fact.

Watch the sequence in daylight once and you cannot unsee it. Dose, brief brightening, dip, call, next dose. The brightening gets the praise. The dip gets called life, work, age, weather, character. The call gets called hunger, need, deserving, occasion. Every part of the loop is renamed except the dose, and the dose keeps the one bright name. Rename the parts correctly and the play collapses. The tripper stands visible. The rescuer costume falls.

Ask the picture its hard question. If the helper truly helped, why must he help again by eleven, again by four, again by ten? What kind of help expires in ninety minutes? What kind of friend lifts you only to watch you fall in the same lane tomorrow? A true helper leaves you steadier after he leaves. This helper leaves you less steady, more flat, more twitchy, more certain you cannot walk the lane without his hand. That is not help. It is hire. It is the other way around.

Name The Gift

So let us finish the audit.

We collected your phrases honestly. “I want to get off the roller coaster.” “I-need-to-eat-something-RIGHT-NOW feeling.” “I feel like i cant stop once I start.” “I’ve managed to be good this week.” Those are not confessions of weakness. Those are field notes from inside the loop. They belong in the left column, under what is done TO you, not in the right column as proof of value.

Now point to one item in the right column and defend it out loud.

Is it taste? Take a square after a plain satisfying meal when hunger is gone and the mouth is clean. Does it sing, or does it cloy? Take the fifth square the same way. Does pleasure grow, or does it turn sickly while the hand keeps moving? A true taste does not sour by the fifth bite. A true taste does not need hunger’s absence to hide in.

Think of your own tastings. The first bite after a gap can seem bright. The second less so. The third dull. The fifth faintly nauseating, yet the hand moves for a sixth. What kind of delicious behaves so? A peach does not behave so. Bread does not behave so. A meal eaten hungry pleases and then satisfies and then says enough. The dose pleases briefly and then dulls and then urges more. That urge is not appreciation. That urge is the loop calling for its repeat under the name of taste.

Is it company? Sit with friends laughing, warm room, story flowing. Remove the bowl from the table in memory and replay the minute. Does laughter thin? Does love leave the room with the bowl? Or does the evening stand warm and whole without it, the bowl only ever sneaking a ride on a joy that was already there?

Run that replay carefully, because this is where the con hides its best coat. We remember sweet beside warmth and credit the sweet for the warmth. Yet warmth was there before the bowl arrived. Warmth came from faces, from news, from laughter, from being held in a circle after a long day. The bowl arrived into warmth and borrowed its glow. Take the bowl away in memory and the glow holds. Take the company away and leave only the bowl in an empty room at ten, and the glow dies. Which was the source?

Is it help? Watch the hour after the help. Bright ten minutes, then fog, then the same call louder. Does a helper act so? Does a friend lift you only to drop you lower than he found you? It is the other way around. The low follows the dose as night follows day.

You see how the credit shifts when the variable is isolated. The warmth was the people. The rest was the rest. The energy was the body’s own, borrowed and returned damaged. The calm was the pause in the day, not the packet in the pause.

That is what we meant all those years by a genuine treat or fuel. Say the phrase slowly and hear how hollow it rings against Tuesday. Genuine would not need a rule to limit it. Treat would not punish by the boxful. Fuel would not leave the engine flatter after filling. We called it genuine because we were taught the words before we ever tested the thing.

It does plenty TO you. It does nothing FOR you.

Read that verdict again and let it stand without padding. TO is money, minutes, mouth, mood, teeth, time, Tuesday after Tuesday. FOR is the empty column, the gift that cannot be named when asked for plainly. One column crowded. One column clean. That clean column is the whole argument.

3. JUDGE ONLY BY WHAT IT DOES FOR YOU
Forget harm versus benefit and ask for the benefit.

SUMMARY
- The harm debate keeps the trap alive by assuming a benefit exists to weigh.
- A normal Tuesday shows plenty done to mood, teeth, wallet and time, with no gift to set against it.
- Teeth pay a real price for frequent sweet doses, and ordinary eating passes guidance without feeling large.
- Fear can chill the mind, but fear is not the reason to move; emptiness is.
- A tiny body echo plus a large learned reading was mistaken for help, rescue and reward.
- When each supposed gift is isolated, the pleasure belongs to the moment, the people and the body, never to the dose.
