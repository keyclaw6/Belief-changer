### "I DESERVE IT"

"I deserve it."

Do you hear how soft that sounds? How kind?

We say it after the hard meeting. We say it after the kids are finally in bed. We say it to ourselves the way we would speak to someone we love.

"I've been good this week. I've earned this."

"Going to the cinema, celebrating, rewarding myself with chocolate."

"Hard day. I need my little love."

I hear you. I do not argue with the need underneath. Of course you deserve comfort. Of course you deserve love. Of course you want a reward after you have carried so much. That want is real. It is human. It is good.

The question is not whether you deserve sweetness. The question is whether BAD SUGAR ever gave it to you.

We fell we into the Sugar Trap on phoney information. We were taught to call a dose a treat. We were taught to pay ourselves in sugar. Did you ever test that payment? Honestly?

When you say BAD SUGAR is a genuine treat or lift because it comforts you, do you mean it comforted you for the evening — or for ten minutes before it asked for more?

Keep that question open. Your own memory will answer it.

You are not weak for wanting comfort. You were conned about where comfort comes from. That is not your fault. It is what the trap does.

And do you see how this belief keeps the loop alive? If you call BAD SUGAR love, how can you imagine leaving it without feeling you will lose love? The word itself holds you.

Is that love? Or is it labelling?

### TEN MINUTES LATER — WHAT ACTUALLY COMFORTED YOU?

Recall your last treat moment. Not the idea of it. The real one. Recent. Specific.

A Friday. Or a Tuesday that felt like a Friday. You had pushed through. You said, "I deserve it." You bought the chocolate. Or cut the cake. Or poured the bowl. Perhaps you were alone on the sofa. Perhaps you were celebrating with people you love. Bring it back.

Where were you? Who was with you? What had you just finished? How did your body feel before the first bite — tight, buzzy, a little hollow?

Now walk the next ten minutes with me, slowly, without the story.

You took the hit. Sweet. Soft. Quick.

What happened next in your body? Mouth cloying? Thirst? A stick on the teeth? A brief quieting?

And then? Ten minutes later. Twenty. What did you feel then? Lighter and loved? Or a little heavier? A little foggier? A faint need to have more? A whisper of regret? "Why did I do that again?" Did you feel rewarded — or did you need another reward soon after?

Ask the disarming question. If that dose was love, why did it leave you wanting more love ten minutes later? Does love behave like that? Does genuine comfort ask to be repeated immediately or it sours?

Think again. What actually comforted you that night?

Was it the sugar — or was it sitting down at last? Was it the sugar — or was it the film starting, the room warm, the day done, your shoulders dropping? Was it the cake — or was it people laughing around a table, being together, being celebrated, being seen?

Do you see the split?

The occasion carries the warmth. The rest carries the relief. The people carry the love. Your body carries the tiredness and the pride of getting through. All real. All yours.

BAD SUGAR was only sneaking a ride on that moment. It arrived after the comfort had already begun — the pause, the breath, the belonging — and it took the credit.

We miss this because the trap times it so well. The hit lands exactly when relief would have arrived anyway. The sofa. The blanket. The pause after effort. You were already stepping out of strain. Then the dose lands and says, "That was me."

Was it?

Try the simple subtraction test. Replay the same evening without BAD SUGAR, and change nothing else. Same sofa. Same film. Same people. Same rest. Same permission to stop and breathe. Picture it fully. Warm light. Your body settling. Laughter or quiet. No dose in your hand. Just you, resting, celebrating, belonging.

Does that scene lose its warmth without the hit? Or does it feel cleaner? Calmer? Kinder in the mouth? More settled in the stomach? Does your pride at getting through the day get louder or quieter when it is not followed by regret?

Be honest. Which version leaves you more comforted at ten minutes? At an hour? At bedtime?

The fact is, your body knows comfort when you let it. Comfort is rest, warmth, company, safety, food that satisfies. BAD SUGAR imitates comfort for minutes and then takes it away. Does that sound like a reward?

Think of the community voice that says it plainly: "celebrating, rewarding myself, going to the cinema, etc with chocolate." Do you hear how naturally we stitch celebration to chocolate, as if one cannot happen without the other? Yet when you separate them — celebration in one hand, chocolate in the other — which hand holds the joy you actually wanted?

Ask yourself: if the chocolate had been dreadful — stale, waxy, oversweet — would the celebration have been ruined? No. You would have laughed. You would have left it. Because the night was not about the dose. The dose was a gatecrasher.

And here is the quiet fact that lands behind the story. When researchers followed large groups of adults over years and accounted for other life factors, they found that men who got more of their sugar from sweets and everyday treats were more likely to experience low mood and common mental distress later on. This was an observational link, seen in men in that study, not proof that one sweet causes depression in anyone, and certainly not proof for everyone. Correlation is not sole cause. Do you see the direction, though? The daily "treat" predicted a future low, not relief. The treat did not pay out comfort later. It borrowed against it. I do not want you to stop from fear. See it, then put it behind you. You do not stop because you are frightened. You stop because you see the payment never arrived.

Does a reward that predicts a low still count as a reward?

### THE LIFEGUARD WHO PUSHED YOU IN

I want to show you one picture fully, because this chapter turns on it.

You are in water. Out of your depth. You thrash. You are tired. A hand reaches down, strong, kind, and pulls you out. You gasp. You thank the rescuer. You love that hand. You would do anything for that hand to be there again tomorrow.

Then you learn something that changes everything. The same person pushed you in.

Do you still love that hand the same way?

This is the rescuer-as-perpetrator. This is the Sugar Trap wearing the costume of comfort.

BAD SUGAR creates a low — a faint restlessness, a hollow, a need for something — and then offers itself as the rescue from the low it created. You feel relief when you take the hit. Of course you do. You are being pulled from water you would not have been in without the push. You call that relief "comfort." You call that relief "love." You call it "my treat after a hard day."

But who made the day feel harder?

Think of your hard day. Long hours. Little rest. Little real food, perhaps. Lots of hits. Each hit a bucket over the calm river, then a dip, then a reach. By evening you feel tired, tense and deserving. The hit arrives and briefly quietens the agitation the previous hits left behind. You feel rescued. You call the rescuer love.

Do you see it? The love was misassigned.

I say this tenderly. You deserve rescue. You deserve comfort that does not create the need for more comfort. Real comfort does not leave you thirstier. Real love does not ask for another dose ten minutes later.

Ask yourself: does genuine comfort make you hide the wrapper? Does genuine love make you feel a little ashamed after? Does real reward make you promise "never again" at bedtime?

No. That is not love. That is the trap's payola — a small, sweet bribe to keep you believing the trap is your friend.

Try this lens on the very phrase "my little love." Is it love when the beloved makes you feel less loved an hour later? Is it love when you need more of it to feel okay, and less of you to afford it?

You were not loving yourself when you took that hit.

**YOU WERE NOT LOVING YOURSELF — YOU WERE FEEDING THE TRAP.**

Feel that split. Short sentence. Clear.

Loving yourself would have been the rest. The pause. The kind food that satisfies. The water. The walk. The early night. The call to a friend. The moment of saying, "Well done, you got through hard things today." BAD SUGAR did not do that. You did.

Do you still want to pay yourself in the coin that takes from you?

### DID LOVE BRING YOU TO YOUR FIRST SWEET?

Let us test whether love was ever the original motive. Socratic, kindly.

At what stage did you decide you needed BAD SUGAR to feel loved or rewarded? Did you weigh it? Did you sample different forms of love and choose this one? Or did you simply find it where it was put — in the kitchen, in the ad, in the birthday ritual, in the adult hand that offered it when you were small?

We never thought it over when we found it. We were shown. We copied. We were praised for it. We still are.

Think of your first sweets. Who gave them? An adult who loved you? A celebration? A school prize? Were you hungry for love and you reached for sugar — or were you simply given sugar and told this means love?

Do you see? The link was installed. Not chosen.

And has that link ever proven true in your own life, cleanly isolated? When you felt truly loved — held, seen, safe — did you need a hit to feel it? When you gave love — to a child, a friend, a partner — did you need BAD SUGAR to make the giving real?

No. Love was present before sugar arrived and stayed after it left.

So why does sugar keep claiming the credit?

Because we let it keep sneaking a ride. We rehearse the ceremony: cake means birthday, chocolate means cinema, biscuits mean break. The ceremony is beautiful. The gatecrasher is not.

Try the isolation test, gently, in memory.

Recall a celebration where BAD SUGAR was present — birthday cake, shared dessert, Friday-night chocolate on the sofa. Now mentally lift the BAD SUGAR out and leave everything else exactly as it was: same people, same jokes, same film, same rest. Does the love shrink? Does the reward vanish? Or is the love exactly where it was — in the people, the pause, the occasion, your own tired body being kindly tended?

Now do the opposite. Keep the BAD SUGAR and remove the people, the rest, the occasion, the pride. Just the dose, alone, standing at the kitchen counter at midnight, eating from the box. Does that feel like love? Like reward? Like comfort?

Which experiment tells the truth?

If love were in the substance, the second scene would feel loving. It does not. It feels lonely. If love were in the people and the pause, the first scene would feel loving without the dose. It does.

What does that prove? That BAD SUGAR never brought the love. It borrowed it.

Ask the tender question, without shame: when you said "I deserve it," who was the "I" who deserved, and what did that "I" actually need? Did that "I" need a hit — or did that "I" need rest, kindness, food that satisfies, to be told "well done"?

Do you hear how self-payment in sugar quietly replaces self-compassion? Real compassion says, "You had a hard day. Let's care for you." The trap says, "You had a hard day. Let's feed me."

Which voice was truly on your side?

We were not fools. We were taught to mislabel a bribe as love. The word "treat" did a lot of work. A treat is something a good person deserves. Who would deny a good person a treat? So we ate the treat and felt good for minutes and a little less good after, and called both feelings deserved.

Do you still believe BAD SUGAR is a genuine treat or lift of the heart?

Or do you see now that calling it a treat keeps the Sugar Trap fed and your own kindness misdirected?

The kindness you wanted was real. The address was wrong.

### FRIEND OR ENEMY — WHICH FUNERAL ARE YOU PLANNING?

I ask you to picture two funerals.

Same factual loss: no more BAD SUGAR hits. Never again. From today.

At one funeral, you mourn a friend. You stand by the grave and you grieve. You say, "I loved you. You comforted me. You rewarded me. You were my little love. Life will be colder without you." Your eyes are wet. Your heart pulls backward. Of course you will crave. Who would not crave a lost friend?

At the other funeral, you celebrate the death of an enemy. You stand taller. You say, "You took my money. You took my calm. You pushed me in and sold me the rescue. You made my hard days harder and then charged me for relief. Good riddance." Your chest lifts. Your heart pulls forward. Do you crave an enemy you are glad is dead?

Same loss. Two opposite emotions. Only one is relapse-proof.

Which funeral have you been rehearsing when you think "I deserve it"? Be gentle and truthful. When you picture life without BAD SUGAR, do you picture losing a friend — or losing a con?

The trap wants the friend story. The friend story keeps the Sugar Trap alive. It keeps every future hard evening pre-labelled "this would be better with a hit." It keeps every celebration pre-labelled "this is not complete without cake." It keeps your own deserving nature leased out to a substance that never deserved you.

Do you want to mourn a bribe?

Or do you want to celebrate freedom from a bribe you were taught to call love?

Try the reframe now, warmly, in advance.

Next time the thought arrives — and it will, softly, out of habit — "I've earned a treat. I deserve my little love." Do not argue with the deserving. Honour it. Say, "Yes. I do deserve. I deserve real comfort. I deserve real reward. I deserve to not be conned about where love comes from." Then notice: the thought that looked like a reason to take a hit was actually a reason to be free of it.

Do you feel the turn? The same sentence becomes the cue to rejoice, not to reach.

This is not suppression. You cannot not think of sweets by trying not to. Try and you will think of nothing else. The move is reframe. When the thought comes, think it through to the truth.

Say to yourself, plainly, kindly: "That was never love. Love was the people, the pause, the pride. That was only sneaking a ride."

Does that feel like deprivation? Or like reclamation?

Think forward with me, to a near, ordinary evening.

You have had a hard day. You come home. You feed yourself properly with fresh food until you are satisfied, light, energetic. You shower. You sit. The thought flickers: "treat?" Old habit. No drama. You smile. You feel the rest itself as the treat. You feel your own "well done" land without a wrapper. You sleep without regret. You wake without fog. Nothing was given up. Something was returned.

Is that not more loving?

Ask the child question again, tenderly. Did little you need BAD SUGAR to feel loved at birthdays? Or did little you feel loved because people gathered, sang, played, and you were the reason? Which part was love? Which part was just sugar along for the ride?

You already know. You say it yourself when you are free of the ritual: "I don't even enjoy it that much. I just wanted the moment to feel special. The moment was already special."

That sentence is clarity. Let it stand.

We keep the kindness. We return the credit. The warmth, the reward, the comfort — all real. All yours. None of it was ever a genuine treat or lift from BAD SUGAR.

And when you take the credit back, what is left for BAD SUGAR to claim? Nothing. No warmth. No love. No reward. No just cause. Only the Sugar Trap, asking to be fed, and once fed asking again.

Do you want to keep feeding it and calling that feeding love?

Or do you want to love yourself properly and let the trap starve?

Choose. Clearly. Now.
