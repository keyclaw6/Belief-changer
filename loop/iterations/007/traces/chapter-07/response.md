### "EVERYONE DOES IT"

We have taken three big excuses off the table together. You saw taste was not the point. You saw energy was the other way around. You saw the so-called reward was only sneaking a ride on real comfort.

And now the softest one remains. The cosiest one. The one that doesn't argue — it just shrugs.

"But everyone does it."

"It's just normal."

"It's the sociable thing."

"Dessert is the point of the meal."

Do you know that voice? Of course you do. We all heard it first as children. The cake at the birthday. The sweets after the match. The tin that lives in the office kitchen, always full, always offered.

Ask yourself plainly. If BAD SUGAR were truly a genuine treat or lift, why would you need everyone else to prove it? Does honey need a vote? Does fresh fruit need a crowd to make it sweet?

We say "everyone does it" when we have run out of reasons inside ourselves and we look to the room instead.

I understand why. Belonging matters. No one wants to feel odd. No one wants to sit out the ritual while friends tuck in. And you have been told, kindly, by people you love, that this is how we celebrate, how we belong, how we show we are easy company.

Is that sociability? Or is it camouflage?

We fell into the Sugar Trap together. Not because we were weak. Because we were shown. The trap looks normal because it is everywhere. But everywhere is not the same as natural. Not even close.

How many of your reasons for BAD SUGAR this week were yours alone — and how many were "it's there, so I have it"?

Hold that question. Your own normal day will answer it.

### THE AUDIT OF NORMAL — AISLES, PARTIES, AND THE BISCUIT TIN

Do the audit with me. Not in theory. In your normal.

Walk your normal day, hour by hour, as you actually lived it last week.

Morning. Cereal, toast, juice. That's three hits before you leave the house. Did you choose three hits? Or did the packet choose for you?

Mid-morning. The biscuit tin at work. The email that says "help yourself in the kitchen." The meeting with pastries in the middle. Do you want that pastry — or do you take it because it is there and everyone else is?

Lunch. Bread, sauce, drink. Each one sweetened whether you asked or not. Did you taste sugar in your sandwich yesterday? No. It was there anyway.

Afternoon. "I don't keep it in the house," you say, and you mean it — at home. But the shop on the corner does. The vending machine does. The colleague's drawer does. The petrol station does.

Evening. Dessert as punctuation. The point of the meal, as if the meal without it were unfinished. Why does a meal need a sweet finish to be complete? Who taught your mouth that rule?

Kids' party. Supermarket aisles. Look honestly. How many aisles would shrink to half an aisle if BAD SUGAR were not added? How many colours in that aisle were chosen to shout at a child's eye?

Count them. Not to shame yourself. To see.

The fact is, populations today eat far above the level global health guidance suggests — guidance that for many adults works out around fifty grams of free sugars as a ceiling, with extra benefit if it's half that — and that is not because bodies changed. Bodies didn't change. Supply did. The normal you see is a supply-made normal.

Does that sound like free choice? Or like availability trained you?

See it from the non-user's view for a second. A visitor from a place where meals end with fruit and water watches your day. She sees sugar in the bread, in the sauce, in the drink, in the snack, in the dessert. She does not think "what great taste those people have." She thinks "why do they put it in everything?" Do you see the shift? When you step outside the room, normal stops looking natural.

And here is the quiet help hidden in the numbers. When researchers use validated questionnaires for highly palatable food, about one adult in seven reports enough addiction-like patterns — craving, loss of control, use despite cost — to tick the boxes, at rates in the same ballpark as alcohol or tobacco in the same kind of surveys. Those scales are not a clinical diagnosis, they are self-report, and they do not prove disease in any one person. But do you hear what they do prove? You are not uniquely weak. You are not odd. If one in seven feels this pull around this food, the trap is common, not personal.

Does common mean normal? Or does it mean the Sugar Trap is just big?

Look again at your audit. How many hits yesterday did you enjoy as a genuine treat or lift — and how many did you have because it was there, because it was offered, because it was "just what we do"?

If you removed every hit you did not truly choose, how much BAD SUGAR would be left? Very little. Perhaps none.

Who chose normal, then? You? Or the shelf?

### THE FROG THAT NEVER JUMPS

There is a story about a frog. Put it in cool water and heat the water slowly. The frog never jumps. The water gets hotter by a degree, then another. The frog adjusts. It calls the warming normal. By the time the water is boiling, the frog has cooked — never because the heat was invisible, but because it was gradual.

Use the picture once and put it down. The biology is not the point. The point is normalisation.

That is what happened to sweetness.

We did not wake up one morning and decide to eat fifty-plus grams of free sugar a day. Intake crept. A spoon in tea. Then two. Then a sweetened yoghurt that was labelled healthy. Then a drink that replaced water. Then a snack that replaced nothing — an extra. Year by year, the water got sweeter. The palate adjusted. The shops adjusted faster.

When everyone's tired, no one calls it tired. When everyone's foggy in the afternoon, no one calls it fog. When everyone's grazing, no one calls it grazing. We call it normal. We call it "just a treat." We say "once a week is fine," even while we do it daily.

Do you remember being a child and finding a can of cola almost painfully sweet? Do you remember how plain water tasted clean after sport? What changed? Your tongue? Or the water you now swim in?

Ask the non-user test again. Imagine you had never been taught that dessert crowns a meal. You eat a satisfying meal with fresh food. You are full, light, energetic. Someone slides a heavy, cloying cake in front of you and says, "Now you must finish with this or the meal doesn't count." Would you feel deprived if you said no? Or would you feel imposed on?

The trap reverses that sentence in your head. It says the meal is incomplete without BAD SUGAR. I say the meal was complete, and the push to add sugar on top was incomplete thinking.

Consider the phrase we all use: "I don't keep it in the house." Do you hear the tell inside it? If BAD SUGAR were a genuine treat or lift, why would you need to keep it out of the house to avoid it? You don't say "I don't keep apples in the house or I'll eat them." You don't need to hide water from yourself. What does that sentence admit, quietly?

It admits the cue controls the choice. The house is quiet. The kitchen without the packet is peaceful. The kitchen with the packet hums. You were not choosing freely in the kitchen that hums. You were responding to a cue that was placed there.

Is that homesickness? Or house-design?

Look at the party table. Thirty kids, thirty paper plates, a mountain of sweets that would have been unimaginable on a weekly table a hundred years ago — when bodies were the same bodies. Genes did not change in two generations. Supply did. Serving did. Marketing did. What you call normal, your grandparents would have called feast-day.

So I ask you directly, kindly, as one who was also cooked slowly: if normal was engineered slowly, is continuing to do what everyone does proof that it benefits you — or proof of how big the Sugar Trap has grown?

Think carefully. A crowded trap is still a trap. A popular con is still a con.

**NORMALISED IS NOT NATURAL.**

Short sentence. Hard stop. Let it stand.

Normalised means made normal. Natural means of nature. The first is done to you. The second was yours before.

Do you still believe everyone does it because everyone enjoys it? Or do you see now that everyone does it because everyone was taught, and everyone is offered it, all day, every day, from childhood?

Which answer makes you the odd one? Neither. It makes you the awake one.

### SOCIABLE WITHOUT THE SUBSTANCE

I grant you this freely. Sociability itself is real.

The warmth around a table is real. The laugh when the candles are lit is real. The ease of sharing something is real. People matter. Belonging matters. Ritual matters. I do not argue against any of it. I argue for it.

The question is whether that warmth needs BAD SUGAR to work.

Try the isolation, as you did in the last chapter, but now with people.

Recall your last "sociable" sweet moment. Not the idea. The real one. Office biscuits. Birthday cake. The film with chocolate. Who was there? What were you talking about? What was funny? What was kind?

Now lift BAD SUGAR out and leave everything else exactly as it was. Same people. Same jokes. Same room, same light. Same pride that you were together at all. Picture it. Fully. Close range.

Does the warmth shrink? Does the friend become less funny without the biscuit? Does the child blow candles less brightly if the cake is fresh fruit and laughter, not a dense slab that leaves everyone thirsty and sticky?

Now do the opposite. Keep BAD SUGAR and remove the people, the talk, the occasion. Just the dose. Alone. Kitchen counter. Late. No warmth, no witness, no celebration. Just the hit. Does that feel sociable? Does that feel like belonging?

Which experiment tells the truth about where belonging lives?

The fact is, sociability works with any food or none. The table is sociable because people are there and you are together and you have paused your hurry to be with each other. Tea works. Water works. Fruit works. Nothing works too — people who love each other can sit and talk and rejoice with nothing in hand at all and still feel full.

If sociability needed BAD SUGAR, how do you explain every sociable meal you have ever enjoyed where BAD SUGAR was absent? The barbecue where you forgot dessert and no one suffered. The walk with a friend where you talked for an hour and never ate. The holiday lunch where the best moment was someone's story, not someone's pudding.

Do you see? The occasion carries the pleasure. BAD SUGAR was only along for the ride. You credited the rider because the horse arrived at the same time.

Does the rider own the horse?

Ask the sharper question. Who was sociable to whom? Did you offer BAD SUGAR to be kind — or did you offer it because it was expected and you wanted to be seen as kind, as easy, as normal? Is that choice for them? Or for the trap's script?

I say this warmly, because families love each other and mean well. Your mother did not push BAD SUGAR to harm you. She pushed it because she was pushed. Your friends do not pass the tin to trap you. They pass it because it was passed to them. No one to blame. Everyone to free.

But do you want to keep calling a cue-induced chew sociability? When you say "it's the sociable thing," do you mean you enjoyed the sugar — or you enjoyed not feeling awkward for a minute?

Can awkwardness be cured by a biscuit? For how long? One minute? Then you need another.

Real sociability does not ask you to feel foggy afterward to feel included now. Real belonging does not need a dose to sign its name. You belong when you are there, listening, laughing, being kind. Your body is there more cleanly, your attention brighter, when you are not chewing through cloy.

Try this at the next table, not as avoidance, as freedom. Sit. Eat fresh food until you are satisfied. Stay. Talk. Stay longer. Notice who is with you. Notice what you taste. Notice how easy your mouth feels after. Notice how steady your energy stays when you are not riding a spike and a dip while trying to be interesting.

Does anyone leave saying, "That would have been sociable if only we'd had BAD SUGAR"?

No. They say, "That was lovely. Let's do it again."

Who got the credit?

Try it and see. Sociability was never a genuine treat or lift from BAD SUGAR. It was people, place and pause. BAD SUGAR was only drafting behind them and calling the wind its own.

Do you need a dose to be friendly? Or do you need to stop believing you do to discover you already are?

### WHO MADE NORMAL NORMAL? — A GLIMPSE INSIDE THE LAB

So if your audit shows most hits were not chosen, if your frog story shows slow cooking can be called normal, if your table shows people are the point, then a hard question remains, and it is wonderful news.

Who made normal?

Not you. We already saw that. Not your friends. Not your grandmother, who baked on Sunday and called it Sunday.

I want you to glimpse one room. Not to frighten you. To dignify you. You were never meant to win this on willpower.

In the Bliss-Point Lab, white coats are not making food. They are engineering desire. They dial sugar up, fat up, crunch up, melt down, aroma up — until a panel of tasters says "more-ish." That's the word. More-ish. Not "lovely." Not "satisfying." More-ish means you cannot stop at satisfied. You stop past it. That is the point.

The dials are not hidden. Food companies patent them. Supermarkets sell them at eye height. Advertisers teach children their jingle before they can read. The budget for that teaching dwarfs any campaign for water or apples. Is that a fair fight between you and your instincts — or a designed mismatch?

The fact is, intake did not drift naturally above guidance; it was built above it. The supply was built to sit above your appetite and call its own push "normal choice." You did not choose to need dessert to finish a meal. You were taught that meals finish that way, and every menu confirmed it.

Does that make you foolish? No. It makes you engineered around. It makes you the heroic underdog who spotted a con that was sized to be invisible.

Look at the aisle again, but now with the lab in mind. Those colours are not food's colours; they are signals. Those crinkles are not potatoes; they are textures tuned to shatter and melt at a speed that quickens the next bite. Those health haloes — "energy," "natural," "light" — are words lab-tested to move hands. You were not weak in that aisle. You were outgunned in that aisle.

And what did that aisle steal while you were deciding nothing? Time. Money. The quiet freshness after a meal when your mouth is clean and your mind clear. The afternoon when you could have been steady. Do you call that sociable?

Hold the anger where it belongs. Not on the friend who offered the tin. Not on yourself for taking it. On the system that profits when "everyone does it" is true.

Ask one plain question, and let the answer close this chapter.

If normal was manufactured in that lab and delivered by that shelf, is doing what everyone does evidence that BAD SUGAR gives you something — or evidence that the Sugar Trap has been normalised at industrial scale, and you are seeing its size clearly for the first time?

If the trap is that big, do you need a bigger willpower to leave it? Or do you need to see it plainly and step out — because you have nothing to lose and everything to gain except the fog you never chose.

There is nothing sociable in being foggy together. There is everything sociable in being free together.

Do you want to keep performing normal for an audience that was also sold the script?

Or do you want to be the one at the table who remembers that natural is not the same as normalised, and quietly, kindly, shows another way simply by being happily free there?

We will open that lab door fully in the next chapter. For now, let this sit.

When you think trap, you think room. When you think room, you think lab. When you think lab, you remember: you were not the chef. You were the target.

And targets can walk away.
