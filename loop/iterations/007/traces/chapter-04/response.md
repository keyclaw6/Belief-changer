### "I LOVE THE TASTE" — LET'S TEST WHAT YOU ACTUALLY MEAN

"I love the taste."

Do you hear yourself say it? Do you hear how sincere it sounds?

"I just love sweet things. I have a sweet tooth. It's delicious. It's my treat."

I hear you. We have all said it. We say it warmly, almost proudly, as if we are describing a personal trait. The secret binger says it after the box is empty. The daily grazer says it while reaching for the biscuit that goes with the tea he insists he doesn't need. It feels true. It feels obvious. Of course you love the taste — why else would you keep eating it?

But is it true?

Let us be precise, and kind, and forensic. What taste do you actually love? Can you name it without borrowing from something else?

We were conned into the Sugar Trap, remember? We first chose BAD SUGAR on phoney information. A parent's reward. A friend's dare. An advert with a smiling child. We did not choose with knowledge. We learned to call the feeling afterwards — relief from the low the last hit left — "taste." Do you see how that learning could stick for a lifetime if no one asks you to look?

So I will ask you to look now. Gently. Directly.

Think of your favourite BAD SUGAR hit. Name it honestly. Is it chocolate? Is it biscuits with tea? Is it ice cream after dinner? Is it the cola that "hits the spot"? Keep it in mind. Taste it in memory.

Now ask yourself the isolating question. The question that kills the illusion by separating the variable.

If BAD SUGAR were a genuine treat or lift for taste alone, would it taste wonderful on its own, plain, isolated, with nothing else to carry it?

Would you sit down and eat a spoonful of plain white sugar and call it delicious? Would you chew a mouthful of plain white bread — no butter, no jam, no cheese, no ham — and call it a feast? Would you drink unsweetened coffee made deliberately weak and then say sugar made it taste of something beautiful, or did it just make it taste of sugar?

Do you love BAD SUGAR? Or do you love the thing BAD SUGAR hides behind?

Keep that question open. We will answer it together, with your own mouth as the witness.

The fact is, we rarely taste BAD SUGAR at all. We taste the confection around it. We taste the context around it. And we rarely check. Why would we? The wrapper tells us we love it.

### THE LONELY CARB THAT NEEDS COMPANY — THE HOUSE-PARTY GATECRASHER

Let me show you the loneliest thing in your kitchen.

Take white bread. Plain crackers. A dry biscuit without chocolate. A plain refined roll. Taste it without company. Go on, in your mind. What is it?

It is not delicious. It is bland. It is papery. It is faintly sweet in a flat, empty way that makes you want water. It sits on the tongue and waits. It is a wall, not a flavour.

Do you know what that lonely carb does next? It invites company over. It cannot stand to be alone.

It needs butter. It needs jam. It needs cheese. It needs ham. It needs chocolate spread. It needs sauce. It needs salt. It needs crunch. Without a co-conspirator, you would not eat it twice.

I call this the House-Party Gatecrasher. Imagine a dull guest who arrives alone and knows he is dull. He lingers by the door until someone more charming arrives. Then he attaches himself. He laughs at their jokes. He follows them round the room. Later you remember the party as fun and you mistakenly think he was fun too. He was not fun. He was sneaking a ride.

That is BAD SUGAR as taste.

You say white bread is nice. Is it? Or is butter nice? You say crackers are more-ish. Are they? Or is cheese nice? You say pastries are irresistible. Is it the stripped, sugared flour or is it the butter-fat, the melt, the salt, the warm smell of the bakery, the fact you are sitting down at last?

Do you see the trick? BAD SUGAR never stands alone. It always arrives with fat, crunch, melt, chill, salt, scent, company, rest, and the story "I've been good." Then it takes the credit.

Try the isolation test at home, for curiosity, not willpower. Eat a bite of plain white bread entirely alone, slow, with attention. No spread. No filling. What does it actually taste like? Now eat butter alone. Now eat jam alone. Which one carried the pleasure?

Do you notice how quickly the answer becomes obvious once you separate them?

We love to say "just a treat" about BAD SUGAR. But even our own phrase betrays the borrowing. "Just a treat" never means the lonely carb itself. It means the whole scene we dress around it so we can love it.

You have not lost a taste pleasure. You have found a gatecrasher who was never delightful on his own.

### ON ITS OWN — WHAT DOES BAD SUGAR TASTE LIKE WHEN ISOLATED?

Let us isolate further. Because the binger and the grazer both keep a private defence: "But I do love the sweet taste itself."

Do you? Let's taste it truly.

Take plain sugar. Not in chocolate where fat and cocoa carry it. Not in cake where butter and scent carry it. Not in cola where chill and fizz and ritual carry it. Just sugar. On a spoon. Or dissolved in water without anything else.

What happens in your mouth? Be honest.

First second: intense sweetness. Sharp. One-note. Then almost immediately: cloy. Stick. A coating on teeth that wants to be brushed away. A thirst that asks for water. A sweetness that turns from sweet to sickly if you keep it there.

Does that sound like a genuine treat or lift you would travel for? Would you sit down and eat it by the spoonful for delight? Would you call your friends and say, "Come over, I have sugar"?

No. You wouldn't.

Think of the sweetest hit you binge on when you say "I can't stop once I start." That hit is never pure sugar alone. It is sugar plus fat plus texture plus melt plus crunch engineered to be more-ish. A biscuit is not sugar. It is weaponised sweetness glued to butter and crunch and salt so your mouth cannot register the cloy. The food that most triggers "one bite becomes the box" is always the most engineered, never the plainest. Is that taste? Or is that design?

Do you love the taste? Or do you love the rush of sensations that hide the cloy?

Try another memory audit. Coffee that tastes harsh or bitter until you add sugar. Did sugar make that coffee taste good? Or did it mask that the coffee was poor and make it tolerable? If you buy better coffee, you rarely need sugar at all. What does that tell you? It tells you sugar was the concealment, not the flavour.

Think of sweets you ate as a child and now find "too sweet." What changed? Your taste did not ruin sweetness — your discrimination improved. You now notice the oversweetness because the brainwashing has thinned in that one corner. The stick, the thirst, the faintly chemical after-taste — they were always there. You were taught not to notice.

Do you feel the amusement rising? That faint disgust that replaces mouth-watering when you look closely? Good. That is the truth arriving.

A genuine taste pleasure does not demand water afterwards to wash it away. A genuine taste pleasure does not leave your teeth feeling filmed. Fresh fruit does not do that. A ripe peach does not do that. Fresh bread with structure and fibre does not do that. BAD SUGAR does.

You were not loving a taste. You were enduring a cloy because the sensations around it made it tolerable for thirty seconds before you needed another hit.

I want you to try something simple, not as a rule, as an observation. Your next BAD SUGAR hit — if you have one while you are still reading — eat it with absolute attention. No phone. No TV. Slow. Notice the first second, the third second, the fifth. Notice sweetness, then cloy, then stick, then the wish for water, then the faint letdown. Don't judge. Just watch.

You may find you don't even need me to argue. Your own mouth will tell you.

### WHY YOUR MOUTH WATERS BEFORE THE BITE

"But my mouth waters when I think about it! That proves I love it!"

Does it? Or does it prove you have been trained to want it before you taste it?

You know this experience well. The wrapper rustles. The advert plays. The clock hits 3pm. Your colleague opens the biscuit tin. Your mouth waters. Your attention sharpens. You feel a pull: "I need something RIGHT NOW."

The fact is, that is not taste. That is cue.

Your brain learned the association. Wrappers, smells, times, places, feelings — "I've been good this week," "to get me through" — they fire the wanting before any flavour touches your tongue. The mouth waters not because the lonely carb is delicious, but because your cue circuit is firing. It is a well-measured response. It looks like hunger. It feels like desire. It is really a learned signal.

Do you think your mouth would water for plain white sugar on a spoon if I showed you a white spoon on a grey table with no brand, no crinkle, no smell, no story? Try it. Picture it. Nothing happens. Now picture the glossy wrapper, the snap of the bar, the chill of the ice cream, the word "treat." Your mouth answers. Which one was taste?

We say "I can't stop once I start" and we think it proves love. It proves the cue-plus-hit loop. One bite re-primes the wanting circuit, and the wanting feels like "more." That is not love of flavour. That is the little hit re-triggering the search. The more-engineered the hit, the more it triggers the next.

Do you see why "I don't keep it in the house" feels like a solution? You are trying to avoid the cue that fires before taste. You are not avoiding a taste you love. You are avoiding the bell that makes you salivate.

This is why looking only at taste demolishes the strongest defence you thought you had. If cue drives the mouth-watering before flavour, then flavour cannot be the cause of your desire. Something else is using flavour as its costume.

Ask the disarming question: if you truly loved the taste itself, would you need the wrapper, the advert, the time of day, the feeling of "I deserve it" to make it appealing? Would a genuine taste pleasure need that much marketing?

A peach needs none. Fresh berries need none. Your body recognises them by taste alone, quietly, without a wrapper shouting.

BAD SUGAR shouts because it knows it would be ignored if it stood alone at the party. That is not power. That is insecurity.

You have not been tasting a love affair. You have been hearing a cue.

### WHAT YOU LOVED — AND WHAT WAS JUST SNEAKING A RIDE

So what did you love, then, if not BAD SUGAR's taste?

You loved sensation. You loved texture. You loved occasion. All three are real. None belongs to BAD SUGAR.

You loved crunch and melt. You loved chill on a hot day. You loved warmth and scent with coffee among friends. You loved the pause at 3pm when you finally sat down. You loved rest. You loved laughter. You loved being included. You loved the story "I've been good" after a hard day. BAD SUGAR attached itself to all of it and whispered, "That was me."

It was never him. It was sneaking a ride.

Do you remember a time you ate fresh, wonderful food when genuinely hungry — fruit, a crisp salad, warm fish, nuts you love, bread with substance — and it tasted vivid without any need for a coating? Your taste buds were not broken then. Your body was satisfied then. The pleasure was clean. It finished and you felt light and energetic, not thirsty and in need of more.

That is what real taste pleasure does. It satisfies. It ends. It does not ask for water to erase it. It does not ask for another dose to quiet the restless search.

Now picture tight shoes. You wear them all day. They pinch. When you take them off at last, what a relief! Wonderful! Would you say tight shoes are a genuine treat or lift for your feet? Would you miss them tomorrow? Would you lace them tight again to feel the relief again?

No. You would leave them off and enjoy your feet.

BAD SUGAR's "taste pleasure" is brief relief from a discomfort BAD SUGAR trained you to feel between hits — the faint, restless need, the cue firing, the low after the last cloy. When you finally feed it, the restlessness quietens for minutes and you call that quietness "delicious." Is that delicious? Or is that tight shoes removed?

Do you see how the inversion previews itself here? The lift you called taste was the low leaving. We will prove that completely with energy next. For taste alone, it is enough to see: the gatecrasher did not bring joy to the party. He stopped shouting for a moment and you thanked him for the silence.

You might worry, "If I stop BAD SUGAR, will food become tasteless?" Not at all. You are not quitting taste. You are quitting the coat that dulled it. Sweetness as taste remains — in fruit, in roasted vegetables, in a good meal — bright, clean, in proportion, finishing naturally. You are not giving up sweetness. You are giving up being tricked about sweetness. Your tongue will thank you, quickly.

So ask the question that collapses the doubt, and answer honestly:

Was that memory delicious because of BAD SUGAR — or was the film delicious, the company warm, the rest real, and BAD SUGAR was only ever along for the ride?

Which answer is true for you, in your own mouth, now that you have isolated the variable?

Be decisive. Taste is one FOR. It is the easiest to test. It fails the test.

**IT WAS NEVER THE TASTE.**

The lonely carb needed company to be noticed at all. The intense hit cloys and asks for water. The mouth waters for the wrapper, not the spoon. The crunch, the melt, the chill, the laughter, the pause — all real — were never BAD SUGAR's gifts. They were yours. He was sneaking a ride.

Do you feel the small shift from mouth-watering memory to amusement? From "I love it" to "I see it"? Good. Hold that.

You came into this chapter thinking "I love the taste" was a reason to stay. You leave it seeing taste as the first FOR you can confidently cross off the ledger.

That is not deprivation. That is reclamation. That is one power cord unplugged, fully, so it cannot run the machine again.
