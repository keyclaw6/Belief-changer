Chapter 8
WHO BUILT THE WANT

**IN THIS CHAPTER** — Bright supermarket aisle with bliss-point packs and cartoon smiles, the till ambush, the mirror that says personality, an open stone cell, a lion pacing by an open gate, a seatbelt clicked for a jolt

*You did not invent this want and your fear is not wisdom; the want was built for profit and both your fears are held by the same hand.*

You come in here with the sizes seen clear.

You have watched the early-evening wave rise and pass unfed. You have held the tiny grumble in one palm and heard the loud translator in the other ear. You know the Nibbler is small and dying and the Sweet Con is vast and does all the damage. Keep that split in your hand. You will need it now, because we widen the lens from your kitchen to the world that filled your kitchen.

I speak as one who lived the same widening. For years I thought my want was mine. My taste. My nature. My private comfort. Then I saw the aisle with the light on, and the mirror with the light on, and the fear with the light on, and I could never unsee them. What I took for personality was planting. What I took for wisdom was wiring laid by other hands.

Walk with me. You will leave here knowing the want was engineered and both fear ropes belong to the Trap.

THE AISLE THAT KNOWS YOUR NAME

Walk with me down the aisle where it happens daily.

You are not hungry. You came for bread and milk. Fluorescent light hums. The floor shines. To your left a wall of colour shouts. Red packs, gold packs, cartoon tigers and grinning bears with arms wide open. Pouches that crinkle to call a child from three aisles away. Bars lined like bricks, each wrapper promising joy, reward, deserved, love. To your right, family-size bags leaning toward you like old friends. At the end, a tower of chocolate cut to the price of a coin. At the till, where the queue drags and children whine and tired hands fidget, small sweet hits wait at hand height, at child-eye height, at tired-mind height. You did not plan them. You meet them.

We all know this walk. We have all lived it a thousand times.

We tell ourselves we chose. We say, “Everyone eats it so it must be fine. It is normal. It is love. It is childhood. It is celebration. I could stop if I wanted, I simply like it. Sugary somethings get me through.”

I hear you. There is no shame in that thought. I said it for years. We all did.

But look closer with the light on.

Nothing in that aisle is accident. The crunch that shatters exactly so. The melt that floods exactly so. The salt edging the sweet to keep the mouth awake. The chew that never quite satisfies so the hand goes back. The fizz that lifts the nose. Teams of clever people in white rooms tested hundreds of blends to find the point where your tongue lights up and your hand cannot rest. They built it for your tongue the way a locksmith builds a key for a lock. Sweet upon sweet upon fat upon salt until the mouth says more while the belly says nothing.

Then they dressed the key as love.

The advert where the laughing family passes the box and the room glows. The film where the lovers share the bar and music swells. The packet that says treat, reward, good, deserved. The mascot that taught your child before your child could read. The shelf paid for at eye level. The tower paid for at the end where trolleys slow. The till ambush paid for where will is thinnest and the wait is longest. The school prize. The birthday table. The office drawer. The cinema hand-off. Year after year the same bell rung with laughter until laughter itself seemed to ring the bell.

Did you choose that? Did you sit as a clear-minded adult and decide that a sweet hit at four and eight and with every tea and every hard feeling would improve your life? Or did you grow up inside rooms where sweet hits arrived at four and eight and with every tea and every hard feeling until the hour itself rang?

When you travel where bright packets are scarce and plain meals fill the day, does your terrible personal need follow you like a shadow? Or does it fall silent for hours, for days, until bright packets surround you again and the old pull wakes on cue?

And who gains when you answer the bell? You, left flat and hungry for more within the hour, reaching again? Or the maker who sells another dose for the low his last dose dug?

You know. We all know once the aisle is seen plainly.

The fact is desire was manufactured. Your want feels personal because it was planted personally — in childhood kitchens where good meant sweet, in school prizes where brave meant chocolate, in screens that tied energy to a bar and love to a box, in family tables where the prize closed every meal, in friends who passed sweets as kindness. What was planted can be pulled. What was built for profit can be seen through and left behind.

You are not looking at normal. You are looking at the Sugar Trap from outside for the first time.

Feel what that does. It lifts blame off you in one move. You were not foolish for wanting. You were conned by armies, as we all were. And it lifts credit off the dose in the same move. The glow you taste is not kindness in food. It is engineering in food. The warmth you feel is the room, the company, the hour of rest, the human faces — with a sweet hit sneaking a ride and taking the applause.

Ask your own mouth. When a plain meal eaten hungry leaves you warm and done, what added the packet except noise? When laughter fills a room, what added the bar except rustle? When love sits beside you on the sofa, what added the box except interruption? The scene gave. The dose took.

It does not love you. It uses you.

That voice that says normal, love, mine — hear it now for what it is. That is the Sweet Con speaking, not you.

THE MIRROR THAT LIES

There is a second builder, quieter than the aisle, and it lives in the mirror.

It speaks like this:

“I am the addictive type. My family all have a sweet tooth. My mother hid biscuits. My father needed dessert. I have always been like this. Other people can take or leave it. I cannot. Something in me is wired wrong. I feel like i cant stop once I start.”

I hear that voice exactly as it speaks in your head, because it spoke in mine. We all obeyed mirrors for years.

Hear the truth with clean ears.

You are not the addictive type. There is no sweet-tooth fate that orders evening boxes. What you call personality is history written in sugar.

Think how it runs. A child praised with sweets learns sweets mean love. A tired house where the box closes every hard day learns sweets mean relief. A school where the prize is chocolate learns sweets mean pride. Years of dose at stress, dose at boredom, dose at celebration, until stress and boredom and celebration themselves call for a dose. Then we look at the adult shaped by that training and say, “See, addictive personality.”

It is the other way around.

The traits we share were made by the loop, not born before it. The evening restlessness. The all-or-nothing eating once the packet opens. The flat mornings. The secret cupboard. The I-need-to-eat-something-RIGHT-NOW feeling at the old hour. The roller coaster we swore we wanted to leave. We did not bring these traits to BAD SUGAR. BAD SUGAR brought these traits to us.

Do you doubt it? Look at how common the snarl is, and let the commonness free you rather than frighten you.

When asked on questionnaires about rich sweet food, about 14 in 100 adults and about 12 in 100 children answer in ways that look addiction-like — craving, loss of control once started, use despite regret — at a rate much like alcohol and tobacco. I am not saying you carry a diagnosis, and no questionnaire can hand you one. I am saying you are not uniquely weak and not strangely wired. The loop is common because the planting is common. Millions walked the same bright aisle and learned the same bell. If low mood or eating trouble ever grips you hard, speak to your clinician and use this book for the belief alone.

Feel the dignity return. You were not singled out by nature. You were recruited by nurture with a price tag.

And here is the grace that closes the mirror for good. Even if you were the sensitive type, even if your family taught you hard, even if your nerves fire fast — this method still frees you. Why? Because freedom here does not ask you to rebuild your character. It asks you to see one lie clearly and let the echo starve. Sensitive or steady, cautious or bold, you can see a shove once it is shown. You can stop thanking a hand once you have seen it push.

This was seen in rats, not people, and rats are not people, so I sell you no human proof from cages. I tell you only the shape because the shape echoes our lives: when sweet access comes and goes in bursts, bingeing and craving and restless signs grow in those small bodies, while steady plain access does not grow the same snarl. Does that not echo our diet-binge yo-yo? Forbid by day, burst by night, vow on Monday, empty by Friday. Good all week, wild by weekend. The schedule feeds the snarl. The snarl is not your soul.

Watch your own life for the two footprints of that schedule, and you will see training where you feared fate.

First, the creep. One square was enough once. Then the bar. Then the bar plus biscuits. Then the family bag that no longer lasts the week. You need more for the same brief hush because the circuit gets crowded and dull. Life runs flat, foggy and never satisfied between hits, and each hit lifts less and costs more. That dulling is not character. That is the loop charging interest.

Second, the firing before the bite. Wrapper crack and mouth waters. Sweet smell at the doorway and feet turn. Four o’clock and attention snags. Cinema foyer and hand itches. Till queue and eyes drop to hand height. That firing is learned, not compulsion. Bells ring because they were rung with food for years. Bells are not orders. Your nerves fire from memory. Memory fades when unfed.

Ask your life. Does the smell drag a clear eater to a box? Does the clock contain sweets, or does the clock contain tiredness that BAD SUGAR itself deepened, plus a memory laid over it? When you were lost in good work or laughter and the hour passed clean, where was the mighty wiring then? Gone. Silent. Forgotten until the first bite reminded it to roar.

You know. We all know once hunger speaks plainly. The cue suggests. The lie translates suggestion into order. Remove the translator and suggestion fades to background noise within days.

You are not broken. You were trained. Training unfed dies.

THE TREAT THAT DARKENS MOOD

Now meet the kindest lie of all, the one that sounds like care.

“At least it cheers me up. Life is hard. A daily treat keeps low mood away. Celebrating with chocolate is joy. Without my sweet comfort I would sink. I have managed to be good this week, I deserve one.”

I speak as one who whispered that nightly. Warm to you, harsh to the lie: it runs backward, and the backwardness can be seen in lives.

In men followed over years, those who ate more sweets were more likely to feel low later, not less likely. The daily treat predicted later low mood rather than fixing mood. I am not saying a bar writes your future, and I am not handing you a sentence on your mind — this showed across a crowd over time, not what must happen to you, and a single dose never seals a fate. If gloom dogs you, keep your clinician in charge and use this book for the belief alone.

But hear the direction. The rescue runs backward.

Why? Because the lift borrows from tomorrow. The rush fades. The dip follows. Sleep thins. Mornings weigh. The hollow calls for another lift to correct the last lift. Week by month by year, the rescuer digs the ditch deeper and sells more hands. What you call comfort is relief from discomfort the comfort itself renewed. What you call reward is a loan signed in bright paper and collected by night.

Look at your own afternoons. The sweet top-up at three, the ten-minute buzz, the four o’clock fog, the second reach. I would have something quick and sweet but now know that will only give a short term lift which will inevitably be followed by a fast drop. We all lived that yo-yo. We called the buzz energy and the fog need for more energy. We never saw the buzz and the fog belonged to one motion.

If sweets truly lifted mood, why do the heaviest grazers report the flattest days? Should not the most dosed be the most joyful? Are they? Or are they the most flat, foggy and tired between fixes, bright for minutes, dim for hours, kind for a bite, edgy till the next?

And if one dose truly fixed low mood, why has no box ever fixed it? Why did relief always expire before the wrapper settled? Why did the permitted one become the evening? Why did the evening demand the morning? Does a true friend behave like that? Or does a pusher behave like that?

You know. We all know once the days are added honestly.

The fact is sweets do not mend mood. They rent mood at cruel interest and collect by night. Seeing that ends the debt.

Do not change from fear of future gloom. I do not want you quitting from fright. I want you quitting from sight — because there is nothing to lose and everything to clear. The moment the lie dies, mood has one less weight dragging it down, and mornings have one less ditch to climb out of. That is relief, not warning. That is escape, not threat. You walk out lighter now, not heavier with dread.

THE OPEN DOOR AND THE PACING LION

Come into the fear itself, because fear is the last guard at the gate, and it wears two faces.

Stand with me at night in a small stone cell. The door stands ajar. Light from the corridor falls across the floor in a long bar. Air moves. No lock holds. No guard watches. Dust hangs in the light. Freedom is a step away.

Inside, a prisoner sits on the bunk clutching his knees. He stares at the open door and begs to stay.

“Do not make me go. Outside I will fail. I will crawl back. I will be seen trying and failing. Better to stay where I know the rules. At least here the pain is familiar. At least here no one watches me fall. I have failed diets. I have failed Mondays. I have sworn and broken and sworn. What if I try and fail again? What if craving proves stronger? What if I am the one this book cannot free? Safer not to step. Safer to keep the cell I know.”

We all know that voice. We have all sat on that bunk. We all felt the cold wall at our back feel safer than the open corridor.

Now look to the field beyond the prison. Grass runs to the horizon. Morning light lies wide. Dew shines. By the open gate of an empty cage paces a great lion, back and forth, back and forth, wearing a path in the dust. The gate hangs open. No bars hold. No chain pulls. Food and water wait outside in the open. Yet he paces the old square, turning where the bars used to be, lying down where the corner was, rising at the old feeding hour, sniffing the air and settling again.

A keeper says, “You are free. Walk out. Get on with enjoying your life.” The lion looks up, uneasy, and paces again.

“Outside who am I without the old hours? Without the evening box, what closes the day? Without the desk drawer, what gets me through? Without the birthday prize, what is celebration? Without my sweet self, who will I be at family tables? I fear success as much as failure. At least the cage gave me a name. At least the pacing gave me a day.”

We all know that pacing. We have all worn that path in thought. We all feared the open field would be grey.

Two fears. One begs to stay from dread of falling. One hovers from dread of flying. Failure fear says you will not be able. Success fear says you will not know yourself once able. They pull opposite ways and tear the middle. Craving says feed me or suffer. Fear says change or suffer. Pull, pull, pull. Night after night the same ache. Morning after morning the same bargain.

Say it in your own voice and feel its geometry. Craving pulls one arm toward the packet. Fear of losing the packet pulls the other arm toward the cell. Both ropes burn. Both promise pain whatever you do. No wonder you stand frozen. No wonder quitting felt like torment before you understood. You were not weak for freezing. Anyone held at both ends freezes.

This is the tug-of-war of craving and fear.

Feel the shape land. One rope is want. One rope is dread. Both cut the hands. Both keep the feet in place while the seller watches. Once named, the shape can be seen through. Once seen through, it can be dropped.

Now see who holds the ropes.

Who taught you that life without sweet hits means failure and torment? The same makers who taught you that sweet hits mean love and energy. Who taught you that evenings without a box will be empty and tables without dessert will be grey? The same voices that sold you the box as comfort and the dessert as the point. Who gains while you pull and freeze and buy? Not you, pacing. The seller, watching. The Sweet Con holds both ends and calls the burn proof you need him.

Both ropes belong to the Trap.

The prison door was left ajar by nature, yet belief keeps the prisoner seated. The cage was emptied by nature, yet habit paces on because belief still feeds the old square. Fear of failing is the Trap whispering you are too weak to leave. Fear of succeeding is the Trap whispering you are too empty to live outside. One voice, two masks. One owner, two ropes.

Ask three questions whose only honest answer cuts both ropes.

First. If fear of failure were wisdom, why did every white-knuckle week fail while belief stayed intact, yet clear seeing succeeds without struggle? Was the failure in you, or in the method that told you to fight a mouse while obeying a loudspeaker? Did strong will ever fail you, or did the wrong method fail your strong will?

Second. If fear of success were truth, why do clear eaters — children lost in play, friends who leave sweets untouched — live vivid evenings without your “essential” close? Are they deprived ghosts staring at a wall? Or are you mistaking the cage path for your nature? When did a peach eaten hungry fail a child? When did a plain meal shared laughing fail a friend?

Third. When hours passed in love or work without thought of sweets, did life feel greyer without the packet, or brighter without the tug? Did you miss your cage then? Did you sit mourning the bars? Or did you forget the cage entirely until the first bite reminded you to pace?

You know. We all know once the holders are seen.

Let both ropes drop in one move. There is no failure to fear because there is no battle to fight — only a lie to stop believing. There is no success to fear because there is nothing to lose — only mornings, shops and meals lived clear, hungry when hungry, done when satisfied. The prisoner can walk because the door was never locked by nature. The lion can leave because the bars were habit, not need.

I do not want you stepping out as a brave sufferer. Step out as a freed eater. Pacing fades in days when unfed. The path in the dust grows over. The bunk smell leaves the clothes. You do not pace for months. You do not white-knuckle for years. You walk, eat, sleep, laugh, and wonder why the small square ever looked like home.

Look both ways down time and choose with open eyes. Stay and you will graze and crash for the rest of your life, paying daily for a comfort that renews the ache. Walk and you will eat and live clear for the rest of your life, with attention freed for people and work and taste itself.

Which rest of life do you want? The question answers itself.

THE MACHINE THAT WARNS

Stand back now and look at the instrument you were taught to mistrust — your body.

Your body is a precision machine, finer than any packet built in a white room. Hunger rises cleanly. Smell sharpens toward bread and soup and fruit. The mouth waters at real food. The belly warms and says done while food still tastes good. Energy returns after rest, not after rush. Sleep deepens when days run clear. Taste brightens when noise falls silent.

BAD SUGAR taught you to read its warning lights backward.

Afternoon fog after a sweet flood? You read it as need more fuel, when it was information that the flood had passed and the machine wanted plain food and air and movement. Evening twitch at the old hour? You read it as order for a hit, when it was information that the old rhythm was firing from memory and would fade unfed. Mouth-memory of melt? You read it as hunger, when hunger welcomes meals and only the echo narrows to one packet.

We unscrewed the bulbs rather than heeding the lights. We silenced each signal with the very dose that caused it, then praised the silence as healing. No engineer would call that repair. It is like cutting the wire to a flashing lamp and saying the engine is mended.

I speak as one who cut wires for years. I dosed the fog and called it focus. I dosed the twitch and called it comfort. I dosed the flatness and called it reward. Each silencing felt like help for minutes and left the engine running rougher for hours — days that ran flat, foggy and never satisfied while I blamed age and work and character. We all did. We all mistook the lamp for the fault and the wire-cutter for the mechanic.

Ask your machine plainly.

When you eat a plain favourite meal hungry and stop at satisfied, does the lamp flash red or glow green? Warmth, clearness, done-ness. When you follow with a sweet flood, what happens within the hour? Dullness, pull for more, second reach. Which state is information? Which is interference? Which eater do you trust — the one who leaves the table light, or the one who leaves the table hunting?

You know. We all know once the lights are read rightly.

Trust hunger and satisfaction and the machine steadies itself. No new rules. No new system. Change nothing else in life. Walk the same aisle, sit the same sofa, meet the same hour, and let the old bell ring once while you smile. See. It rang. Nothing followed. The echo thins. The lights glow steady. Attention returns to faces and work and taste itself. That steadiness is not luck. That is the baseline returning because noise stopped.

And about the road itself, hear this small truth the way you hear a belt click. A seatbelt is sewn for an accidental jolt, not for steering into walls. Your body can meet an accidental crumb in a sauce and roll on clear, but your mind cannot afford a deliberate swerve for an old thrill. Guard the belief and the road stays clear. If any medical risk rides with you — sugar illness, medicines that move hunger or blood, pregnancy, eating trouble past or present — keep your clinician in charge and use this book for the belief alone.

Do not fear the machine. Revere it. It carried you through every ditch and still reports faithfully. Stop cutting its wires and it returns to baseline on its own, quietly, without struggle, the way water clears when the mud is no longer stirred.

You stand now where the builders lose their power. The aisle is seen. The mirror is seen. The mood loan is seen. The ropes are seen. The machine is heard. What remains is to shut out every voice that would rebuild the walls around you. Other diets, other theories, other well-meaning friends with tales of struggle, other adverts dressed as science — each would plant the lie again and call the mouse mighty. You have no need of them. You have sight.

**SUMMARY**

- Want feels personal but was planted by makers and rooms, not born in character.
- Craving once started carries the binge, and creep to larger hits shows the loop at work.
- Daily sweet comfort predicts flatter mood later rather than mending mood now.
- Firing at wrappers and hours is memory ringing, not an order to obey.
- Fear of falling and fear of flying pull opposite ways but share one owner.
- The body reports faithfully, and heeding hunger steadies days on its own.
