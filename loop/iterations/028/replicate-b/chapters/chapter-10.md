Chapter 10
NO SAFE SWEET

**IN THIS CHAPTER** — A Friday calendar circled in red, a cliff edge with a promised short fall, a kitchen box with one biscuit missing, a seatbelt on a night road

*Inside the line there is no small sweet, only the whole trap kept alive.*

You have seen the golden picture lose its gold.

Lights low, laughter rising, hand in hand — and the sweet in the middle adding nothing. The scene gave everything. The dose signed its name on your memory. You tested it with your own mouth and your own evenings. You know.

And right on cue, as it happens with every eater I have freed, a new voice arrives. Quieter. Reasonable. Almost helpful.

It says, “All right. I see it. So I will be sensible. I will allow Fridays. Just one. I will keep it out of the house except for birthdays. I will wean down. I will start properly tomorrow, after the holidays.”

I know that voice. We all know that voice. Warm to you, harsh to the lie behind it: that is not reason talking. That is the Sweet Con changing costume to survive.

We close every door now. Not with strain. With sight.

THE FRIDAY THAT ATE THE WEEK

Live one week with me exactly as the negotiator lives it, and watch what moderation does to a trapped eater.

Monday morning. You declare the rule. No BAD SUGAR in the week. Friday evening, the family bar, the film, the sofa. Just that. Sensible. Controlled. Adult.

Monday passes clean enough. There is a small lift in saying the rule out loud. You feel tidy. You tell yourself this time will be different because this time you are not banning, you are managing.

Tuesday, the thought arrives by mid-afternoon. Friday. Three days to go. You smile at the thought. The picture brightens. You picture the foil, the crack, the first melt while the credits roll. Your mouth waters a little. You tell yourself this proves control. Look closer. A free eater does not water at a calendar.

Wednesday, the glow grows. Work drags, and the mind slips forward. Only two more days. The Friday bar grows larger in imagination than any bar ever grew in the mouth. You rehearse which bar. Milk or dark. Large or family. You rehearse where you will sit. You rehearse the moment you will open it. You walk past a shop and your eyes flick to the bright wall without permission. You tell yourself you are only planning. Does that feel like freedom? Does a person who wants nothing spend Wednesday rehearsing Friday?

Thursday, the creep begins. We all know this creep. The rule says Friday. The mouth says Thursday is almost Friday. You have been good all week. You have managed to be good this week. Surely one square tonight will not ruin Friday. Surely starting the glow a little early proves how controlled you are. The hand moves toward the drawer for guests. The drawer for guests opens for the host. You take a square to take the edge off the waiting. Then the old hunt wakes.

You tell yourself Friday will still be clean. Friday will be the real one.

Friday arrives — and it cannot carry the weight you loaded on it. All week of waiting funnels into one evening. Hands shake a little as you open. First bite, flood, brief hush. Second bite, already less. Third bite, chasing the first. The bar that glowed all week in imagination dulls within minutes in the mouth. So you reach for a little more to recover the glow. The family bar thins. The evening that was meant to prove control ends in the same fog and second reaching you swore you had left.

Saturday morning, the rule lies broken. The voice rewrites quickly. Well, the week is spoiled now. I will restart Monday. A few more doses over the weekend will not matter. I will enjoy the last hurrah. Saturday afternoon, a packet. Saturday night, biscuits. Sunday, the I-need-to-eat-something-RIGHT-NOW feeling by four, answered with sugary somethings to get me through till dinner.

Monday, the same vow. Same red circle. Same creep.

Ask as an investigator, not as a judge.

Did the Friday rule make BAD SUGAR smaller in your life or larger? Did waiting five days make the dose less precious or more precious? Did one allowed evening quiet the thought or train the thought to shout all week toward Friday?

You know. We all know. The rule did not shrink the trap. The rule polished it.

A forbidden sweet grows a halo. A delayed sweet grows a spotlight. A rationed sweet grows a ceremony. Moderation does not teach the mouth indifference. Moderation teaches the mouth worship between doses.

That is why the Friday-allowance week always widens. Anticipation intensifies the glow. The glow drives the creep to Thursday. The disappointment of the dulled Friday drives the spill to Saturday. What was meant to be a fence becomes a funnel.

Hear the house-rule cousins speak in the same dialect and watch them fall the same way.

“I don't keep it in the house.” Who says that with pride on Monday, and who walks it back in through the door on Friday by their own hand? The house stays clean for three days while the mind stays dirty all week with planning. Then the bag arrives for the children, the tin arrives for visitors, the drawer fills for emergencies. Emergencies arrive nightly at ten. The rule about place becomes a rule about time, then a rule about amount, then a memory.

“Only at birthdays. Only on holiday. Only when offered to be polite.” How many birthdays does a year hold when you are hunting them? How long is a holiday when every café becomes an exception? How polite is a mouth that says yes while the heart sinks because the hunt has woken again for three days after one polite slice? Place-rules and date-rules do not weaken preciousness. They perfume it.

If cut-down worked, would you be reading this book? How many years have you run some version of Fridays only, weekends only, one square only, none in the house except — and where did each version lead? Back to daily grazing. Back to evening hunting. Back to the roller coaster you said you wanted to leave.

Which proves what? Not that you lack control. That control is the wrong tool for a con. You cannot control a lie by scheduling it. You dissolve it by seeing it.

ONE IS NEVER ONE

Hear the next negotiation, spoken in your own dialect.

“All right, forget the week. Just one. Surely one small sweet cannot matter. Surely I can take one and leave the rest.”

We have all spoken that line standing in kitchens at ten. We have all believed it for the three seconds before the hand moved.

Picture your own hand in memory. The sincere promise. The first bite, sweet flood. The second half calling before swallowing ends. The hand moving toward the packet while the mouth still chews. That old rustle that meant no return.

“I just crave sweets and when i eat them, i feel like i cant stop.” That is how trapped eaters describe it, plain and true. Not as theory. As witnessed evenings. The box empty before the film ends.

Why does one carry the binge? Not because you are weak. Not because you are broken for life. Hear me flat on this, because I will not label you and I will not frighten you: one carries the binge while the belief stays alive.

While one dose stays precious — while the mind still reads BAD SUGAR as pleasure, reward, comfort, fuel — the first taste re-lights the whole script. The Nibbler stirs, tiny and already starving, and whispers for its feed. The Sweet Con leans in and translates that whisper as proof. See? You needed it. Enjoy this. Have another. You have blown it now, so enjoy.

Three questions, and only honest answers will do.

When you took just one and stopped cleanly, was it because the one satisfied you — or because no more sat in reach, or eyes watched, or the packet could be closed without witnesses? Did the one close the hunt, or pause it till privacy?

And when you took just one with a full box beside you, alone, tired, at night, how often did one stay one? Once in twenty? Once in fifty? What does that ratio tell you about one — that one is safe, or that one is the trigger while belief feeds it?

And when one did stay one, what did the hours after feel like — clean indifference, or loud bargaining, staring at the remaining squares, proud and tormented together, thinking about sweets while not eating sweets? Is that freedom? Is that what you want for the rest of your years?

You know. We have all counted that ratio on our own skin.

This is not a life sentence. This is a mechanism seen clearly. While the belief lives, one awakens the hunt. When the belief dies, there is no hunt for one to awaken. The danger was never your character. The danger was the preciousness kept alive by the exception.

Which leaves only one stable answer inside the line. Not one. Not most days. None deliberately, ever, with rejoicing. Not because you must police yourself forever. Because there is nothing worth policing for.

It does plenty TO you. It does nothing FOR you.

Feel that land on the single-biscuit picture. What did that one do for the evening? Did it add rest? Did it add love? Did it add energy? Or did it do plenty to the evening — noise to attention, fog to head, ache to belly, hunt to midnight — while doing nothing for joy? The verdict stands on every kitchen floor you ever stood on.

THE YO-YO THAT FEEDS ITSELF

There is a deeper reason the rule always fails, and you have lived it under another name.

Call it diet week. Call it good week. Call it clean days and bad nights. The shape never changes. Tighten. Hold. Crave. Burst. Blame. Restart.

Monday to Thursday, tight. Forbidden must mean must-have by Wednesday. Friday, burst. Saturday, flood. Sunday, vow. The tighter the days, the louder the nights. The louder the nights, the tighter the next vow. Round and round on the same wheel, each turn grinding deeper groove.

We did that for years. We called the groove lack of will. It was not. It was schedule feeding craving.

Restriction by day intensifies dreaming by night. Dreaming intensifies relief at the burst. Relief teaches the mouth that bursts matter. Bursts teach the day that restriction must tighten. The wheel turns because the wheel is built to turn. Take the schedule away and the wheel has no axle.

Look at your own holidays without rules. Hours on a beach, hours in talk, hours in play, plain food eaten hungry, no thought of sweets for half a day. Then rules return, packets return, clocks return — and sudden urgency by hour. Was urgency born in the body? Or was urgency born in the pattern of deny then permit, deny then permit, till the mouth learned to shout in the gaps?

I speak as one who ran that wheel till exhaustion. White-knuckle days. Forbidden-must-have rebound. Monday restart. I thought I failed the method. The method failed me by leaving the object precious and asking my hands to fight my belief. Hands always lose that fight. Belief always wins. Change the belief and hands have nothing to fight.

So hear the weaning proposal in that light and watch it fall.

“I will cut down slowly. A little less each day. My body needs time.”

Whose need? The tiny physical echo passes in days, small and passing, not a lifelong battle. What needs weeks is the fantasy. Weaning feeds fantasy daily. A little less today means dreaming more tonight about tomorrow’s little less. Each reduced dose arrives wrapped in ceremony, attention fixed on taste, loss rehearsed in advance. Each gap between doses stretches into longing. The mouth learns to sip slower, close eyes longer, sigh deeper after each hit, as if saying goodbye to a lover. Goodbye rehearsed daily for a month is not goodbye. It is courtship.

Do you taper a con to see through it? Do you read half a lie daily to stop believing it? You see it whole, at once, and the hunger for it dies whole.

And hear the tomorrow proposal beside it, because tomorrow is weaning by another name.

“I will quit tomorrow. After the birthday. After the holidays. After this box.”

Three questions close this door for good.

First. If Friday sweets and birthday sweets and holiday sweets truly added joy, why would you need to quit after them — and if they add nothing, why feed the Sugar Trap another day to prove it? What does tomorrow buy except another turn of the wheel?

Second. When has tomorrow ever arrived as tomorrow? Has tomorrow not always arrived as today with a fresh excuse pinned to it? How many tomorrows have you already paid for with foggy mornings, with boxes hidden, with vows restarted?

Third. Do you tell a prisoner with open door to wait till Monday for freedom? Do you tell a person stepping out of smoke to finish the packet first? Which is kinder — another week of slavery to honour a date, or freedom now?

You know. We all know once dates are weighed. Tomorrow is the Con’s favourite room. It keeps the belief alive while promising to kill it later. “I’ll allow Fridays, I’ll wean, I’ll start after” are three costumes on one actor, and the actor is fear of living without the prop.

THE CLIFF WITH THE SAFE FALL

Stand with me now at the picture that kills cut-down forever. Live it fully, because half-looking lets it survive.

A high cliff over grey water. Wind tugging coats. Gulls crying far below. Wet grass at the edge, dark rock, white spit of surf. A guide beckons, cheerful, clipboard in hand.

“Jump,” he says. “Only fall a few metres. Use this special Friday parachute. Land on that ledge. Safe. Controlled. Sensible.”

You peer over. The ledge is narrow, wet, crumbling at the edge. The drop below runs sheer. The wind gusts sideways. Your stomach tightens in the honest way stomachs tighten at edges.

“Only a few metres?” you ask.

“Absolutely,” he says. “Just desserts on Fridays. Just one at birthdays. Just a taste to be polite. Just none in the house except the drawer for guests. Just wean. Just tomorrow. Just the soft stuff. Just this once to prove you can.”

Would you jump? Would you trust a small fall off a high cliff because the guide promises you will stop mid-air? Would you call that control — or madness dressed as moderation?

Yet that is exactly what the special sweet asks. Jump into the Sugar Trap provided you only fall a little. Feed the Nibbler provided you only feed it Fridays. Keep the Sweet Con alive provided you only consult it at celebrations.

Gravity does not negotiate. Neither does preciousness.

One ledge crumbles to Thursday. One taste widens to the box. One drawer for guests opens at ten for the host. One Friday glows till it lights the whole week. One polite slice wakes three days of hunting. The fall never stops where promised because the belief that makes it a fall stays intact.

Ask with me, plain and stern, because I warn as an escaped eater who stood at that edge for years.

If there is no benefit in many, what benefit lives in one? If the object does plenty to you and nothing for you daily, does arithmetic turn poison in sweet coat to nourishment on Fridays? Does a con become honest when consulted occasionally? Does a trap become safe when visited weekly?

It is the other way around. The occasional visit keeps the trap sacred. The sacred visit keeps the hunger lit. The lit hunger guarantees the daily return.

There is a second edge to see from that cliff, and it faces backward.

Look at the ground you stand on when you do not jump. Solid grass. Clear air. Shops passed without pull. Evenings closed clean. Hunger arriving cleanly and leaving cleanly after plain food enjoyed hungry. Laughter unbroken by rustle. Mornings clear. That ground is wide. It holds.

Look at the guide’s face again. He is not evil. He is trapped politely himself, selling small falls because large falls frightened him. Pity him. Do not follow him.

There is no safe fall inside this cliff. There is only stepping back from the edge with relief and getting on with enjoying your life on solid ground.

THE SEATBELT ON THE NIGHT ROAD

At this point every clear eater asks the honest question, and you should ask it now before fear asks it for you.

“What if a crumb slips in? What if a sauce hides sugar? What if a kind hand offers cake and I taste before I see? Have I failed? Must I start again?”

Breathe. This is where sight replaces panic.

Picture a night road, headlights steady, engine warm. Hedges sliding past, white line unreeling, radio low. You wear a seatbelt. Not because you plan to crash. Not because you drive erratically to test it. Because roads carry jolts you do not steer.

The seatbelt exists for the accidental jolt, never as permission to steer into walls.

So it runs with BAD SUGAR. Your body, that marvellous machine that makes its own fuel from plain meals and meets true hunger with satisfaction, can cope with an occasional accidental blip — a hidden spoon in sauce, a mistaken bite, a crumb unnoticed. The body absorbs and moves on. No doom. No crash. No restarting of slavery.

But the mind cannot afford a deliberate one.

An accidental taste, noticed, smiled at, left behind, revives nothing. A deliberate exception — chosen, planned, romanticized as deserved or special — revives everything, because it revives the belief. The seatbelt saves the passenger who hits a bump. It does not save the driver who steers off the road weekly to prove the belt works.

That distinction guards you without vigilance. You do not need to police every label with dread. You need to guard the belief with joy. Eat when hungry. Stop when satisfied. Enjoy real food as favourite. When an accident happens, note it, laugh lightly, move on. When the thought of planning an accident arrives — Friday, birthday, just one to be polite — see it for what it is. Not your reasoning. The Con’s script, arriving pre-labelled.

And one spoken care beside the road, because bodies differ and I will not play doctor with your life: if you live with diabetes or take medication that moves blood sugar or appetite, if you are pregnant, if food has ever gripped you into illness or distress, keep your clinician in charge of anything medical and use this book for the belief alone. If you ever feel faint, ill, or distressed around food, seek care promptly and set these pages aside till you are cleared.

No panic. No failure machinery. Belief guarded, body trusted, help honoured where help belongs.

Do you see how light this makes you? Not a tightrope walker counting steps. A free eater on a wide road, belt worn lightly, eyes on faces and hills, not on crumbs. A rumble, if it comes, is a lesson to slow down, never proof you have blown it. And a deliberate swerve is never licensed by the belt. Got away with it never means do it again.

“Just this once to prove I am free.” Free from what, by feeding what? Does a prisoner prove freedom by visiting the cell Fridays? Does a clear eater prove ease by scheduling doses? Freedom proves itself by forgetting — hours in work and laughter with no thought of packets, shops passed without pull, evenings closed clean.

THIS SOUNDS EXTREME

Now meet the last guard, the one who defends the method of moderation itself. He speaks reasonably. He sounds kind.

“You people are extreme. Totality is fanatical. Normal people eat sweets normally. Just keep it in moderation. Just keep it out of the house. Just have willpower like the rest of us.”

Pity him, but do not obey him. His moderation is not proof of ease. His moderation is proof the trap still holds him politely.

Ask three questions whose only honest answer retires his school.

First. If moderation is normal and easy, why does his normal require rules, drawers, house bans, good weeks and bad nights, and talk of being good? Does a free eater need rules for something indifferent? Do you need a house ban on boiled cardboard? Do you need a Friday calendar for plain bread?

Second. When he says “I don’t keep it in the house,” who emptied the house — and who filled it last Friday by his own hand? When he says “just one,” where does his one lead by nine o’clock? Has his sensible system ever delivered a year of thought-free ease — or a year of thinking about sweets while eating sweets to stop thinking about sweets?

Third. If totality is extreme, why do clear eaters live wider, not narrower — plain meals enjoyed hungry, birthdays sung with full hearts, cinemas laughed through with empty hands, mornings clear? The fact is totality ends all bargaining in one stroke. Is stepping back from a cliff extreme — or is scheduling small falls extreme?

You know. We all know once teachers are weighed by fruits. The voice that leaves you dreaming, counting, rehearsing Fridays, creeping to Thursdays, spilling to Saturdays, has not freed you. It has caged you with a calendar. The sight that leaves you indifferent, present, done when satisfied, has freed you.

Hear the weaning teacher beside him, white coat of kindness on.

“Slow down gently. Your body will protest. Your mind will shatter. Taper with care.”

Whose protest? The Nibbler is tiny and already dying. Its grumble passes in days. What shatters is not the body. What shatters is the ceremony that weaning polishes daily. Each farewell dose says, this matters, this is precious, this is hard to leave. Farewell said twenty times becomes twenty lessons in preciousness. One clear seeing says, this does nothing for me. That lesson lasts.

I do not ask you to trust me because I use strong words. I ask you to trust your own Fridays. Your own boxes. Your own wheel. Evidence you already own beats any lecture.

Totality is not harshness. Totality is kindness. One rule that ends all rules. One closing that ends all bargaining. No halo. No spotlight. No ceremony. The object returns to what it always was — noise doing plenty to you, nothing for you — and attention returns to where joy always lived. People. Rest. Hunger met. Work absorbed. Laughter unbroken by rustle.

It does plenty TO you. It does nothing FOR you.

Marvellous relief. Wonderful lightness. Freedom felt as gain, never as loss.

11. NEVER KEEP A SPECIAL SWEET
One exception keeps the whole Trap alive.

Step back from the edge lightly. No small sweet. No Friday shrine. No guest drawer that calls at ten. No weaning parade. No tomorrow. The Sugar Trap lives only where belief feeds it. Starve the belief and the Nibbler starves with it, tiny and already dying. Rejoice you are free to forget it. Get on with enjoying your life with empty hands and a full heart.

**SUMMARY**

- A Friday rule makes the dose more precious, so waiting glows and the glow spills to Thursday and Saturday.
- One taste re-lights the hunt while the belief stays precious, which is why one rarely stays one.
- Tight days drive burst nights, so the deny-permit wheel turns itself.
- A small fall off a high cliff is still a fall, which is why cut-down and weaning cannot hold.
- An accidental crumb is a bump the body absorbs, while a planned exception steers back into the trap.
- The voice that calls totality extreme still lives by rules and dreaming, which proves moderation is the cage.
