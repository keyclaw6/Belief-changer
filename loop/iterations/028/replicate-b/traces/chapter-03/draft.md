Chapter 3
WHAT DOES IT DO FOR YOU

**IN THIS CHAPTER** — A Tuesday notebook, a till receipt, the bathroom mirror, crumbs in the bin, one quiet question

*Forget what it costs you for a moment and answer this honestly — what does it ever actually do for you?*

YOU SAW THE HAND

You saw it last night. The hand moving before thought. The box emptying while you watched.

You came in telling yourself one, and you left with crumbs on your fingers and that cold wash of what is wrong with me. We stood there together. I have stood there a thousand times. You are not weak. You were caught.

Good. Keep that clarity, because we need it for the next step.

Most sweet-eaters I meet say something like this when I sit down with them:

“Look, I know it is not perfect for me. I know my teeth, my weight, my money. But I love it. It gets me through. Life would be grey without my little treat.”

Do you hear the move in that sentence? Harm admitted, benefit defended. As if we are in a courtroom weighing harm on one side and pleasure on the other, and pleasure wins.

I understand that voice. I spoke it for years. We all did. We learned to count ourselves as moderate, sensible people having a harmless pleasure. A biscuit with tea. A chocolate after a hard day. Nothing crazy. Everyone eats it.

I want to take that courtroom apart, gently, because it is the wrong courtroom. While we argue harm against pleasure, the real question never gets asked.

I am not going to lecture you about harm. I am not going to list diseases or wave a finger. You already know sweet hits cost teeth and money and time. If harm settled it, you would have walked away long ago. Harm does not settle it, because the belief holding you is not about harm.

The belief holding you is about gift.

You believe, somewhere low and unexamined, that BAD SUGAR hands you something real. A lift. A reward. A lightening. Something that makes the day sweeter and more bearable.

I am going to ask you to put harm to one side entirely and look only at that supposed gift. Not what it takes. What it gives.

That is the only ledger that matters.

THE TUESDAY AUDIT

Let us do something plain and honest together. No theory. One ordinary Tuesday.

Take yesterday, or any recent normal day. Not a binge day. Not a good week Monday. A middle day, the kind you call moderate. Run it hour by hour with me, notebook open.

Morning. Tea with two sugars, or a sweetened cereal you eat standing up. Mid-morning at the desk or in the kitchen, a biscuit with the second cup, then another because the packet is open. Lunch, a sandwich and a fizzy drink, or a dessert you did not really plan but took because it was there. Afternoon, the drawer opens. A bar, a roll, sugary somethings to get me through the slide to five. Evening, dinner, then something sweet while the television murmurs. Then the 10pm reach you already know. A biscuit that becomes four.

Now, beside each dose, write two columns. Not good and bad. Something simpler.

Column one: what did it do TO you?
Column two: what did it do FOR you?

Stay with column one first, because it is easy to fill and your own eyes can fill it without my help.

What did it do TO you? Count the money. Keep one till receipt from a normal week and look at it. The bars, the packets, the bottles, the bakery bag, the extra trolley items that slid in because the sweet eye was hungry. Week after week, month after month. Not a fortune in a day. A steady leak. Money walking out for something you needed to buy again the next day.

Count the time. The queue for the vending machine. The trip to the shop in the rain because the cupboard ran out. The minutes scrolling delivery apps hunting the right dessert. The evening eaten in small rustles and chews that never made an evening.

Stand in front of the bathroom mirror at night. Look at the teeth. I speak plainly because I care about you, and I will add the human guard at once — if you live with diabetes, take anything that touches blood sugar or appetite, are pregnant, or carry any eating or medical worry, talk to your clinician first and follow their advice; this book changes a belief, it does not give medical care.

Here is the settled fact about teeth, stated without scare and without softening. Frequent free-sugar doses feed the plaque on teeth, the plaque makes acid, the acid eats enamel, and holes follow. Brushing and fluoride matter, of course they matter, any dentist will tell you so, and I am not giving dental advice here. I am naming a mechanism you already know from your own mouth. That furry feel after a sweet afternoon. The ache that throbs after something cold. The filling that needs redoing. The cleaning that costs more than the sweets ever did. Frequent doses keep the acid coming. That is what frequent doses do.

Now widen the lens for a breath. Population guidance on free sugars sets a low daily ceiling, and ordinary eating now sits far above it. Feeling fine does not mean intake is low. Most of us were stunned when we first added up a normal day and saw how far past guidance a moderate day already runs. I am not saying any single bar seals your fate, and I am not saying a number on a page is poison in any dose. I am saying the normal you call moderate is not small, and feeling normal inside it proves nothing about the level.

That is column one, and it fills fast. Money TO the till. Time TO the hunt. Acid TO the teeth. A day tilted, hour by hour, toward the next dose.

No lecture. Just your Tuesday, added up.

NOW THE OTHER COLUMN

Now column two. The column that keeps you trapped.

What did it do FOR you?

I mean truly FOR you. Not what it cost and you forgave. Not what it risked and you excused. What gift did it place in your hand that you could not have had without it?

Write it down. Be specific. Yesterday at 11am, what did that biscuit do FOR you? Yesterday at 3.40pm, what did that bar do FOR you? Last night at 10pm, what did those four biscuits huddled after the first one do FOR you?

This is where the moderate story goes quiet.

We are fluent in harm. We can list harm all night. We stumble on benefit the moment we are asked to name it plainly, in daylight, dose by dose. We say words like treat, lift, reward, comfort, energy. Big warm words. But point to the hour. Point to the gift.

Did the morning sugar make you bright? Or did you need the mid-morning biscuit because the brightness never arrived? Did the afternoon bar clear the fog? Or did the fog return at four and call for a second reach? Did the evening box soothe you? Or did you fold the empty packet small and feel flat and faintly disgusted with yourself while the television still murmured?

Be exact. If a friend asked you, what did you enjoy yesterday, what would you name? The walk to the shop in fresh air? The laugh at lunch? The hot tea itself? The ten minutes sitting down? The programme you watched? Take BAD SUGAR out of the picture and does any of that joy vanish? Or was the sweet only in the room while joy happened elsewhere?

I know how hard this is to accept, and I welcome your doubt. Bring it close. Hold your favourite dose in mind, the one you would defend to the end, and ask the auditor question: what did that mouthful add that the moment did not already have?

You will find your mind reaching for a phrase you have carried since childhood. That phrase is the whole trap in four words. You believe BAD SUGAR is a genuine treat or fuel. A treat that lights the day. A fuel that carries you through. A small love. A small power.

Say that phrase slowly to yourself. A genuine treat or fuel. Feel how much work those words do. Treat says love, reward, celebration. Fuel says strength, energy, need. Put together they say life is greyer and weaker without it. No wonder the hand reaches. Who would refuse love and strength?

But have you ever tested that phrase against your Tuesday?

A genuine treat leaves the treated person glad. Look at your 10pm face in the mirror. Does that look glad? A genuine fuel leaves the user stronger for longer. Look at your 4pm fog and your second reach. Does that look fuelled? A genuine gift can be taken or left with equal ease. Look at your hand moving before thought. Does that look free?

I speak as one who defended that phrase for years. I called it my reward. I called it my lift. I logged day after day and found the same blank. The treat never treated. The fuel never fuelled. The words were beautiful. The column stayed empty.

What if you learned that someone had been tripping you quietly every morning so he could offer you a hand up and be thanked — would you call him a rescuer or would you see the trick?

Hold that question. Do not answer too fast. Let your Tuesday answer it.

THE VERDICT ON TUESDAY

Let me state what your audit already states, flatly, as one escaper to another:

It does plenty TO you. It does nothing FOR you.

Read that twice. Let it sit without argument.

It does plenty TO you. Money TO the shops. Minutes TO the hunt. Acid TO the teeth. A day chopped into reaches. A night that ends in crumbs. That column overflows. You wrote it yourself.

It does nothing FOR you. No hour yesterday was brighter because of the dose. No task was done better because of the hit. No love was added. No strength was lent. The pleasure you remember is thin, hurried, half-tasted, gone before the swallow ends, followed by the call for another. That is not a gift column. That is an empty column with a beautiful label stuck on it.

Do you object, “but I like it”? I hear you, and I ask with warmth: which part did you like? The first three seconds before the restlessness returned? The tenth hurried chew eaten standing up? The folding of the empty packet? The child with a peach knows liking — hunger, joy, enough, run back to play. The 10pm eater knows chasing. Point to the liking you defend and tell me where the joy sits in that blur.

Do you object, “but everyone eats it, it must give something”? I hear that too. Ask it plainly: if everyone doing it proved gift, would your Tuesday column be full? Would one be enough? Would the hand wait for permission? Normal does not mean generous. It means widely caught.

Do you object, “but I was good all week, surely my Friday sweet rewarded me”? Ask your Friday night for evidence. Did one reward and stop the hand? Or did the permitted one light the fuse for the box, and leave you flat for the rest of your life if you keep repeating that circle? And turn the phrase around, because both sides matter: imagine mornings, shops, meals lived clear, hunger met, satisfaction heard, no 10pm blur — to live bright and free for the rest of your life, with the same days minus the reaches. Which rest-of-life do you want to sign? The one where the empty column keeps costing, or the one where the empty column is seen as empty?

The fact is, there is no benefit to weigh against harm, because there is no benefit. There is only a belief reading a tiny body echo as a gift. You feel a low hum, you take a dose, the hum quietens for minutes, and the mind writes thank you on a trick. Later we will look closely at that hum and its feeder, the tiny grumble and the story that feeds it. For now only hold the shape: an emptiness, a dose, a brief quiet, a louder emptiness. That circle is not generosity. It is a handout from the same hand that took.

So the courtroom collapses. Harm against benefit was the wrong trial. There is harm, yes, plenty TO you. There is no benefit, nothing FOR you. Once the second column is seen as empty, what is left to weigh?

Nothing to weigh. Only a con to leave.

3. JUDGE ONLY BY WHAT IT DOES FOR YOU
Forget harm versus benefit and ask for the benefit.

You came in believing harm might be outweighed. You leave this room with a notebook that will not lie. Tuesday cost you money, time, teeth, ease — and handed you nothing you can point to and keep. That blank is not your failure. That blank is the truth about BAD SUGAR, seen at last in your own handwriting.

Bring that blank with you. Do not fill it with old phrases. Do not lend it beauty it never earned. Keep it empty, and keep walking. The next room will show you why strong people failed inside this emptiness — and why that failure was never yours.

**SUMMARY**

- The harm debate hides the real test, which is to name one true gift dose by dose.
- A normal Tuesday logged hour by hour fills fast with costs to money, time, mood and teeth.
- Frequent sweet doses feed plaque acid that eats enamel, while brushing care still matters.
- Ordinary moderate eating already runs far above population guidance, and feeling fine does not make it small.
- The benefit column stays empty when each dose is examined, not remembered in warm words.
- Seeing the empty column removes the weighing act and leaves a trick to escape.
