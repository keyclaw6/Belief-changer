Chapter 13
THAT'S JUST HOW I AM

IN THIS CHAPTER
The kitchen table in 1972, the same table in 2026, the school photograph in the hallway, the supermarket aisle that did not exist when your grandparents shopped, the mirror where you practised saying it's just my nature

*You are not a sugar person who got trapped — you are a trapped person who was told you are a sugar person.*

### THE LABEL THAT EXPLAINED EVERYTHING

There comes a point when argument is almost done and something small, stubborn and strangely comforting steps in front of the door.

It says: none of that applies to me. I am just like this.

I have a sweet tooth. I have always had a sweet tooth. My mother did, my father did, it runs in the family. I have an addictive personality. If it were not sugar it would be something else. It is genetic.

I understand why you say it. I said it myself for years. It sounds like honesty. It sounds like self-knowledge. It even sounds humble — I am not blaming anyone, I am just describing what I am.

And because it sounds like identity, it feels unarguable. You can argue with a habit. You cannot argue with who you are. So you stop trying. You make peace with a life tired, wired and craving, because peace with a cage is easier than believing the cage was built around you.

We need to look at that sentence together, warmly and plainly, because it is the last kind thing the trap says to keep you inside. It does not shout. It whispers: do not bother. This is just how you are.

Do you want to be free? Of course you want to be free. That is why this thought hurts. It tells you freedom is not for people like you.

Is it true?

Think of the table where you first learned it.

For most of us the memory is warm and ordinary. A tin on the worktop. A grandmother who showed love with a biscuit. A father who came home tired and made himself a sweet tea and said, that is my treat, I have earned it. A child — you — given something sweet when you cried, or when you were good, or when everyone laughed around a birthday. No one meant a trap. Everyone meant love.

Little by little you collected evidence. You were the one who always wanted seconds. You were the one who could not leave the packet alone. Your brother could take one and leave it; you heard yourself say, I just crave sweets and when I eat them I feel like I can't stop, and you believed that sentence was a report on your character. Your family even laughed kindly, you have always had a sweet tooth, just like your mum. It became a story about you.

We slip into that story because it seems to explain the mess. The drawer you open at 3pm, the bargaining — I've managed to be good this week so just a treat, once a week is fine — the evening when one bite becomes the box. If you are a sweet-toothed person, all of that makes sense. The cause is inside you. The behaviour follows.

I want to ask a very simple question, and I want you to answer it from your own life before you answer it from theory.

When did you decide that?

At what stage did you sit down, calmly, and decide that a particular kind of engineered dose was your nature? Did you weigh your genes, your childhood, your temperament, and conclude, I am the type who needs BAD SUGAR? Or did you find yourself doing it and then reach for the nearest story that would make the doing feel inevitable?

The fact is we never thought it over when we found it. We found a hit that relieved a faint discomfort we did not understand, we did it again, the discomfort returned, we relieved it again, and after enough loops we looked back and said, that must be who I am. The label followed the loop. It did not create it.

That matters because a label that follows a trap feels like proof of the trap, and then it protects the trap.

### THE WORLD CHANGED. YOUR GENES DID NOT.

Let us look outside the house for a moment, because inside the house everything feels personal. Outside, you can see scale.

Your grandparents did not live in the world you shop in. In their streets there was no wall of bright packets at child height, no bliss-pointed biscuits engineered to melt and crunch and bypass fullness, no rows of sugary drinks sweating by the checkout, no office drawer stocked as standard at 3pm. When they ate, they ate food. When they celebrated, they celebrated with people, not with a wrapper that had been tuned in a laboratory to call to you.

If being a sweet-toothed, addictive-personality type were fixed in your genes, what would you expect to see across those decades? You would expect the number of people caught in this way to be roughly steady. Genes do not rewrite themselves in fifty years.

What do we see? We see a flood. On every high street, in every trolley, in every child's lunchbox where there was once an apple there is now a packet. The bodies in old school photographs look different not because the children had different genes, but because the supply changed. The trap moved from rare to everywhere.

That one observation dissolves the genetic story without a lecture. The world changed. You did not. The trap was built around a normal brain.

And here is where honesty requires me to tell you what science does and does not agree on, because this is exactly the place where the Big Confection likes to sell certainty in either direction.

Leading Cambridge neuroscientists, after reviewing the same animal binge models that get called proof of sugar addiction, argue the bingeing reflects the pattern of access — intermittent, restrict-then-binge, on-off — more than any singular chemistry of sugar itself. They point out that when animals have sugar freely available they do not binge in that way, and that in humans the evidence for a recognised clinical withdrawal syndrome from sugar alone remains thin. It remains an open scientific question, not a settled consensus.

Other groups read the same animal data — bingeing, craving signs, dopamine release on each binge in the wanting circuit, a measured dip below baseline after, opioid cue circuits lighting up at the sight or smell of a wrapper — as a well-defined but mild dependence. They describe the loop as real and well-defined but small in magnitude, and human work that has tried to measure a hump after cutting down describes, where anything is felt at all, a brief peak in the first days that then passes.

Both camps, even while they disagree on the label, grant the same two facts that matter to you: binge behaviours do happen when access is intermittent, and the dopamine hit is wanting, not liking — it re-arms the next want, it does not prove a genuine pleasure. Contested does not mean imaginary. Supported does not mean severe.

I tell you that not to make you fear a diagnosis and not to deny your experience. Your experience — the yo-yo, the one bite becoming the box, the I-need-to-eat-something-RIGHT-NOW feeling — is real and shared. It is common, not a personal flaw, and the scales that try to measure food-addiction-like scores find similar answers in about one person in seven when they ask the same questions of alcohol or tobacco populations. Those are self-report scores, not a disease stamped on you, not a medical verdict on your future. I mention it for one reason only: you are not uniquely broken. You are not the only one who heard celebration needs chocolate and discovered that belief keeps the loop alive. That is circuitry and schedule, not character.

Do you see what that does to the label?

If many different people, with many different genes and personalities, fall into the same loop when the schedule is intermittent and the product is engineered to call, then the loop cannot be proof of your nature. It is proof of the trap's design. The sugar person did not build the Sugar Trap. The Sugar Trap built the sugar person.

And the sentence you practised in the mirror — that's just how I am — is the Big Confection speaking in your voice. It was the Big Confection that taught you to read a faint, empty, slightly edgy, slightly restless feeling as a message about who you are, rather than as the quiet complaint of a small mechanism you have been feeding without knowing.

Think of it plainly. That tug is the Nibbler dying, and it is small, brief, and already on its way out when you stop re-arming it. It was the Big Confection that employed it and told you its tug meant you needed BAD SUGAR, that you were wired to love it. Remove the employer and the tug has no job. You do not need to change your nature to starve it. You only need to stop believing the job description.

### IT IS THE OTHER WAY AROUND

This is the inversion that finishes the identity excuse, and once you see it you cannot unsee it.

You believed: I have these traits — sweet tooth, addictive personality, comfort-seeking, family pattern — therefore I get hooked.

The truth is: you got hooked, therefore you developed the traits that look like proof.

That is why the traits cluster so neatly in trapped people. The person who grazes, binges, or negotiates daily is tired, wired and craving not because they are constitutionally tired, wired and craving, but because intermittent doses produce exactly that weather. The person who says they think about food all the time is not obsessed by nature; they are responding to cue circuits that have been trained to fire at wrappers, smells, and the clock. The child who once ate when hungry and stopped when full learns to eat on schedule, to eat the engineered packet, to get the short lift inevitably followed by the fast drop, and then to explain the drop as need. The behaviour teaches the belief. The belief then hires the behaviour again.

Ask yourself: before the loop was established, did you feel you had a sweet tooth? Most of us did not. We liked a sweet taste when it was there, as children like it, but we did not feel we were a sweet-toothed person. The personhood arrived after the repetition, the same way a person who wears tight shoes all day starts to think of themselves as a person who loves taking tight shoes off. The relief is real. The story about who you are is backwards.

It is the other way around.

You are not a comfort eater who needs BAD SUGAR because you are uncomfortable. You became uncomfortable in that specific, restless, faintly edgy way because you were fed intermittent BAD SUGAR. You are not an addictive personality who therefore likes the hit. You practised a loop that looks exactly like the behaviour both scientific camps agree happens under intermittent access, and then you called the loop your personality.

That is not a criticism of you. We all did it. We were given a confidence trick at a table that meant love, and we did what any trusting person does when given phoney information: we acted on it, and then we explained ourselves with the information we had been given.

Look at the family table again with this lens.

You see your mother reaching for something sweet at the same time each evening. As a child you thought, she needs it, that is her nature. Now you see a woman who was herself recruited by a new food environment, who felt the same faint, empty, slightly edgy, slightly restless feeling you feel, who was told by every advert that this was a treat, a reward, a comfort, and who did what the trap invited. You see your own hand reaching, not because your hand is different from your brother's, but because your schedule, your stress, your shop, your drawer, your particular route through a world now engineered to call, gave the Big Confection more chances to speak in your ear. The same brain, different exposure, different loop. No destiny in it.

And consider the gentlest fact of all: even if you had come into this life more sensitive, more novelty-loving, more intense in how you want — even if you were the most comfort-seeking, most easily hooked person in the room — this method would still free you. That is the graceful concession the trap hopes you will never grant yourself. Because freedom here does not require you to become a different temperament. It only requires you to see where the want came from.

You do not need to edit your genes. You do not need to re-make your personality. You need to stop feeding the story that keeps the Nibbler employed. The temperament stays. The trap goes. You remain you — only clean, light, steady, and no longer interrupted.

Have you noticed how this reframe returns dignity? It says you were not weak. You were outgunned. Armies of engineers tuned crunch and melt and bliss-point to bypass your natural fullness, to make a dose feel like love, fuel, reward, celebration. A lone person against that is not weak-willed. They are a normal person in a trap that removed their choice after the first exposure on false information. That is the confidence trick: not foolishness, but deceit. And deceit does not require a particular personality to work. It only requires a human brain.

So when the thought comes, I am just wired to love sugar, answer it plainly, in your own voice, as a person who has looked:

No. I was wired to love ease, company, laughter, a body that feels steady after a good meal. BAD SUGAR sneaked a ride on those wires. The wires are mine. The rider is leaving.

### EVEN IF IT WERE TRUE

Let us concede the strongest form of your objection, because conceding it and still escaping is the proof this is not a trick.

Suppose you do have the most crave-prone temperament anyone could have. Suppose your family story is entirely true in the way you fear — you really are more drawn to intensity, to sweetness, to the quick hit, to the late-night packet. Suppose, for the sake of argument, the label fits.

Will you?

If temperament were the cause, you would need to change temperament to be free. And temperament is supposed to be fixed, so you would be stuck for the rest of your life — hooked for the rest of your life — a sentence.

But here you are, reading a book that does not ask you to change your temperament at all. It asks you to change one belief: that BAD SUGAR does something for you. That belief is not temperament. It is an idea that was installed, and an idea can be uninstalled without you becoming a different person.

The fact is you do not have to become someone else to stop feeding BAD SUGAR. You do not have to become disciplined, or calm, or a different family, or a person without desire. You only have to see that the desire you call sweet tooth is not a tooth at all. It is a loop: a mild, empty tug fed by a big story. Starve the story and the tug starves with it. Your intensity, your love of life, your warmth — all of that stays, and now it has somewhere better to go. You can get excited about that, because nothing about you needs to be lost.

Do you want to be free? Of course you want to be free — that is why this chapter is rising, not pressing down. You came in believing you were the problem. You leave seeing you were the host of a small parasite with a large press office. The parasite is the Nibbler, already dying. The press office is the Big Confection, and you have just withdrawn its licence to speak for you.

Feel what that means tomorrow morning, tonight, at the next table where sweets are passed. The thought will arise, as thoughts do. You will hear the old line: that's just how I am. Do not fight it. Do not suppress it. Reframe it, joyfully, in the way we have practised.

Rather than think, this is my nature, think: fantastic, that old story has no employer now. The faint tug, if it comes at all, is only habit losing its job. It passes. I remain. I am free to get on with enjoying my life.

That is not positive thinking. It is accurate thinking. You are not a sugar person. You are a person who was recruited, who learned a loop, who explained the loop with a story about self, and who can now see the loop for what it is and walk away from it without a fight.

Guard that belief. Guard it kindly, without vigilance, the way you guard any true thing you have seen and do not wish to pretend you have not seen. Let the shop aisle be an aisle. Let the family photograph be love without a tin. Let your own body be the authority that tells you when you are hungry and when you are satisfied, clean and steady without the yo-yo.

You did not fail because you are you. You were kept because you believed what the trap told you about you. Change that, and the door that was already open stays open for the rest of your life — not hooked for the rest of your life, but free for the rest of your life, with the same horizon and the opposite meaning.

You have nothing to become. You have something to cease believing. And the moment you cease, you are already out — marvellously, wonderfully, excitedly out — and you can rejoice that the enemy you were told was yourself was never yourself at all.

SUMMARY
* The label that's just how I am did not cause the loop — the loop created the label as an after-the-fact explanation.
* Population change proves the trap, not your genes — the food environment and schedule changed in decades; genes did not.
* Science on sugar addiction remains contested — both sides agree intermittent access drives binge-like behaviour and wanting, not liking — and shared scores are common, not a personal diagnosis.
* Traits like sweet tooth and addictive personality are the result of the trap, not its cause — tired, wired and craving is the weather inside the Sugar Trap, not your nature.
* Even if you were the most crave-prone person, freedom does not require becoming someone else — starve the belief and the small physical tug starves with it.
