Chapter 8
WHO BUILT THIS TRAP

IN THIS CHAPTER
The aisle at eye level, the bright wrapper, the checkout queue, the white-coated laboratory benches, the hand that reaches before you decide

*You did not invent that craving — it was engineered for you before you walked into the shop.*

### IT FELT LIKE YOU

For years you told yourself a private story about why your hand moved.

That you love sweet things. That you have no discipline around biscuits. That other people can take one and leave the rest, and you cannot, so the flaw must be in you.

I want to lift that story off you, because it was never true.

We fell into the Sugar Trap the same way we fall into any confidence trick. The trick does not work because you are foolish. It works because someone sold you phoney information while you were not looking, and then your own brain did the rest. You were conned, not defective. That is not a softening of the language. It is the mechanism.

You did not walk down that aisle and freely choose a dose because you are a sweet-toothed person. You walked down an aisle that was built to choose you.

Look at that aisle with me, as it is.

At eye level, exactly where your eyes fall when you are tired, are the hits that are sweet and fat together. The biscuit that snaps, then melts. The chocolate that is smooth, then crunch. The pastry that is salted on top and sugared underneath. Nothing on that shelf is there by accident. Every colour was tested. Every crinkle of the wrapper was tested. Every word — treat, reward, share, love — was tested.

You thought you were standing in a shop. You were standing at the end of a pipeline. And at the other end of that pipeline is a room you never saw.

### THE LABORATORY YOU NEVER SAW

I want to take you into that room, because once you have seen it, you cannot unsee it, and the anger that follows is the anger that frees you.

It is a bright, white room. Quiet. Stainless benches. A team in white coats, not one person but armies of them, paid to do one job for months and years: make you want it, want it faster, and want it again sooner.

They start with a plain food — flour, fat, sugar, salt. Real food would satisfy you and release you. That will not do. They tune it.

A little more sugar until the brain says want. A little more fat until it says want more. A little salt until the sweetness shines brighter. Then they tune the shape: the crunch that shatters at exactly the bite, the melt that coats the mouth and vanishes, so the brain never gets the signal that food has arrived and it asks for another hit before the first is finished. They tune the bliss-point — the perfect peak of sugar, fat and salt where craveability spikes and satiety disappears. Not too little to ignore, not so much you stop. The peak. The hook.

They bypass you. Your body is a marvellous piece of sophistication. Your tongue tells your brain what you have eaten. Your gut tells your brain you are satisfied. Your brain then tells you, quietly and cleanly: enough. It is an elegant loop, honed over millions of years to keep you steady, light, and free.

Their product is designed to break that loop.

The fact is refined carbohydrates and fats evoke striatal dopamine akin to addictive substances, and the fat-plus-sugar combo seems to have a supra-additive effect — the combined hit seems greater than either alone. I say seems because that is the honest word; the pattern is there, the proof is not yet clinched. But the intent is not contested. The food is engineered to crowd the same wanting circuit that drugs crowd, by design, and the most craveable product is the one that mixes fat and sugar together so the brain gets hit from two sides at once.

This is not food that happens to be sweet. This is a crude fix built to override a sophisticated system.

You did not crave too much. You were given something engineered to make you crave.

Do you feel the shift? The hand that reached for the wrapper at the checkout — the wrapper that called you before you decided — that hand was not confessing a character flaw. It was completing a circuit they soldered for you.

And you were not weak for completing it. You were outgunned.

### WHY ONE BITE CALLS THE NEXT

Now I will show you what that engineered hit does inside you, so you see why one bite becomes the box.

The fact is each sugary binge releases dopamine in the nucleus accumbens — the very centre of wanting. Not liking. Wanting.

Liking is the pleasure of eating a good meal and feeling satisfied. Wanting is the nagging tug that says have another, now.

Each dose does not give you pleasure. It re-arms the want. You feel the hit for a few minutes as relief — because it briefly quietens the faint, low-grade want the last hit created — and at the exact same moment it plants the next want. The circuit fires, says more, and drops you again.

You thought the chocolate gave you something. The chocolate made you want chocolate. It's the other way around.

That is why the same product that promises reward leaves you tired, wired and craving. That is why you could be full from dinner and still hear the call from the kitchen. The call was not hunger. It was circuitry.

Are you seeing who the villain is now? It is not you. The Big Confection — that big story that told you BAD SUGAR is a genuine pleasure or treat, that tells you you need a fix to get through — did not grow in a vacuum. It was fed. Adverts fed it. Shelves fed it. Wrappers fed it. Adults who loved you and were themselves trapped fed it innocently at birthdays and rewards, because they believed the same lie.

You inherited the story. Then the engineered dose proved the story true for five minutes, by relieving the low it had created. And every time that loop ran, your brain learned the wrong lesson a little more deeply: dose equals relief.

No wonder you believed it. We all did.

### AN HONEST WORD ABOUT THE SCIENCE

I want to be straight with you, as I have been in every chapter.

There are careful scientists who say: be cautious with the addiction word here. Human evidence is thin, says that camp. Rats under intermittent sugar access binge and show these dopamine and withdrawal patterns cleanly; people live more messy lives. Whether sugar addiction is a proven diagnosis in humans remains an open question, not a consensus. To call every sweet-eater addicted would be to overclaim.

The other camp says the behaviors are real and occur under the same intermittency — restrict, then binge — and that the wanting circuit is recruited in the same way.

You might expect me to pick a side and shout. I will not, because the honest position is the one that frees you.

The fact is both camps grant the same two crucial points. Binge behaviours do occur when access is intermittent — the yo-yo of rules and rewards drives escalation — and dopamine here is wanting, not liking.

That is enough to dissolve your shame. You do not need a disease label to explain why you could not stop. You need only this: a highly craveable product was engineered to fire your wanting circuit, your brain learned to want it when cued by sights and wrappers, and your culture taught you to misread that want as pleasure.

Engineering of desire is not the same as consensus on addiction — and you do not have to wait for that debate to end to be free. The desirability was engineered whether or not any committee ever calls it addiction. The trap runs on belief, not on a diagnosis.

So I will not call you an addict. I will call you what you are: a person who was recruited.

### NOT WEAK — OUTGUNNED

Do you feel how that reassigns the blame?

You blamed character. Look again at the match-up.

On one side: you, one person, with a body built for real food, hunger as an honest signal, satisfaction as a quiet, steady arrival.

On the other side: the Sugar Trap — armies of scientists weaponizing abundance, supermarkets built as recruitment funnels, checkout queues lined with hits at the height of a child's eyes, advisories paid for by the same industry that taught you moderation was virtue.

You were not weak-willed. You were wilful, persisting for years in something that went against all your instincts. It takes a strong will to keep overriding the body's signal that says enough, and you did it because you were taught to misread that signal as need. The villain is not your will. The villain is the method that stole your interpretation.

The Big Confection stood at your shoulder in that aisle and whispered, "You deserve a little treat. Once a week is fine. You have been good. Just one."

That was not your voice. That was the trap speaking in your voice.

Have you ever noticed how the exact thought that keeps you trapped sounds most like your own reasoning? That is the confidence trick at its finest. It does not need to force your hand. It only needs to supply the sentence you will say to yourself.

You say it, and the hand moves, and then you blame the hand.

Stop blaming the hand. Look at who wrote the sentence.

I do not want you to leave this chapter frightened. I do not want you to white-knuckle past every aisle, holding your breath and trying not to look. I do not want you to change from fear. Fear keeps the trap alive by making BAD SUGAR big and powerful. See it, instead, as small and crude.

Think of the body's natural sophistication with awe for a moment. Without any instruction, without any diet, it knows how to ask for food, how to taste it brightly, how to signal satisfaction, how to release you so you can get on with your life. A real meal does that: it satisfies and releases. No buzz, no crash, no chatter, no negotiation.

Now think of the product: sweet plus fat, crunch plus melt, tuned to do the opposite — to satisfy nothing, to release nothing, to keep the circuit humming so you reach again. Against your body, it is not sophisticated. It is a blunt crowbar. It does not outwit your body; it jams the signal.

Which would you rather trust — the instrument that kept your ancestors alive for thousands of years without counting, or the wrapper that was designed last quarter to make you miss satiety on purpose?

When you see it that way, anger arrives cleanly, without shame. Not at yourself, not at any single shop or person, but at the manufacture itself, at the predatory recruitment. And clean anger is useful. It is the emotion that says: I was not being indulged; I was being used. It is harsh to the trap and warm to you. It does not say you are broken. It says you were targeted, and you are now seeing the targeting.

If BAD SUGAR were a genuine pleasure or treat, the engineers would not need to engineer it. It would not need a bliss-point, a wrapper, an ad, a placement at eye level. An apple does not need a laboratory to be wanted. A glass of water does not need a team to make you thirsty. The fact they have to work so hard to make you want it is proof it gives you nothing you wanted before they made you want it.

The craving you felt in the aisle was not your appetite talking. It was their brief being fulfilled.

Hold that picture the next time a wrapper calls you.

You are not watching your lack of control. You are watching their recruitment attempt, in real time, arriving as a thought in your head. You can watch it and feel relief — because you do not have to answer recruitment you have seen through.

You came into this chapter asking, "If this is a trap, who set it?"

Now you have seen them. White coats. Bright aisles. A crude, clever product tuned to crowd your wanting and bypass your satisfaction.

Do not go forward to frighten yourself with that. Go forward to rejoice that you are no longer a good target.

We have installed the villain where he belongs — outside you. In the next chapter we will take the warmest moment you have ever used to defend BAD SUGAR — the sunlit birthday where laughter and love felt inseparable from cake — and we will replay it without BAD SUGAR and find that not a drop of warmth is missing. Because once you know who built the trap, you can strip the sweet from the celebration and see whose joy it really was.

SUMMARY

* Engineered hits crowd the brain's wanting circuit, and the fat-plus-sugar combination seems to amplify that want; the hit re-arms the next want rather than giving pleasure.
* Whether human sugar addiction is a consensus diagnosis remains contested and human evidence is thin; the fact of engineered craveability and cue-driven wanting stands regardless.
* You were outgunned by a predatory manufacture, not defective in character; anger belongs to the trap and the industry, not to you.
