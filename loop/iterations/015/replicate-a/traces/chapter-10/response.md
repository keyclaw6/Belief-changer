Chapter 10
HOW YOU REALLY EAT

IN THIS CHAPTER
The quiet kitchen at seven, the window light, a bowl and a spoon, the first honest hunger, taste coming back over days

*Your body already knows how to eat; BAD SUGAR only taught you not to trust it.*

### THE SIGNAL YOU STOPPED HEARING

We stopped hearing hunger a long time ago.

Not because our bodies stopped speaking. Because BAD SUGAR taught us to listen to the wrong voice, at the wrong time, for the wrong thing.

Think of the mornings you know. You wake and before you have even stood up you are already doing maths in your head. What will get me through? The drawer. The tin. The sugary something to get me through until lunch. And you eat it, and for five minutes you feel lifted, and by ten o'clock you are restless again. By eleven you have that I-need-to-eat-something-RIGHT-NOW feeling that is not hunger at all — it is edginess wearing hunger's clothes.

That is not hunger. That is the loop.

Real hunger does not ambush you. It does not shout. It arrives.

It arrives as a clean, quiet signal, somewhere behind the breastbone, a little hollow, a little interested. It grows slowly. It asks, not panics. And when you feed it with real food — not a dose, not a fix — it says thank you and leaves.

Have you felt that in the last year? Honestly? Many of us have not, because we have been feeding a different creature on a different clock.

For months, for years, we have lived on hits. A hit is not food. A hit spikes you, then drops you, then tells you the drop proves you need another hit. You called that hunger. You called it energy. You called it just how I am in the mornings.

It was never you. It was the Sugar Trap running its schedule inside you, and we obeyed it because we thought it was guidance.

Here is the liberating fact your body has been waiting to tell you.

The fact is your body makes the glucose it needs from protein and glycerol. You do not need BAD SUGAR imported to keep the lights on. Eliminating BAD SUGAR is not deprivation. This is not your body being denied fuel. This is your body being released from a noisy substitute so it can return to its clean, steady baseline.

Do you hear what that means? It means the thing you feared would leave you empty is the very thing that kept you feeling empty. Without it, you do not run out. You return.

And I want you to notice something kind. Whole fruit, vegetables, food eaten as food — that is GOOD SUGAR. That is not the target, not counted, not restricted. This book has never asked you to count sweetness as virtue. It asks you to stop feeding the confection that was using sweetness against you. GOOD SUGAR stays on the menu because GOOD SUGAR is food. BAD SUGAR was never food. It was a dose wearing food's face.

So let us do something very simple this week. Not a rule. An observation.

Tomorrow morning, do not eat by the clock. Do not eat by habit. Wait until the signal comes. You will know it when it does. It will not be a craving. It will not be tired, wired and craving and then looking for relief. It will be a calm, hollow interest, as if your stomach has politely cleared its throat.

That is hunger. That is your authority.

When it comes — and it will come, because a body that is not being spiked wants to eat — you answer it. And you will see, in real time, that you are not someone who cannot control eating. You are someone whose eating was controlled *for* you, by a trap that mistook every dip it created for proof you needed it.

You are about to remember how you really eat. And you will find you have nothing to lose and everything to gain in listening again, for the rest of your life.

### A SIMPLE MEAL, EATEN SLOWLY

We are so used to eating fast, sweet, and anxiously — standing at the counter, hand to bag, one bite becomes the box — that we have forgotten what a meal feels like when it is allowed to finish itself.

So let us eat one, together, exactly as you will when you are free.

It is midday. You are hungry — that honest, gradual hunger we just named. You sit. Not because a rule tells you to sit, but because you are hungry and a chair is where you eat.

On the table is simple, real food. It might be soup and bread and an apple. It might be oats with berries. It might be rice and beans and greens. It is not engineered to crave. It is made to nourish. There is colour. There is texture. There is a little chew.

You eat slowly. Not as a discipline — as interest.

Do you remember interest in food? Not urgency, not guilt, not bargaining — *I've managed to be good this week so I can have...* — just interest. You lift the spoon. You taste salt, then sweet where sweet belongs — the bright sweetness of fruit, which is GOOD SUGAR doing what sweetness is meant to do: point to real food and make it enjoyable.

Notice what happens after a few minutes.

Something very ordinary happens, and it is wonderful precisely because it is ordinary.

Satiety arrives.

It does not slam the door. It does not need willpower to hear it. It arrives quietly, like a light being turned up until you can see. The hollow eases. The taste is still pleasant, but the urgency thins. You take two more bites and you realise you do not want another. Not because you are being good. Because you are done.

That is the finish. That is the body saying, that will do nicely, thank you.

Compare that to a dose. A dose never finishes. A dose finishes and immediately asks for repetition. One biscuit opens the negotiation. One sweet creates the need for its successor. You called that enjoyment. It was incompleteness by design.

Real food completes.

And here is what I promise you, because I have seen it thousands of times and lived it myself: that quiet completion is not disappointing. It is relief. For the first time in a long time, eating ends without chatter in your head. No shall I, shan't I. No just one more. No box calling from the cupboard. You ate, you are satisfied, you get on with enjoying your life.

Is that less exciting than the hit? At first your Big Confection will tell you so. It will say, but where is the lift? Where is the buzz?

Ask it back, honestly: where was the lift ever, except before the drop that always followed? The meal that satisfies does not lift you above normal. It returns you to normal — clean, light, steady — and lets you stay there. That is not flatness. That is freedom. The buzz you thought was pleasure was only relief from a low the last dose made. This steadiness needs no rescue because it creates no low.

You do not need to learn a new system to eat this way. You do not need to count, weigh, or label. Your body is not a project to manage. It is an authority to hear.

You fell into the Sugar Trap as we all did — by listening to adverts and habits instead of your own signals. You will walk out of it the same way you walked into any trap: by seeing it and then simply using your eyes.

Eat when you are hungry. Eat real food until you are satisfied. Taste as you go.

Do that once, with attention, and you have felt more truth about eating than any rule could teach you.

And if a faint, empty tug appears an hour later — not hunger, but a slight restlessness at the old time — you need not be worried. That is just the after-echo.

### THE TASTE THAT COMES BACK

Your taste will come back. I say that with cheerful certainty because it does.

When you have lived for years on engineered sweetness — bliss-point sweetness, loud, blasting, designed to crowd your tongue — ordinary food can seem quiet at first. An apple tastes like water. Berries taste sharp. Soup tastes plain.

That is not proof real food is boring. That is proof your palate has been shouted at.

Give it days, not weeks. Taste recovers.

By day two, water tastes like water again — clean, a little mineral. By day three, an apple snaps and you actually taste the skin and the juice separately. By day four or five, a strawberry tastes like a strawberry, not like a carrier for sugar. You will laugh at how much you missed that was already there.

I tell you this now so you are not tricked by a passing phase.

For some of us — not all, and never dramatically — the first two to five days after we stop feeding BAD SUGAR bring a faint, empty, slightly edgy, slightly restless feeling in waves. It is strongest at the old times: four o'clock, eight o'clock, the shop doorway, the sofa at night. It peaks in a couple of days and then it thins and passes. It is not illness. It is not hunger. It is the trivial physical echo as the wanting circuit stops being re-armed.

If you feel nothing at all — no tug, no restlessness — that is also normal. Many feel little or nothing, and they worry that means they were not really hooked. You were. The trap was never mainly physical. It was the belief. The physical part was always small and already dying the moment you stopped feeding the Big Confection that fed it.

Do not measure your freedom by whether you feel a tug. Measure it by what you see when you eat.

Do you feel real hunger arriving honestly and then leaving cleanly when you eat? Yes.

Do you notice satiety releasing you, instead of a dose demanding you continue? Yes.

Do you find real food becoming favourite — not as a worthy choice, but as a wanted one? Yes.

That is the whole replacement, and it costs you nothing.

I know the old thought will try one last disguise. It will say: but I love food. If I love food, surely I must love sweets. Are you asking me to love less?

I am telling you the opposite. You love food, and BAD SUGAR was masking it.

Think of a fine meal ruined by someone shouting sugar over every bite. That shouting is not flavour. It is the confection stealing the applause that belonged to the cook, the ingredients, your own tongue. When the shouting stops, you taste again. You taste fat where fat is beautiful, salt where salt lifts, acid where acid brightens, GOOD SUGAR where fruit is sweet as itself. You thought sugar made food more delicious. The fact is it made food less delicious and then claimed credit for being delicious. It's the other way around.

We spent years tired, wired and craving and called that normal, called that energy, called that just busy life. Now, for a day, try steady. Steady is not dull. Steady is marvellous. Steady is being light and clean in the same kitchen at the same time of day and finding you have attention left over for people, for work, for joy that does not need unwrapping.

And steady lasts. It does not ask you to repeat it every hour to stay steady. It simply stays, because you stopped interrupting it.

You do not need to earn this. You do not need to become someone else who likes healthy food. You are already someone who likes real food when your taste is not being blasted. You are not learning a new way to eat. You are returning to the way you ate before the trap taught you otherwise — as a child eats, as any untrapped body eats — hungry, satisfied, free.

Let me speak to you directly now, as the person who has walked out ahead and turned to call back.

You are not going to live flawlessly. If a little hidden sugar slips in with a sauce, your body can cope — but your mind must not use it to re-invite the belief. An accidental blip revives nothing physically; only the thought that BAD SUGAR gave you something revives the trap. Guard the belief, not the mouthful.

You do not need to change anything else in your life to be free of BAD SUGAR. You do not need to rearrange your mornings, avoid your kitchen, or lecture your family. Eat at your table, shop in your shop, live your same days — only now you will live them without the dose that was doing plenty TO you and nothing FOR you.

And you will see, in the quiet of a normal Tuesday, what a gift that is.

The bowl emptied because you were satisfied, not because you were strong. The hunger came because you were alive, not because you were broken.

REAL FOOD IS FAVOURITE — AND STEADY IS SWEET.

SUMMARY
* Hunger is a calm, honest signal that arrives and leaves cleanly; it is not the urgent I-need-something-now feeling that BAD SUGAR created.
* A simple meal of real food, eaten slowly, finishes itself through satiety and releases you without chatter or negotiation.
* Taste recovers over days when it is not blasted by engineered sweetness; real food becomes favourite because it is tasted as itself.
* Any faint physical tug in the early days is brief, peaks in 2–5 days for those who feel it and then passes, which proves the physical hold is small while the freedom is steady.
