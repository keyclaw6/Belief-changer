Chapter 4
THE TREAT THAT NEVER WAS

IN THIS CHAPTER
A plain biscuit on a saucer, a great meal steaming on the table, a sugar hit torn open, a child's plate eaten clean with nothing sweet on it

*That taste you called pleasure was never the sugar — it was the moment, the food, and you.*

### THE THOUGHT THAT KEEPS YOU HOLDING ON

You have done the hard part. You looked at the kitchen table and at the teaspoons lined on the wood. You stopped haggling about how much harm you can live with and you asked the freeing question instead: what is this stuff doing FOR me?

And still a voice says, very reasonably, very warmly:

"Yes, but it tastes amazing. That is real pleasure. You cannot tell me that sweetness is not a genuine pleasure or treat. I like it."

I hear you. Of course you say that. We all did. We said it with complete sincerity while we were living inside the Sugar Trap, because the trap needs you to say it. If BAD SUGAR is a genuine pleasure or treat, then taking it away must be a loss. And if it is a loss, you would be right to hesitate.

So let us not argue about whether sweet things can be pleasant. Let us do what we did on the kitchen table — isolate the variable. Let us see, in your own experience, where that pleasure actually comes from.

You do not need my word for it. You need yours.

### THE PLAIN BISCUIT TEST

Think of the plainest biscuit you have ever eaten. Not the one from the beautiful tin with butter and sugar and salt engineered to melt on the tongue. The plain one. The dry, pale, slightly dusty biscuit from the bottom of the packet that someone left out overnight. The one that tastes mostly of flour.

Now imagine it. You break it, you chew. Is it a genuine pleasure or treat?

No. You know it is not. You would leave it on the saucer.

Now take that same biscuit and coat it thick with sugar. Same flour, same dry crumb underneath, now masked in sweetness. Suddenly it is edible. More than edible — you reach for another.

What just happened?

Did sugar create pleasure? Or did sugar hide the absence of it?

We do this everywhere and call it liking. We take a cheap, mass-made product that you would not choose on its taste, we drench it in sweetness, and then we credit the sweetness with the pleasure. The fact is sweetness can mask a poor taste and make a poor food pass. That is not proof that you love BAD SUGAR. It is proof that BAD SUGAR can make a poor food palatable enough to sell.

Now put the other comparison beside it.

Think of a great meal. Real food, properly cooked. It might be soup that smells of the garden, or meat and vegetables with salt and fat and heat, or bread still warm, or fruit that bursts when you bite it. You eat it slowly. Your senses wake up. You taste salt, sour, bitter, savoury, sweet — all of them — in balance. Your body says yes. You stop when you are satisfied, and the satisfaction stays with you.

Where is the need for a sugar hit there?

There is none. The meal was already a pleasure, complete in itself. The pleasure belonged to the food, to the cooking, to your hunger arriving honestly and being met.

Now take that same fine meal and do what we were taught to do: finish it with a sugar hit. A spoonful of something engineered to be sweeter than anything in nature. What does it add?

Watch closely. The hit is not more pleasure. It is a louder note that drowns the others. It flattens the subtle tastes you just enjoyed and replaces them with one shrill sweetness that crowds everything else. For a few seconds it feels intense — then it is gone, and you want another, not because the meal was not enough, but because the hit taught your brain to wait for the louder note.

And then ask yourself the simplest test of all:

If you loved BAD SUGAR for its own pleasure, why do you never sit and eat it by the spoonful from the bag? Why must it always ride on something — a biscuit, a chocolate bar, a drink, a dessert — to be enjoyed? The sugar is not the pleasure. It is sneaking a ride on the food, the occasion, and you.

It is the other way around. You are the one who brings the pleasure. The moment, the hunger, the company, the taste of real food — that is where the pleasure lives. BAD SUGAR arrived afterwards and took the credit.

### THE CHILD'S PLATE

If you doubt that, watch a child eat when no one has taught them what a treat is supposed to be.

Give a child who has not yet been told that sweet means love a plate of good food when they are properly hungry. They eat with their whole face. They make noises. They go quiet with concentration. A strawberry is an event. Bread with butter is wonderful. They do not miss BAD SUGAR because they have not learned to call that missing a lack. Their pleasure is immediate, sensory, complete.

We were that child. Then the lesson arrived. Adults, advertising, the packet on the shelf — all smiling, all saying: this sweet thing means you are loved, you have been good, you deserve something special. We learned to glue a feeling to a wrapper. And once the glue set, every good moment seemed to need the wrapper to be complete.

But look again at that child's plate. The enjoyment is there, vivid and whole, without a single dose of BAD SUGAR. How can that be if sweetness is the source of pleasure? It cannot.

The fact is refined carbohydrates and fats can evoke the brain's wanting circuit in a way that looks akin to how addictive substances do, and the fat-plus-sugar combo seems to have a supra-additive effect — the two together seem to crowd the circuit more than either alone. That is not proof that you are compelled. It is why the product was engineered to be craveable. The pleasure you remember from that biscuit, that chocolate, that sweet drink, is not the simple sweetness you think. It is the engineered hit, designed to feel like wanting more, followed by the faint dip that makes you think you needed it.

A person said it plainly, in our own words: "I would have something quick and sweet but now know that will only give a short term lift which will inevitably be followed by a fast drop." That is not a medical diagnosis. That is the lived shape of the trick — the brief lift that guarantees the drop, remembered as pleasure, revealed as the first half of a yo-yo.

You never loved the yo-yo. You loved the moments it interrupted.

### WHERE THE PLEASURE REALLY LIVED

Let me take one more scene and strip the sugar clean out, so you feel it.

You come home tired. The kitchen is warm. Someone you love is there. Food is on the table that you are hungry for. You sit. You taste. You talk. You laugh at something small.

In the old story, that moment needed a sugar hit to be a treat. Without it, you would be missing something. With it, you were rewarding yourself.

Now play the same film with one change only: nothing sweet from a packet, nothing refined, nothing engineered. Just the hunger, the food, the warmth, the person.

What is missing?

Nothing.

The laughter is still there. The relief of sitting down is still there. The taste is still there — more there, because no single shriek of sweetness is flattening the rest. The love is still there. The treat was never in the wrapper. It was in the room. BAD SUGAR was only ever sneaking a ride on a moment that was already sweet.

We have been conned into crediting the confection with the pleasure that belonged to our own lives. The Sugar Trap does that by design. It puts its name on your good moments, like a thief signing his name on your photographs and telling you the photograph was his.

And here is why that matters beyond taste. The treat that never was leaves you not satisfied but wanting. That is its signature. Real food satisfies and then releases you — hunger gone, taste content, body steady and light. The dose satisfies for seconds and then hands you back the very feeling it claimed to fix.

Ask your own evenings. The biscuits meant as a treat. The sweets meant as a little pleasure. How did you feel after? Not clean, not light, not steady. You felt tired, wired and craving. That triple is not an accident. It is the steady state of the trapped eater. Tired because the lift was not energy. Wired because the hit crowded the circuit and left it buzzing. Craving because the dip after the hit re-armed the want. That is not pleasure. That is a mild, borrowed buzz followed by a long, quiet cost, mislabelled as enjoyment because the wanting felt so strong.

Do you see the switch?

We thought we ate BAD SUGAR because it gave us pleasure.

The fact is BAD SUGAR made us feel we needed pleasure precisely because it took it away between doses, and then sold it back loudly and briefly as the rescue.

I know this is hard to accept. For years you felt that sweetness was one of life's genuine pleasures, and I am saying it never was. Sit with your own test, not my argument. The plain biscuit you would not eat without sugar. The great meal you enjoyed more before the sugar arrived. The child's plate that needs no sweet to be joyful. Your own evidence says the same thing from three different angles: the pleasure belongs to real food and real moments and to you. The sugar adds nothing that you did not already have.

And if it adds nothing — then what are you being asked to lose?

### NOT A LOSS, AN EXPOSURE

There is a last knot to untie.

You might say, "Even if some of it is borrowed, I still enjoy the taste. Are you saying sweet taste is wrong?"

No. Sweet taste, in fruit, in real food eaten as food, is lovely. Your body knows sweet. Children know sweet. The tongue knows sweet. That is not the target. When this book says sugar, take it to mean BAD SUGAR — the added, refined sugar and the foods where sugar has been engineered in, and the ultra-processed refined carbs that behave the same way. Sweets, biscuits, pastries, desserts, sugary drinks, confectionery, and the white and brown refined packets that spike the same loop. Whole fruit, vegetables, and unrefined foods eaten as food are not counted, not restricted, not to be feared. I am not prescribing a diet. I am drawing the line around the confection that was built to bypass your satisfaction.

Do you feel how light that line is?

You are not being asked to lose taste. You are being asked to see where taste was already yours, and where an engineered dose was masking taste, crowding the wanting, and leaving you tired, wired and craving for a pleasure that never paid what it promised.

Let me land it as one short verdict, because this is the belief that changes in this chapter and you will carry it:

There was no treat. There was only the moment, and you in it.

SUMMARY
* The pleasure you credited to BAD SUGAR belonged to real food, real moments, and you; sweetness was masking poor taste and sneaking a ride on a satisfaction already there.
* The plain biscuit test and the great meal show the variable isolated: poor food becomes palatable with sugar, good food needs no hit to be complete.
* A child's un-sugared enjoyment proves pleasure is whole without BAD SUGAR; sweetness is not the source.
* Engineered products are designed to be craveable — refined carbs and fats can evoke striatal wanting, and a fat-plus-sugar combo seems to crowd it more — which explains the sharp wanting, not genuine pleasure.
* The after-state of a sugar treat is not relief but a steady cost you can name: tired, wired and craving, while the treat itself never gave anything real to lose.
