Chapter 6
THE REWARD YOU PAY YOURSELF

IN THIS CHAPTER
The hard-day kitchen, the late-night sofa, the quiet desk after stress, the empty wrapper in the hand

*You were not loving yourself — you were paying the trap with your own goodness.*

### I DESERVE THIS

You know the exact feeling.

The day has taken a lot out of you. It was not dramatic. It was just long, and full, and a little grinding. You have held things together for other people, answered what needed answering, kept the show on the road. You come into the kitchen in that thin, flat moment between finishing one thing and starting the next. The house is quiet. Or the office is quiet. The light is low.

And a thought arrives, soft and entirely reasonable, almost tender:

"I deserve it. It's my treat, my comfort, my de-stress. I've managed to be good this week. Just a little something to be kind to myself."

Of course you think that. We all did.

If you have ever said to yourself, in our own dialect, "celebrating, rewarding myself, going to the cinema, etc with chocolate," you are not odd. You are not weak. You are speaking the exact language the Sugar Trap taught us to speak. The trap needs you to speak it. Because if that sweet is reward, then reaching for it is self-care. If it is comfort, then taking it away would be unkind. You would be denying yourself love.

I want to validate something before we go further. The feeling underneath that thought is real and good.

The need for kindness is real. The need for rest is real. The need to mark that you have done well, that you have endured, that you are worthy of care — that is real and human and right. You were not wrong to want comfort. You were not wrong to want a reward. You were not wrong to want to be good to yourself after a hard day.

You were wrong — as we all were — about what was actually giving it to you.

Let me ask you plainly, as one person to another: when you had that private sweet after the hard day, what part felt like love?

Was it the sugar? Or was it the pause? The exhale? The five minutes where nobody needed anything from you? The warmth of saying to yourself, *you did well, let me be kind to you*?

We gave the credit to the dose. But the dose did something else entirely.

### WHAT YOU ACTUALLY BOUGHT

Stay with me in that kitchen and watch what really happens, without the story on top.

You have been good. You have kept the rules you set: no sweets before dinner, no biscuits in the house, just a treat when you feel you deserve one, once a week is fine. That is the restrict part. It feels virtuous. It feels controlled.

Then the hard day arrives, and the rule bends, because it was always meant to bend. "I've managed to be good this week" becomes the exact permission slip the trap was waiting for. The hand reaches. The mouth wants something sugary. You tell yourself it is earned.

You eat it. For two or three minutes there is a little lift. You feel it — I will never tell you that you imagined it. Then what?

Ten minutes later, do you feel loved? Comforted? Restored?

Or do you feel a little more tired, wired and craving — tired because the lift was brief and already fading, wired because the hit has pinged your wanting circuit and left it buzzing, craving because the dip that follows is already queuing the next thought: *one more won't hurt*?

One sweet becomes a short run. One biscuit becomes the packet. One dose becomes one bite becomes the box. Not because you lack character. Because the schedule itself drives the bingeing.

We thought the bingeing meant we were indulgent people. The fact is the intermittency is what drives the escalation. When the treat is allowed only sometimes, as a reward for virtue, the brain learns to binge when it arrives. When the same substance is simply available without the rule and the reward story, that frantic escalation does not happen. The restrict–reward yo-yo is the trap's form. Your virtue became the spring.

Do you see who was paid?

You paid. With your own goodness. You took a genuinely good thing — your effort, your endurance, your desire to be kind to yourself — and you handed it to BAD SUGAR as its fee. The confection did not reward you. You rewarded it. You paid it with the very virtue you had earned.

And what did it give back?

The deal it offered was: I will comfort you. I will de-stress you. What it delivered was a few minutes of relief from a low it had helped create, followed by a lower mood and a stronger want. That is not comfort. That is a loan.

Across large groups of people followed over time, the pattern is clear and sobering: those who more often reached for sweets as a treat were more likely to feel low later — and checking whether low mood was driving the sweet-eating did not explain it away. That is not a sentence on you, just what showed up across many people, and in that long follow-up the clearest signal was in men, and like all population patterns it cannot prove cause in any one person — but the direction is the opposite of what the reward promise claims. The daily treat did not relieve tomorrow. It predicted it.

And you have already felt that direction in your own life, without a study to tell you. Think of the evening you told yourself the sweet was comfort after stress. Did you wake the next morning steadier, calmer, more rested? Or did you wake a little flat, a little foggy, already thinking about sugar before breakfast, already a little less kind to yourself?

We told ourselves BAD SUGAR was a genuine pleasure or treat that made a hard day softer. The evidence of your own evenings says it made the next hours harder and the next morning flatter, while calling that cost comfort.

It is doing plenty TO you. It is doing nothing FOR you.

### THE SAME NIGHT, WITHOUT THE DOSE

I want to replay that same private moment with you — the hard-day kitchen, the late-night sofa, the quiet desk — and strip out the one thing that never belonged, while leaving every drop of the kindness intact.

Nothing else changes. Not your day. Not your worth. Not the need for care.

It is the same evening. You have done enough. You are tired in that honest, earned way. You come to the kitchen and you pause. Instead of paying the confection with your virtue, you give the virtue directly to yourself, where it was headed all along.

You make something simple and real. Soup that steams. Toast that crackles. Fruit that snaps and tastes bright because your taste is not being shouted over. Or you make tea and sit down properly. Or you do what your body has been asking for: you sit on the sofa, feet up, phone down, and let the day finish. Five minutes of nothing to prove. A shower that is a little longer than necessary. A window opened to cold air. A page of a book that has nothing to do with food.

Notice what happens to the word reward when you watch it closely.

The relief you felt after the sweet was not the sweet. It was the pause, the permission, the exhale, the decision to be kind. The sweet was only sneaking a ride on that kindness. It added noise, then charged you for the quiet.

When you keep the pause and leave the dose, the kindness remains. Completely. Your body gets what it actually wanted: rest, warmth, steadiness, a moment that is yours. No yo-yo. No bargain. No quiet arithmetic of *have I been good enough to be allowed this*.

Let me ask you a question that tests this.

Think of two versions of that late-night sofa. In one, you eat the sweets you told yourself you deserve. In the other, you make the same pause — same sofa, same tiredness, same need to be held — and you rest, or you eat something real that satisfies and releases you. Which of those two leaves you actually comforted? Which leaves you more at peace when you get into bed? Which leaves you waking tomorrow more on your side?

You know.

And think of the quiet desk after stress. You tell yourself you need a sugary something to get me through. You have that I-need-to-eat-something-RIGHT-NOW feeling, that slightly edgy, pushy tug that will not wait. You feed it with BAD SUGAR and for a moment the signal goes quiet. Then it comes back louder.

Now imagine you answer that same signal differently, not by white-knuckling, not by being strong, but by seeing it for what it is: a faint complaint from a loop that is hungry to be fed, not a need for fuel or love. You drink water. You eat real food if you are honestly hungry — food that needs chewing, that tastes of itself, not of the lab's bliss-point. You give yourself five minutes to breathe. What happens to the urgent tug? It passes. Not because you fought it. Because you stopped paying it.

This is important: I am not telling you sweetness itself is forbidden or that you must live without pleasure. GOOD SUGAR — whole fruit, vegetables, unrefined foods eaten as food — is not the trap and is not counted. Your body knows the difference between food that satisfies and a confection that spikes. The issue has never been sweetness. The issue has been the story that BAD SUGAR equals love.

Real self-care does not leave you tired, wired and craving. A real reward does not make you need another reward an hour later. A real comfort does not leave you less comforted than before.

If BAD SUGAR were comfort, it would comfort. If it were love, it would leave you feeling more loved. It leaves you wanting — and wanting is not proof you are being cared for. It is proof something was taken.

### IT WAS NEVER COMFORT. IT WAS THE INTEREST.

Let me land the inversion, because once you see it you cannot unsee it.

We told ourselves: I feel bad, so the sweet helps me feel better.

It is the other way around.

The sweet helped you feel worse, then offered to relieve the first part of the worse, and you called that relief kindness.

We fell for it as we all did: we were taught to call a dose love. We were taught that a hard day earns a hit, and a hit is care. We accepted that phoney information on trust, because everyone seemed to believe it, because the ad showed it, because the drawer was there and the wrapper promised ease. We never stopped to ask who benefits from that belief.

Who benefits when you translate I've had a hard day, I deserve kindness into I've had a hard day, I therefore deserve BAD SUGAR? The confection benefits. The labour, the virtue, the goodness — yours. The payment — yours. The tiredness afterwards — yours.

You did not fail at comforting yourself. You were tricked into paying the wrong recipient.

Do you remember the yo-yo from the afternoon? It is the same yo-yo here, just dressed as love. Restrict, deserve, dose, dip, want again. The restrict makes the dose precious. The preciousness makes the dose feel like love. The love story guarantees the dip. The dip guarantees the next dose. The loop keeps itself alive on the very virtue you bring to it.

And the most dignified fact of all is this: repeat the loop and you do not feel more rewarded. You feel emptier. If BAD SUGAR were a genuine pleasure or treat, more of it would mean more pleasure. But more of it never means more pleased. It means more tired, wired and craving. The wanting grows, the liking does not. That is the fingerprint of the trap. It runs on wanting, not on pleasure, and wanting feels like need only while the Big Confection tells you wanting means you are getting something.

You are not getting something. You are paying for the feeling of having paid.

I am not asking you to be harder on yourself. I am asking you to be more precisely kind.

Next time the deserve-it thought arrives — and it will arrive, because old scripts arrive — hear it with love, then answer it with love that actually lands.

"I deserve kindness. Of course I do. So I will give myself real kindness, not a dose that leaves me more in need of kindness."

You do not need to argue with the thought. You do not need to push it away. Thoughts are not commands. You only need to re-label what it is offering. That offer of rescue is the perpetrator dressed as the rescuer.

You have already lived the proof. The nights you gave yourself real rest, real food, real pause — you felt more held than the nights you gave yourself sweets and then needed more sweets to feel held. Your body told you. Your mornings told you.

So let me leave you with the one short verdict this chapter exists to install, spoken in ordinary words you can carry:

Your goodness was the reward. The sweet was the bill.

You do not need to pay yourself with something that takes from you. You were already the thing worth rewarding. Keep the goodness. Keep the pause. Keep the kindness. Let the confection keep nothing.

SUMMARY
* The private deserve-it sweet felt like self-love, but the love was yours — the pause, the permission, the exhale — and BAD SUGAR only sneaked a ride on it.
* Treating BAD SUGAR as a reward for virtue keeps the restrict-binge loop alive; the intermittency drives the binge, not your character.
* Across many people, more frequent sweets predicted later low mood rather than relieving it, which is the opposite of what the reward promise claims.
* A real reward leaves you satisfied and steady; BAD SUGAR leaves you tired, wired and craving and wanting another dose, which proves it took rather than gave.
