Chapter 11
CLOSING EVERY ESCAPE ROUTE

**IN THIS CHAPTER** — the negotiator's table, the weekend box that promises to stay shut, the birthday slice on its plate, the cliff edge with its careful measuring tape, the switchboard with one cord still lit

*There is no little bit of the trap that leaves you free — leave one exception and the whole trap stays alive.*

### THE DEAL YOU DIDN'T KNOW YOU WERE MAKING

We got free of the big lies. BAD SUGAR is not a genuine pleasure or treat. It is not fuel. It is not love. You have felt that.

And right on cue, a quieter voice arrives. Not a craving. A negotiation.

You know the voice. It is polite, reasonable, almost wise.

"I won't binge any more. I'll just have a little at weekends."

"I'll keep the birthday cake. That's only special occasions."

"I'll start properly on Monday — one last blow-out and then I'm done."

"I'll cut down slowly. Wean off."

"I'll switch to something lighter — honey, dark chocolate, the 'healthy' bar."

I want you to hear that voice for what it is. It is not you thinking. It is the Sugar Trap negotiating your next dose for you. We have all sat at that table. We thought we were being moderate, mature, in control. The fact is, moderation is how the trap keeps you.

Every one of those deals has the same job: to keep the Big Confection alive while the body waits for permission to dose. You do not need to be stubborn to see it. You only need to see that a trap with a little door left open is not an escape. It is still a trap, and you are still inside it.

You are not weak for wanting a loophole. We were all taught that willpower fails, so the clever thing must be to bargain. The bargain is the trap's cleverest trick. The moment you agree that BAD SUGAR gave you something worth keeping a little of, you have re-fed the belief that feeds the Nibbler, and the whole circuit hums back up. That is why cutting down never feels easier than stopping. It feels harder.

### THE CLIFF YOU CANNOT JUMP OFF A LITTLE

Let me take you to the cliff.

A high rock stands over the sea. The wind is up. Someone tells you, "You may jump off this cliff, as long as you promise not to fall more than three metres. Just a little fall. A controlled fall. A weekend fall."

You would laugh. You know a fall is not controllable after you step off. Gravity does not negotiate. You either jump or you do not.

Telling a person caught in the Sugar Trap to "just have a little" is the same. You cannot eat a little of the thing that teaches you to want more, and then expect not to want more. The little is not a smaller trap. It is the trap, served in a smaller portion, with the same hook set.

We tried it, you and I. We set the rule on Sunday night. One square. One biscuit. Only on birthdays. Only when we have been good. And what happened? The square became precious. The biscuit became the day's event. The birthday became the week before the birthday. We thought about it more, not less. We waited for it. We counted down to it. An intermittent sweet does not quiet the wanting. It rehearses it. It re-arms it.

The schedule is the trap's form. The on-off, good-now-then-indulgent, strict-then-binge pattern is what drives the escalation. Leave it ad lib and you graze; put it on a restrict-and-reward yo-yo and you binge. That is the design. Engineered doses of fat and sugar together seem to crowd the wanting circuit even more than either alone — the cookie, the frosted pastry, the bar that is both — the combo product is the sharpest hook by design, not by accident. You are not proving control when you plan that little exception. You are plugging the trap back into the mains.

Is there an amount of falling that is not falling? No. There is standing back from the edge, free. And there is stepping off, falling.

### THE MYTHS — QUOTED, ANSWERED

Here is the battery you will hear in your own head, often in your own exact words. I will play each line and answer it in a few sentences, so that when it comes it arrives pre-labelled as trap script and you can smile.

**"I'll just cut down. A little is fine."**

If a little were fine, why are you reading this? You have cut down before. How did it feel — free, or more preoccupied? Cutting down makes the dose precious and the relief between doses feel bigger, so each hit re-triggers the want harder. You do not need willpower to eat less of a confection. You need to see there is no good in it at all, and then there is nothing to portion.

**"I'll keep the special ones. Birthday cake, Christmas, holidays."**

A special exception keeps the Big Confection alive for the rest of the year. You will spend every birthday waiting for the cake to do something it never did, and every ordinary day feeling deprived because you are waiting. One cord still plugged in keeps the whole machine running — like that switchboard with 100 cords: pull ninety-nine and leave one lit, and it still runs. Leave one special BAD SUGAR and you have not left the trap. You have decorated it.

**"I'll quit tomorrow. One last blow-out first."**

If tomorrow were a better day to be free, why pay for one more run around the loop? The last blow-out does not close the account. It re-confirms the story that BAD SUGAR is a parting friend worth a farewell. You do not need a farewell binge to prove you meant it. You need to see that the blow-out is not a treat — it is the trap feeding you while you tell yourself you are about to leave it.

**"I'll wean off slowly so it isn't a shock."**

You do not wean off a belief. You see through it and you are free. Tapering only spaces hits and fills the gaps with fantasy about the next hit, so the wanting grows louder between them. The physical tug itself is mild and well-defined — real, but small and already dying when you stop feeding the belief — and any faint flicker tends to hump for two to five days and then pass, if you feel it at all. Stretching that flicker out for weeks by weaning does not soften it. It rehearses it.

**"I'll switch to a lighter sweet — honey, juice, dark chocolate, the 'natural' snack."**

A dose is a dose when it is BAD SUGAR in lighter clothing. The name does not change the circuit it crowds. The body does not read the label; the brain reads the engineered sweetness and the cue. If the swap still serves as a hit after stress or as the reward you pay yourself, the belief that you need a sweet hit is intact and the Nibbler will politely ask for its usual brand again. Choose food, not a lighter fix.

**"It's harmless in moderation — everyone has a little."**

Everyone felt fine in the warm water while it heated slowly. That is why you felt normal while the costs accumulated. The fact is, BAD SUGAR is doing plenty TO you even when it feels like it is doing something FOR you — to vessels, to teeth, to the appetite signal that never quite settles — and you never beat the normal of a person who never started. Moderation is not harmless. It is the story that kept the harm comfortable.

**"I don't keep it in the house — that's my system."**

Keeping it out of the house but keeping it in the mind is still keeping it. The wrapper at the shop, the smell at 3pm, the friend offering the tin — cues can fire the want before the bite, like a bell that rings because it was trained to ring. That bell is not a character flaw. It is understandable circuitry that learned to call. You do not win by hiding packets. You win by re-labelling the bell joyfully when it rings and watching it fade because you do not answer it with belief.

Do you see the pattern? Every deal keeps the idea that BAD SUGAR gives you something. Every deal keeps you tired, wired and craving — not because you failed the deal, but because the deal was designed to keep you there. The creep is not a risk. It is the guarantee. A little becomes a little more because the little taught you to want it.

### ONE CORD KEEPS THE WHOLE TRAP ALIVE

Picture the switchboard again. A dumb appliance with a hundred power cords hanging. You kneel and pull ninety-nine. Clean work. Careful. Proud.

You leave one. Birthdays. Weekends. The one piece of dark chocolate after a hard day that you tell yourself does not count.

The light stays on. The hum stays on. The whole machine runs on that one.

That is totality. It is not punishment. It is how belief works. The trap does not run on sugar alone. It runs on the thought that sugar is a genuine pleasure or treat worth keeping one of. Break that thought completely and the Nibbler has nothing to live on. Keep that thought in one corner and the Big Confection will feed the Nibbler from that corner forever.

I was the person who kept the cord. One biscuit with tea, I told myself, is civilised. I measured it. I kept the packet on the top shelf. I lasted three days of measuring before the measuring became a new way of thinking about biscuits all day. The biscuit had become a ceremony, a reward, a test of whether I was good. I was not eating biscuits. I was feeding a belief. When I pulled that last cord — when I saw that the biscuit added nothing and I wanted no biscuit at all — the ceremony collapsed and the day got quiet. Not harder. Quieter.

You may ask, "What about an accidental blip — sugar hidden in a sauce, a set menu I didn't choose?"

Your body can cope with an occasional accidental blip, but your mind will not if you let the belief back in and turn the blip into permission. A blip revives nothing physically by itself; it only becomes a relapse if you re-invite the Big Confection and decide BAD SUGAR gave you something after all. The margin is for accidents, not for erratic driving. Guard the belief, not the behaviour, and an accident stays an accident.

And the faint tug you may feel early? Name it correctly. That faint, empty, slightly edgy, slightly restless feeling is not a need. It is the Nibbler dying. It is mild, it is brief, and it asks for nothing except not to be fed with a story. You do not fight it. You notice it, you smile that it is going quiet, and you get on with your morning. In two to five days it has usually passed, and often you feel nothing at all except steadiness returning. If you have medical guidance about sugar, speak to your clinician — this book changes a belief about BAD SUGAR, it does not override medical advice — but for the everyday trap, the hump is not a withdrawal to be endured with grit. It is a little echo of a circuit that no longer gets its cue.

So close every escape route now, while you are clear. Not with a vow you will force yourself to keep. With sight.

There is no little trap that leaves you free. There is no special sweet that does not keep the story alive. The cliff does not allow a small fall. The switchboard does not go dark until the last cord is pulled. Every moderation deal says, "BAD SUGAR is still a treat worth scheduling." The truth is the other way around: it never was, and the schedule is what made it feel like one.

You do not need a loophole. You need the opposite — the relief of having no loophole to guard. When there is no exception to protect, no amount to measure, no tomorrow to wait for, the tug-of-war ends because both ropes were held by the same trap and you have put them down. You change nothing else. You walk back into the same kitchen, the same shop, the same birthday room, and you find the same warmth there without the packet. The packet added nothing. The moment did it all.

Leave the edge. Pull the cord. Let the light go out.

**SUMMARY**

- Every moderation deal keeps the belief that BAD SUGAR gives you something, and that belief keeps the trap alive and guarantees creep.
- Cutting down intensifies wanting by making each dose feel like a bigger relief; an intermittent pattern drives escalation, it does not teach control.
- Keeping one special exception is keeping the whole trap — one cord still lit keeps the machine running.
- Waiting until tomorrow, weaning slowly, or swapping to a lighter sweet are not softer exits; they rehearse the want and keep the story intact.
- An accidental blip is not a relapse unless you re-invite the belief; the physical flicker, if you feel it at all, is mild and passes in days, and steadiness returns when the last exception is gone.
