Chapter 13
THAT'S JUST HOW I AM

**IN THIS CHAPTER** — the family table at eight years old, the lunchbox and the tin on the shelf, the school photo where no one was heavy, the supermarket aisle then and now, the crowded waiting room

*You were not born a sugar person — the trap made you feel like one, and that feeling is not who you are.*

### THE STORY THAT SOUNDS LIKE YOU

"That's just how I am."

"I've always had a sweet tooth."

"I have an addictive personality — if it's there, I can't leave it alone."

"I come from a family like that. My mother was the same, my father was the same."

Do you hear how gentle and final that sounds? It does not shout. It settles over you like an explanation that asks for no more examination. It says: the reason you reach, the reason one bite becomes the box, the reason you keep negotiating with yourself and losing, is not the con, not the product, not the schedule you were put on. It is you. Your wiring. Your character. Your genes.

I want to be warm to you here, because that story hurts, and it has been sold to you as kindness. It lets you off the hook for a moment — "it's not my fault, it's my nature" — and then, in the same breath, it puts you back on a hook you can never get off. If it is your nature, what can you do? If you are wired to love BAD SUGAR, then freedom is for other people. The people with discipline. The people with different parents.

We fell for that, you and I. Of course we did. When every attempt to portion, time, or be good with BAD SUGAR collapsed into the same familiar bag or box, the most available conclusion was that we were the problem. The trap stayed invisible and we took its weight onto ourselves.

Tell me: at what stage did you decide you had a sweet tooth? Do you remember sitting down as a small child and reasoning, "I think I will be the kind of person who needs a sweet to get through the afternoon"? You did not decide it. You found it. You were given a world in which BAD SUGAR was presented as a genuine pleasure or treat, as love, as reward, as relief, and you learned to answer the little tug it left behind by feeding it again. That is not personality. That is training.

I say this as an escaped expert who has watched thousands of people tell me the same line on day one and laugh at it on day ten: the sweet tooth is not a tooth at all. It is a story the trap told about you until you told it about yourself.

And I need you to see why that story cannot be true — and why, even if it were true, it would not matter.

### THE FAMILY TABLE — AND WHAT IT REALLY TAUGHT

Come with me to the family table.

You are eight. Supper is done. There is chatter, plates being cleared. And then the moment that was always framed as love: "If you're good, you can have something sweet." "Leave room for pudding." "Go on, just one — you've earned it." The tin on the shelf was not just a tin. It was a signal for who was good, who was celebrated, who was comforted.

We all lived some version of that table. In mine, the sweet was the proof the day had gone well. In yours, perhaps it was the apology for a hard day, or the quiet on the sofa after everyone else was in bed. Watch what that teaches without ever saying it: food that nourishes is the duty, BAD SUGAR is the prize. The body is the bore. The confection is the love.

Years later you sit at your own table and hear yourself say, "I deserve this." You believe you are describing a stable preference: you like sweet things more than other people do. Look again. You are describing a reflex that was installed. We were conditioned, dose by dose, to read a normal human want — for comfort, for rest, for a moment to yourself — as a want for BAD SUGAR. The Big Confection did that.

That is why the family story feels so convincing. "My mother was like that, so I am like that." Of course she was. She sat at a table like yours twenty or thirty years earlier, under the same bright wrappers and the same brainwashing that said sugar is love and love is sweet. Families share tables, share shops, share habits, share the same aisle with the same engineered craveability at the end of it. They share a trap. They do not share a gene for it.

If you doubt that, look at the photograph.

### THE PHOTOGRAPH THAT UNDOES THE GENE STORY

Pull out the school photo from when your parents were children. Then pull out the class photo from last year at the same school.

In the first photo, almost every child is lean, faces clear, no tin in the lunchbox except perhaps an apple, a sandwich, water from the tap. BAD SUGAR existed, but it was occasional, expensive, small. In the second photo, many more children carry the extra weight that comes with constant hits, the tiredness, the box in the bag.

Have our genes changed in forty years?

They have not. Genes do not rewrite themselves in one generation. What changed was the supply. The number of doses. The size of the dose. The engineering. The bliss-point lab that learned to tune sugar with fat and salt and crunch and melt so the product bypasses the body's natural stop signal and calls you again an hour later. The aisle that went from one small shelf to an entire wall that lights up and says, "You need this to feel normal."

That is the population-scale proof working in front of your eyes. If the cause were a stable trait inside some of us — an addictive personality, a sweet tooth coded in blood — the numbers would stay stable. They do not stay stable. They track the trap. When the trap gets bigger, more of us look "addicted." When the trap is small, far fewer do. It's the other way around.

And here is where I must be precise with you, because honesty outranks force. Scientists genuinely disagree about what to call this. Leading researchers argue that the binge pattern matters more than the substance itself — that under an intermittent schedule of restrict and then binge, anyone will start to binge, and that human evidence that sugar alone creates a clinical addiction remains thin. It is an open question, not a settled consensus. I do not need a label to free you, and I will not give you one.

Both sides grant what you need: under the on-off, good-week-then-blow-out pattern, binge behaviours occur. Cues — wrappers, smells, the 3pm drawer — can fire the wanting before the bite. That wanting is circuitry, not character.

So which story fits the facts better? That in forty years a gene for loving BAD SUGAR spread like lightning through whole populations of adults and children at once? Or that an engineered trap got better at catching us, and we — warm, normal people — got caught?

You know the answer because you have already lived it. You never beat the normal baseline of someone who never got hooked. Each hit only quieted the low the last hit created, then guaranteed the next low. The traits you call your personality — the cravings, the loss of control, the tired, wired and craving afternoons, the I-need-to-eat-something-RIGHT-NOW feeling — are not the cause of the trap. They are the result of living inside it.

You do not have those traits because you are that person. You became that person because you fed the trap.

That tug you feel between meals is not proof of who you are. That tug is the Nibbler dying.

That story that says "this is just me" is the Big Confection talking.

You know that a faint, empty, slightly edgy, slightly restless feeling is not a need for fuel — it is the little creature complaining as its food stops arriving.

### EVEN IF IT WERE TRUE — YOU WOULD STILL BE FREE

Now I will give you the graceful concession, because I do not want your freedom to depend on winning an argument about personality.

Let us suppose, for the sake of peace, that you do have something extra — a livelier sweet tooth than your friend, a more reactive temperament, a family history that truly does make you more drawn to hits. Suppose it is true. All the way true.

You are still free, and you are free in exactly the same way.

Because this method does not ask you to become a different person. It asks you to see one thing: BAD SUGAR does nothing FOR you. It is doing plenty TO you.

It is doing plenty TO you. It is doing nothing FOR you.

Even a person who felt a stronger pull would still be pulling toward nothing. There is no benefit to protect, no genuine pleasure or treat to lose, no energy being given, no love being given. The pull is toward relief from a discomfort the pull itself maintains. A sweeter tooth would simply be a tooth better trained to misread a tiny withdrawal as a call for the very thing that keeps it alive.

Think of tight shoes — not as a slogan, as a lived image. If you wore shoes a size too small all day for relief when you kicked them off, would a more sensitive foot make the shoes give you more? No — a more sensitive foot would suffer more, and be freer faster once you took the shoes off for good. The sensitivity is not an argument to keep the shoes. It is an argument to rejoice when you stop.

So you have nothing to prove about your nature and nothing to test about your genes. You do not need to be a different kind of person to be free of BAD SUGAR. You need one belief to change: the belief that the urge proves a benefit.

This is common, not a defect. About one adult in seven and one child in eight report this same loss-of-control pattern with food — as common as the patterns seen with drinking and smoking. I tell you that not to label you, but to remove the label you put on yourself. You are not uniquely weak. You are not strangely wired. You are in the middle of a very large, very normal crowd of people who were taught to answer honest human wants with a dose. That commonness does not make the trap less serious. It makes your self-blame less necessary.

And it hands you the freedom at once. If the behaviour is common because the trap is common, then the ease is common too. Thousands of people who told me on day one, "You don't understand — I'm just wired like this," are today eating food that satisfies, noticing hunger arrive honestly and leave cleanly, tasting more, not less, and feeling light and steady where they felt tired, wired and craving. Not because they became someone else. Because they stopped believing the trap's story about who they were.

You do not need to wait to be re-wired. You already are who you need to be to be free.

### YOU ARE NOT THE TRAP'S BIOGRAPHY

So let us close this excuse with the kindness it deserves.

You are not your history with BAD SUGAR. Your history with BAD SUGAR is a biography the trap wrote while you lived in it — and you were the hero who kept going, wilful, persisting against your own instincts, trying again. Now you are stepping out and the biography ends.

You will still love what you love. You will still be the person who laughs loudly, or is quiet at parties, or is fierce at work, or soft at home. Nothing in your character was made of sugar. Sugar was only ever sneaking a ride on your character and claiming credit for the warmth you brought to the moment yourself.

Ask, one last time, and answer plainly: when you had that birthday without BAD SUGAR and re-found the joy undiminished, did you feel less you or more you? When you ate a simple meal slowly and felt satisfied, steady for hours, was that a stranger's body or yours before the noise?

It was yours.

We all built an identity around the thing that caged us because it gave the day a shape: hunt, hit, crash, promise. Take away the cage and, for a moment, the shape is gone. That moment can feel like losing yourself. It is not. It is finding the floor under the costume. You change nothing else in your life. You walk the same streets, shop the same aisles, sit at the same tables — and you see them as a free person.

If a thought arises tomorrow, next week, next year — "That's just how I am, I need something sweet" — you do not argue with your personality, you re-label with joy. You say: there is the old story, visiting an address I no longer live at. You smile that the Nibbler is smaller today than yesterday. You remember that the Big Confection fed it with a lie about who you are, and you no longer feed that lie.

You are not a sugar person trying to become a free person. You are a free person who was taught to call himself a sugar person for a while.

And the proof you are free is not months away. It is now. In the very next meal you eat because you are hungry and stop because you are satisfied — clean, light, steady — you will feel what you felt before the confection ever taught you to misread it.

You were never the problem the trap described.

You were the person the trap needed you to believe you were.

You no longer do.

**SUMMARY**

- The idea of a sweet tooth or addictive personality as a fixed cause is a story the trap taught; the traits feel inborn because the conditioning started early at family tables where BAD SUGAR was framed as love and reward.
- Population change shows the cause is the trap, not genes — when BAD SUGAR became abundant and engineered, far more people showed the same pattern in a single generation, which genes cannot explain.
- Cravings and loss of control are the result of living in the trap, not the reason you entered it; the discomfort you called proof of a personality is a small, dying physical tug misread by a belief.
- Scientists disagree about calling everyday sweet use an addiction and human evidence remains an open question; both sides grant that restrict-then-binge patterns and cues can drive wanting, which is circuitry to re-label, not a verdict on character.
- This pattern is common, comparable in frequency to alcohol and tobacco patterns, which removes self-blame without labelling you.
- Even if you did have a stronger pull, there is still no benefit being given up — the pull is toward relief from a low the trap maintains — so freedom does not require becoming a different person, only seeing that BAD SUGAR does nothing FOR you.
