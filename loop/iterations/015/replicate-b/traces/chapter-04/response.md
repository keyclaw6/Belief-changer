Chapter 4
THE TREAT THAT NEVER WAS

IN THIS CHAPTER — the biscuit tin on the worktop, a great meal at a real table, a child eating a peach in the garden, the wrapper that promised more than was inside

*BAD SUGAR never gave you pleasure. It only borrowed it and made you credit the loan to itself.*

### THE PLEASURE YOU ARE SURE ABOUT

I am going to take the nicest line you have and hold it up to the light.

That line is the one you protect most warmly. It says: But it tastes amazing. That is real pleasure. You can tell me the health facts, you can tell me the trap, but you cannot tell me I did not enjoy it. That taste on the tongue, that first bite of biscuit, that spoon into ice cream after a meal — that was a genuine pleasure or treat. That was mine.

Of course you think that. We all did. If I told you the pleasure was not there, you would rightly tell me I was not listening. The pleasure was there. The sweetness was there. The mouth said yes. I am not going to tell you that you imagined the taste. I am going to ask you who you should credit for it.

We fell into the Sugar Trap in the same way. We were handed a story before we had language for it. Sweet means good. Sweet means love. Sweet means well done, you have been good this week. Sweet means celebration. The Big Confection did not need to argue. It only needed to sit beside every warm moment and whisper, that warmth is me. After a thousand sittings, you believed it. Not because you reasoned it. Because you never thought to separate the thing from the moment it sat beside.

So let me separate it now, simply, without a fight.

Think of a great meal. Not a sugar hit. A real meal you loved — bread still warm, soup, fish or stew, salad that snapped, potatoes roasted properly, cheese, fruit that smelled like the garden. Friends around you or quiet in your own kitchen, time to eat, hunger honest when you sat down. Do you remember how good it tasted?

You never once said that meal needed sugar to be wonderful. The pleasure was in the food, the cooking, the hunger that asked to be met, the company, your own alive mouth meeting real flavour. That is taste. That is pleasure that belongs to the food and to you.

Now think of the moment you reach for BAD SUGAR. A plain, pale biscuit from the tin. A sweet that looks brown and white and busy in its wrapper. On its own, without the story, what is that taste? Sugar, fat, a little salt, crunch engineered to melt quickly. If the taste were truly magnificent in itself, you would sit with it as you sit with that great meal — slowly, noticing, satisfied. Do you?

You do not. You eat it quickly. You want another. The first bite promised something the second bite is already trying to renew. That is not the signature of genuine pleasure. That is the signature of a wanting circuit being tapped. The sweetness is real on the tongue for a second, but the pleasure you credit to it — the soothed feeling, the little lift, the sense that now the moment is complete — that feeling was already arriving from the room, the pause, the break from work, the people, the relief of sitting down. BAD SUGAR sneaked a ride on it and taught you to thank the rider instead of the horse.

Do you see the trick? I know this is hard to accept, but look at your own evidence.

You have said it yourself about the afternoon lift: I would have something quick and sweet but now know that will only give a short term lift which will inevitably be followed by a fast drop. You were not describing a pleasure that added to your life. You were describing a loan with interest. A short lift, a fast drop. That sentence already knows the game.

### THE MASK TEST

Let me give you a test you cannot argue with, because your own mouth gives the verdict.

Take a plain biscuit, the kind without much taste — dry, dull, a little wheaty and not much else. Eat one plain. Not with tea, not with sugar. What do you taste? Not much. Most people leave half.

Now put sugar on it. Or chocolate. Or a thick iced topping. Eat it now. Better?

Of course better. That is the point you think you are making. Sugar makes it delicious.

It does not. It masks. That is different.

If sugar were a genuine taste pleasure in itself, it would be magnificent alone. Spoon it, by itself, and eat it by the spoonful for pleasure the way you eat that great meal. You do not. No one does for long. A spoonful of sugar on the tongue alone is sharp, sickly, and quickly unpleasant. You do not sit with a bowl of sugar and say, what a wonderful flavour. You put sugar on things whose own flavour is poor, or you put it with fat so the whole engineered bite goes down fast and calls the next bite.

That is the proof your mouth already gave you. The sweetness is not excellent flavour. It is a mask that hides that there is not excellent flavour. The food industry does not take superb food and improve it with BAD SUGAR. It takes cheap, bland, long-life matter and makes it craveable — fat plus sugar tuned together to push the wanting button so hard that you think wanting is liking. The fact is, foods tuned from refined carbs and fats light up the same wanting circuit in the brain that addictive substances light up, and the fat-plus-sugar combo seems to have a supra-additive kick — I say seems to because that extra kick is the best reading of engineered desirability so far, not a proven law — but the intent is not contested: the product is designed to crowd the circuit and call the next dose.

Do you feel how that reassigns the credit?

The biscuit did not become a great food. It became a better trap. The crunch, the melt, the bliss-point — those are not flavours like roast chicken or ripe peach. They are engineering choices to bypass satiety and keep the hand moving. You were not enjoying taste. You were feeling wanting being triggered and briefly relieved, and calling that pleasure. The difference between wanting and liking is the whole trap, and you can taste it now.

Ask yourself, straight: when have you ever finished a bag of sweets and thought, that was deeply satisfying, like a good meal is satisfying? You have not. You have thought, I wish I had not eaten the whole bag, or, one more will finish it. The wanting did not end in satisfaction. It ended when the bag ended, or when you felt a little sick. That is not how a genuine pleasure behaves.

And see what that does to your story about taste. If BAD SUGAR were a genuine pleasure or treat, it would not need to hide a dull carrier. It would not need fat to carry it. It would not need to be eaten quickly before the liking fades. The masking disproves the claim. The mask is the evidence.

I am warm to you here, because you were not foolish to believe it. We were all taught to read the mask as the face. From childhood, the dull biscuit became the nice biscuit only when it was sugared, and we were told the sugar was the kindness. No wonder you credit the sugar. The con was installed by everyone who loved you, not by you.

But now you have seen it. A great meal needs no mask. A peach needs no mask. The carrier that needs a mask is Confessing, not boasting.

### THE CHILD IN THE GARDEN

Come with me to the garden, because the garden tells the truth without arguments.

Watch a small child eat. Not a child handed a bag of sweets as a prize. A child handed food when hungry. Offer that child a peach, or strawberries, or warm bread, or cold milk, or chicken and rice when hunger is honest. Watch the face. Pleasure is immediate, noisy, and complete. The child chews, looks up, goes back to play. Hunger, eating, stopping. No negotiation. No one bite becomes the box. No drawer to open at three o'clock. The child tastes fully, is satisfied, and leaves it.

Now watch the same child meet BAD SUGAR as a treat. What happens? Speed. Grabbing. Eyes on the bag, not on the moment. Not satisfaction but excitement that does not settle. Ask the child an hour later if they are steady and they are not. They are restless for more, or flat and whining for more. That is not taste being enjoyed. That is wanting being taught.

You were that child. Your mouth has not changed. Your body still knows real flavour and still knows when it has had enough — when the signal is not being drowned by an engineered one.

I say that to return the pleasure to its rightful owners. There are three owners and none of them is BAD SUGAR.

First, the food. Real food has real taste. Sweetness as it occurs in fruit, in roasted vegetables, in milk, is part of flavour, not a mask over it. It arrives with fibre, water, structure, the time it takes to chew and be satisfied. That is why it satisfies. You taste it and you stop when you are done, and you feel clean and light after, not noisy.

Second, the occasion. The birthday, the break, the sofa after a hard day, the friends laughing, the film starting — all the warmth you assigned to sugar belonged to the moment. The moment lent its light to whatever was in your hand, and you thanked the wrong guest. Think of the best bite you remember. Was it the sugar, or was it who you were with, where you were, how hungry you were, how the day had gone? Remove the sugared thing and leave the moment intact — the laughter stays, the relief stays, the company stays. BAD SUGAR was only ever sneaking a ride.

Third, you. Your tongue, your nose, your hunger, your ability to feel steady and pleased — you are the taster. You were there before the trap was built. You are the source of enjoyment, not the substance. The trap taught you to credit the dose for pleasure your own body produced. Take the credit back.

Do you want another proof? Notice what BAD SUGAR does to pleasure over time. A genuine pleasure gets better with attention. Real taste grows more interesting the more you notice it — the peach next week is even better, the bread you learn to make becomes more delicious. BAD SUGAR goes the other way. The first dose of a new sweet is the best it will ever be, and then you need more to feel the same flicker. Each hit borrows more and pays back less. That is not pleasure deepening. That is the wanting circuit being crowded and then dulled. In the end you are not eating for taste at all. You are eating to quiet the faint, empty tug that the last dose created and to chase a masking that no longer even tastes good.

Look at that life honestly. Does it look like a person enjoying a treat? Or does it look like a person living tired, wired and craving?

That triple is the state the trap leaves us in, not the state we started in. Tired because the spike-and-drop never lets steady energy arrive; wired because the engineered hit keeps the nervous system buzzing for the next; craving because wanting has been taught to fire at sight, smell, wrapper, three-o'clock light through the office blinds. When I say you live tired, wired and craving, I am not diagnosing you. I am naming the common, ordinary condition of the hooked eater who credits sugar with pleasure while it steadily takes pleasure away. The phrase will stay with you because it describes your afternoons without needing a chart. You know it in your body already — tired, wired and craving — and once named, you cannot credit that state to a treat.

### THE PLEASURE WAS THE SITUATION ALL ALONG

Let me land this where it cannot slip.

You thought BAD SUGAR was a genuine pleasure or treat because the best moments felt better when it was there. The fact is the best moments were best already, and BAD SUGAR stepped into their light and claimed it.

Take any moment you love — the tin opened while the kettle boils, the break you earned, the table after work. Replay it, faithfully, without BAD SUGAR. Keep everything else. The kettle still boils. The break still arrives. The friends still talk. The hunger still asks honestly and food still answers it. What is lost?

Nothing is lost that was ever real. What is lost is the mask, the quick wanting, the noisy drop an hour later, the faintly edgy feeling that sends you back to the tin. What is left is taste that is taste, relief that is real because nothing created the tension it relieves, and a steadiness that does not crash.

I am not asking you to believe me because I say so. I am asking you to believe your own mouth. Your mouth already told you the mask test. Your memory already told you the short lift and the fast drop. Your eye already saw the child satisfied with real food and restless with the bag. Your afternoons already told you the cost triple you live in when you feed the trap — tired, wired and craving — a state no genuine pleasure ever leaves behind.

Hold that and do not turn it into a rule about food. This is not a diet prescription and this chapter is not telling you what to eat. I am not giving you a new willpower plan or a list of forbidden delights. GOOD SUGAR — whole fruit, vegetables, unrefined foods eaten as food — was never the target and is not counted here. The point is not to make eating harder. The point is to see that BAD SUGAR gave you nothing extra to enjoy, only a louder noise that made steady enjoyment harder to feel.

You will still hear the old line flicker. The Big Confection is well rehearsed. It will say, but this one really is a treat. Do not fight the thought. Do not tell it to go away. Replace its credit assignment in plain speech. Say to yourself, flat and warm: that is the old credit error — a genuine pleasure or treat — and then tell the truth: the pleasure was the food, the moment, me. It is doing plenty TO you. It is doing nothing FOR you. That is not suppression. That is seeing.

For years we put sweetness on poor food and called the poor food nice. We put BAD SUGAR beside good moments and called the good moments sugary. We paid for that mistake not only in teaspoons but in the small theft of joy itself — because a borrowed pleasure never satisfies, and a real pleasure is diminished when it must share the credit with a noise.

See it, and the wanting loses its glamour on its own. You do not need to white-knuckle past the biscuit tin. You need to see the tin accurately: not a box of treats, but a box of masks that keep you tired, wired and craving while teaching you to thank the mask for the face.

Once seen, it cannot be unseen. The next chapter will turn to the other story — that sugar gives you fuel and energy — and show you that the lift you felt was the crash arriving in disguise. You do not need that argument to settle taste. Taste is settled here. The treat was never the sugar. The treat was everything else, and it is still there, clean, waiting for you to taste it without the mask.

**SUMMARY**

- The pleasure you felt with BAD SUGAR was real, but it belonged to the food, the moment, and you — not to the dose that sneaked a ride on them.
- Masking a dull carrier with sweetness disproves genuine liking; a truly delicious taste would not need a mask, and BAD SUGAR alone is not enjoyed for its own flavour.
- Engineered fat-plus-sugar products are tuned to trigger the brain's wanting circuit and seem to hit hardest as a combo by design — that is craveability by intent, not proof of a personal defect.
- A child satisfied by real food and restless after sweets shows the natural baseline: hunger, eating, stopping, steadiness — the same baseline you still have under the noise.
- The trapped state is not pleasure but a repeatable cost — tired, wired and craving — which no genuine pleasure or treat ever leaves behind; seeing where the pleasure truly lives dissolves the treat illusion.
