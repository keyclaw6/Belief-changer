Chapter 6
THE REWARD YOU PAY YOURSELF

IN THIS CHAPTER — the kitchen at seven after a hard day, the late-night sofa with the lights low, the quiet desk after stress, the drawer, the wrapper, the empty bowl in your hand

*That sweet was never a reward you gave yourself — it was a fee you paid the trap with the very goodness you earned.*

### YOU EARNED IT — OF COURSE YOU DID

Of course you think you deserve it.

I do not argue with that feeling. Not for a second.

You had the hard day. You handled the difficult call. You got the children fed, the work done, the mess cleared. You were patient when you did not want to be patient. You kept going when you wanted to stop. The evening comes and a voice says, very softly and very reasonably:

I've been good. I deserve a treat.

Who would not want to answer that voice with kindness? You are not greedy. You are not weak. You are a person who did something hard and wants to feel that it mattered. Wanting comfort at that moment is not a flaw. It is human.

We all did the same. We came through the kitchen after the day had taken what it takes, and we reached for something sweet and called it self-care. We sat on the late sofa when the house went quiet and told ourselves: just this. A little chocolate. A biscuit. A bowl of sweets to mark the end of effort. We whispered the line we have all whispered:

"celebrating, rewarding myself, going to the cinema, etc with chocolate."

That is not a confession. That is the trap speaking in your own kind voice. It learned to sound like you.

Let me sit beside you at that kitchen and ask the simplest question, warmly, as a friend who has sat there too: when you gave yourself that sweet, who did you actually pay?

You believed BAD SUGAR was a genuine pleasure or treat, and that this treat was love. Private love. Earned love. The one piece of the day that was finally for you. No one had to share it. No one could judge it. It asked nothing of you. It was yours.

I understand why that felt true. You were tired and you deserved kindness. The Sugar Trap stepped forward and said: I am kindness. Take me.

But kindness does not leave you more tired than it found you.

### WHAT YOU ACTUALLY HANDED OVER

Let me show you what the payment really was.

You had virtue. Real virtue — effort, patience, care. You carried the day. That virtue is warm and solid. It belongs to you. The Big Confection cannot create it, so it steals it. It waits until you have earned something — a completed task, a survived stress, an evening of quiet — and then it offers to convert that virtue into a dose.

Give me your hard day, it says. I will give you sweetness.

You hand it over. You eat quickly. The wrapper opens fast, not slowly like a favourite meal. You barely taste the second bite. For twenty seconds there is sweetness. Then what?

Do you feel loved? Or do you feel a little less steady than before you started?

Ask the body, not the story.

You know the answer because you have kept the receipts without ever writing them down. The late-night dose does not finish the day. It reopens it. You go from tired but done to tired, wired and craving. The rest you earned gets traded for a buzz that wants another buzz. The kindness you intended for yourself gets spent on a hit that leaves you needing another hit to feel kind again.

We call that a reward. A reward should settle. It should say: well done, you can rest now. Does BAD SUGAR settle?

You know the evening that followed the bowl. You sat on the sofa with the empty packet in your lap and the faint, empty, slightly edgy feeling humming again, louder than before. You promised yourself: I will be good tomorrow. I have been good this week, so just this once is fine. Once a week is fine. The rule you made to control it became the schedule that keeps it alive.

The fact is, the pattern that drives the wanting is not having a little all the time. It is the yo-yo itself — restrict, then reward. Be good, then pay yourself with sugar. Be good again, then pay again. That intermittent payment — good, then dose, then good, then dose — is the very rhythm that escalates craving and bingeing. When the reward comes intermittently, the want grows, not shrinks. When it is there to be taken whenever you want without a rule, the drama collapses. We built the drama ourselves with the rule we thought would save us. The reward-frame keeps the loop alive.

You were not rewarding yourself. You were paying the Sugar Trap with the goodness you earned, and it pocketed your virtue and left you emptier.

Is that self-care?

I say this harshly about the trap and warmly to you. You were not foolish to want care. We were all conned into believing care comes in a wrapper. The con took your best moment — the honest pride of having done hard things — and stuck its product on top of it so it could take the credit. You did the hard thing. You felt the warm pride. The sweet happened at the same time, so you thanked the sweet for the warmth that was already yours.

### WHY THE COMFORT NEVER COMFORTS

Here is the cruellest part of the private reward. It does not even comfort later.

Across many thousands of people followed over years, those who ate more sweets reported higher odds of low mood later — not lower — and checking the other direction did not explain it away. Not a sentence on you, just the pattern across people — but it matters because it tests the promise you were sold: take this dose and you will feel better.

You were promised relief from stress. The pattern shows the daily treat predicts more flatness later, not less. The comfort you paid for with your hard day buys a dip you then have to pay to relieve again.

We told ourselves BAD SUGAR was a genuine pleasure or treat against the day's sharp edge. But a genuine comfort would leave you steadier for having had it. This leaves you less steady. A genuine self-payment would leave you satisfied, uninterested in buying the same thing again ten minutes later. This leaves you thinking about the kitchen again before the programme is over.

You have lived that. You know the sofa at ten. The dose is finished and the mind goes looking. One bite becomes the box. The hand reaches before you have decided to reach. You tell yourself: I just crave sweets and when I eat them I feel like I can't stop. That is not you failing to enjoy a treat. That is the loop doing exactly what a trap is built to do: keep you tired, wired and craving so that you will reach again and call the reaching love.

Tired because the spike never lets steady rest arrive. Wired because the wanting circuit is buzzing, watching for the next cue. Craving because every private reward you gave has taught the brain that evening equals dose. That triple is not your character. It is the normal state of a person who has been taught to pay themselves in sugar. It is doing plenty TO you. It is doing nothing FOR you.

And notice what it stole besides steadiness. It stole the proof of your own goodness.

You did something difficult and good today. That goodness is real. It does not need chocolate to be real. When you pay the dose, the memory of the day blurs. You remember the wrapper, not the work. You remember the bargain, not the pride. The trap takes the very evidence that you are a capable, caring person and exchanges it for a sticky aftertaste and a quiet shame you then misread as need for more comfort. You went to bed less proud than you came into the kitchen. No reward should do that.

You deserve better than a payment that diminishes you.

### THE SAME EVENING WITHOUT THE FEE

Let me replay the same evening with you, without a wrapper in it, so you can feel where the warmth really lives.

You come into the kitchen at seven. The day has been hard. The light is low. The counter is a little messy. You are honestly tired. Not broken. Tired in the way a person who did good work is tired. You feel it in your shoulders. You let yourself feel it — not as a problem to be fixed, but as proof you did something.

You do not open the drawer for a fix. You open it for what a kitchen is for. Water. A piece of fruit if you are hungry — GOOD SUGAR, real food eaten as food, never the target and never counted. Or nothing at all, because you are not hungry yet, only tired. You stand there a minute. You breathe. You notice the quiet house, the warmth of the room, the fact that the hard part of the day is done. Is there kindness there? Yes. It is the kindness of being done. It was there before the wrapper ever arrived.

You move to the sofa at ten. The lights are low. The day is finally yours. The old thought arrives, as it will, because the brain learned a cue: sofa equals sweet. The thought says: you deserve it. Go on. Just a treat.

You do not argue with the thought. You do not push it away. You see it for what it is — the Big Confection calling, using your own kind voice — and you answer it with truth.

Of course I deserve kindness. This is kindness.

You make tea because you like tea. You pull a blanket around you because warmth is kind. You let the day settle. You notice that the faint tug, if it is there at all, is small and empty and already passing, not a command. You notice that pride is still there, intact, because you did not trade it. You did not pay a fee. You kept the good you earned.

Does the evening lose anything?

The sofa stays soft. The quiet stays quiet. The love you have for the people you did hard things for stays. The pride stays. The relief of being done stays. The only thing missing is the dose that was sneaking a ride on all of it, taking credit for warmth it did not create and charging you interest in the form of craving later.

You thought the sweet added love to the moment. It did not. You were loving yourself and the dose got in the photo.

That is the whole inversion. It is not that you comfort yourself because you are stressed. You feel stressed because you have learned to comfort yourself with a thing that keeps you tired, wired and craving. Take the sweet out of the picture and the comfort remains, cleaner, because nothing is collecting a fee for it. It is the other way around.

I want you to feel that in your body now, not as an idea. The warmth you called chocolate was your warmth. The rest you called sugar was the rest of being done. The love you called a treat was your own hard-earned love, looking for a place to land. Give it to yourself directly — with rest, with warmth, with the honest satisfaction of food when you are hungry — and you keep it. Give it to BAD SUGAR and you pay it away.

### LOVE BELONGS TO YOU, NOT THE DOSE

You will hear the reward thought again. Of course you will. At the quiet desk after stress, after you have been good, after you have coped well — that is when the trap speaks sweetest, because your virtue is brightest then and the trap wants to bill you for it.

When it comes, do not shame the feeling. Welcome it as proof you did something worthy. Say to it, flatly and kindly:

You earned kindness today. That kindness is yours. BAD SUGAR is not kindness.

Say it and notice what happens. The thought does not get stronger when you see it clearly. It gets smaller. Because you have taken back the credit.

We fell for the idea that BAD SUGAR was a genuine pleasure or treat reserved for the moments we deserve love. That was brainwashing. There is nothing genuine in a payment that leaves the payer emptier. There is nothing loving in a fee that keeps you wanting. A genuine reward settles the day. A con collects it.

You are not being asked to be stricter with yourself. You are being shown to be kinder, accurately. Rest when you are tired. Eat real food when you are hungry. Let pride sit where pride belongs — in the work you did, not the wrapper you bought. The love you were looking for in the bowl was your own love, and it never left you. The bowl just taught you to look in the wrong place.

Once seen, you cannot pay that fee with a straight face again. You would not thank a thief for holding your purse. You would not call a toll love.

You deserve comfort. You deserve care. You deserve evenings that end with you more settled than you began. BAD SUGAR never gave you any of those things. It gave you a noisy twenty seconds and billed you your steadiness. You never needed to pay yourself with it. You needed to keep what you had already earned.

Let that settle now, easily and kindly. The private reward is settled here. Social celebration — the birthdays and tables where sugar pretends it is belonging itself — we will meet later and strip it there, head-on, without losing a drop of joy. For tonight, keep this simple truth and let the relief arrive with it: the warmth was you all along. The dose was only ever taking the credit, and the fee.

**SUMMARY**

- The private sweet felt like self-kindness because it arrived at the exact moment you had earned something. The warmth was real but belonged to your effort and pride, not to the dose that happened at the same time.
- Paying yourself with BAD SUGAR is a fee, not a reward — it trades virtue for a brief hit that leaves you less steady and more likely to seek the next hit to feel kind again.
- The intermittent restrict-then-reward rhythm itself escalates wanting. The yo-yo keeps you tired, wired and craving, which you then misread as need for a treat.
- A daily treat does not predict better mood later — across many people followed over time the pattern runs the other way — so the comfort story fails even on its own promise.
- Replaying the same hard-day kitchen and late sofa without BAD SUGAR leaves every drop of kindness intact — rest, warmth, pride, and quiet satisfaction remain — proving the dose only ever sneaked a ride on love that was already yours.
