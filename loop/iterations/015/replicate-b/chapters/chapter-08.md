Chapter 8
WHO BUILT THIS TRAP

IN THIS CHAPTER — the supermarket aisle at eye level, the checkout lane, the crinkle of a wrapper at 3pm, the white room where sweetness is tuned

*The craving you thought was yours was designed for you long before you ever felt it.*

### YOU DID NOT MAKE THIS HUNGER

If this is a trap, who set it?

You have asked the right question at the right time. You have killed the idea that the sweet was a genuine pleasure. You have seen the lift pose as fuel and then collect its fee as a crash. You have watched the private reward take your virtue and leave you heavier than it found you. And still, on some ordinary Tuesday, the hand hovers. Not because you are weak. Because something was built to make it hover.

We did not build this hunger inside ourselves.

We were handed it. Quietly. Early. Without ever being asked if we wanted it. And the fact that it feels like our own wanting is the cleverest part of the build.

Think of your own week. Monday you decide you will be sensible. By Wednesday the drawer calls. By Friday you deserve something. By Sunday you have had the box again and you say, I just can't stop once I start. That line is not a confession about your character. It is a report about a design you were placed inside.

I tell you plainly, you were not foolish. You were conned. That is the confidence trick at the heart of the Sugar Trap, and naming it takes the shame off you and puts the weight where it belongs — on the trick, and on the people who keep laying it.

Look at the size of what you are up against and you will stop calling yourself weak. For fifty years an entire architecture has been refined to get a child's hand to reach and an adult's hand to keep reaching. That architecture is not in your will. It is in the aisle.

And once you see the aisle as a piece of engineering, the craving stops sounding like your voice. It sounds like instructions you were taught to repeat.

### THE WHITE ROOM WHERE YOUR WANTING WAS TUNED

Come with me up the aisle. Not to taste. To watch.

Every product at eye level paid to be there. The colour was tested. The name was tested. The packet was tested for how it sounds when it opens. The height on the shelf was measured against where your eye falls when you are tired and pushing a trolley at six in the evening. The checkout lane was stacked because they know you are waiting, a little bored, a little empty, and a sweet at arm's length has the best chance of all.

None of that is accident. And none of it is about food.

Behind those choices is a white room. Not a conspiracy in a film. A bright, ordinary laboratory with white coats, white benches, dials and trays. People who are very good at their jobs sit there and tune. A little more fat for mouthfeel, a little more salt to sharpen, a little more sugar to hit the peak called bliss — the point where the tongue lights up and says more, now — then a dial for crunch that shatters at just the right speed, a dial for melt that disappears before you can feel full, a dial for smell that blooms when the bag opens.

This is the Bliss-Point Laboratory in its real form. Armies of scientists — not one villain twirling a moustache, but rooms of careful, salaried professionals — weaponizing abundance. Their job is to make the next bite want to be eaten before the last one is finished. To bypass the signal that says you have had enough. To crowd the very same wanting circuit that drugs crowd, but with something you can buy for a pound and feel virtuous about on the way home.

The fact is, refined carbohydrates and fats evoke activity in that wanting circuit, and when fat and sugar are engineered together the combined cue seems to be stronger than either alone. That seems matters. It is not proven as a law; it is what the pattern looks like so far. The desirability is engineered, not accidental. You are not imagining that some packets call louder than others. They were built to call louder.

Stand in that aisle and watch the packet you have always blamed yourself for. See the dials behind it. The crunch was chosen. The melt was chosen. The bliss point was titrated until your hand would move half a second faster. The dose you called a treat is a tuned hit. The craveability you called your sweet tooth is engineering you can see, once you look.

And here is the awe you have not been allowed to feel. Your body is fantastically sophisticated. It knows hunger from need. It knows satisfaction as a steady, quiet fullness. It knows when food is food and when it is not. Taste recovers. Appetite steadies. A child offered real food eats, stops, and plays — no counting, no bargaining. That system is elegant. What the white room makes is crude by comparison — a loud, blunt trick that shouts over a quiet instrument and then tells you the shouting is your appetite.

You were not out-eaten. You were outgunned. And there is no shame in being outgunned by a room built for no other purpose than to outgun you. There is only relief in seeing it, because what is built can be seen through, and what is seen through no longer compels as if it were love.

You have lived inside the Sugar Trap not because you love BAD SUGAR, but because the trap was laid exactly where you live — at eye level, at the till, at 3pm — and the story that keeps it alive is the Big Confection whispering that the wanting you feel proves the packet gives you something.

### WHY ONE BITE CALLS THE NEXT

Why does one bite become the box?

Not because you lack character. Because each dose re-arms the wanting.

The fact is, each binge releases dopamine in the wanting centre. That firing is not pleasure. It is wanting, re-lit. The brain learns to want again, a little more, a little sooner. You do not feel higher than a person who never took the dose. You feel the empty quieten for a minute because the circuit was lit, then you sit a fraction lower than normal as it falls again, and an opposing chemical rises to push you down. The lift guarantees the next low.

That is why the packet calls before you open it. The same loop recruits the body's own opioid and cue circuitry. Sight, smell, the crinkle, the time of day — all become bells. The wrapper does not need to be in your hand to fire the wanting. Your brain learned the cue. This is circuitry, not character. Understandable reactivity, not proof you are broken.

Watch it happen without tasting and the mystery dies. The 3pm drawer opens. The hand moves. You eat the sweet. For ninety seconds the faint emptiness softens. Then the dip comes. Then the story says, see, you needed it. But the sweet made the dip. The rescue is the perpetrator in a different coat.

Engineered food widens this wheel. The products that shout loudest are the ones tuned to crowd that circuit most efficiently — sugar with fat, salt with crunch, melt that defeats satiety — so one bite is designed to make the next bite feel necessary. You call it losing control. I call it a product doing exactly what it was tuned to do, inside a brain doing exactly what a brain does when it is tuned at.

And now the freeing part. None of this proves you are sentenced. It proves you were recruited.

If this were about pleasure, the wanting would fall after pleasure. It rises. If BAD SUGAR gave you something real, you would feel steady after. You feel more hooked, more hunted, more in need of the next fix. The behaviour advertises itself as relief and delivers recruitment. That is a con, not a treat.

You do not need to fight the cue. You only need to re-label it the moment it fires and feel the little lift of seeing through it. That crinkle at 3pm is not your sweet tooth speaking. It is a bell someone else installed, ringing in a circuit that lights wanting on command. Let it ring. It has nowhere to go when you know who hung it.

### THE HONEST UNCERTAINTY WE WILL NOT HIDE FROM

I will not con you the way the trap did, by pretending certainty where there is none.

On the question of whether BAD SUGAR is an addiction in the same clinical sense as drugs, good scientists disagree. Some of the keenest researchers in Cambridge argue that the bingeing we see reflects the pattern — the restrict-then-binge, the intermittent access — more than sugar chemistry alone, and that human evidence for a true addiction remains thin. The debate is not settled. It is an open question.

I honour both sides because you deserve to. The binge behaviour under intermittent access is real. The dopamine wanting, the after-low, the cue firing — all of it can be measured. Whether we call that whole picture an addiction with a capital A is contested; that it behaves like a trap is not.

This matters for your freedom. You do not need a label to escape. You need to see the engineering of wanting and the story that feeds it. The packet was designed to be craveable. The intermittent rules we live by — be good all day, have a dose at night; be strict all week, pay yourself on Friday — amplify that craveability. The wanting circuit is lit, the low follows, the cue learns. That is enough to explain why you felt hooked without ever calling yourself diseased. And it is enough to break without ever fighting a disease.

If anyone tells you this is just willpower, they are selling the very story that keeps the trap alive. If anyone tells you that calling BAD SUGAR engineered desirability is the same as calling you an addict, they are overclaiming. The fact is more precise and more freeing: an ordinary brain, doing ordinary learning, was outgunned by ordinary engineering, and the belief that the dose gives you something — the Big Confection — was taught until it felt like instinct.

Hold both. The desirability is engineered, so the call you feel is not your greed. The diagnosis is contested, so you are not sentenced. You are a person who learned a false remedy, inside a system that teaches that remedy at every turn.

### SEEING THE BUILD IS ALREADY LEAVING IT

You came into this chapter thinking the craving lived inside you and had to be battled. You leave seeing it was built outside you and only lives inside you as a learned cue.

That inversion changes everything. You do not need to be stronger than a laboratory. You need to stop believing the laboratory's product gives you something.

Try it where the trap is laid. Go up the aisle tomorrow for milk and bread. Keep your eyes open. Watch the colours, the height, the till. Notice the hand wanting to reach at the cue you have now named. Let it want for a second. Smile. That wanting is a bell that someone else hung. It will ring a few times and fade when not fed. It has no power to make you reach once you know who built it.

I have walked that aisle with thousands. I have watched the same surprise dawn on faces that have blamed themselves for years. The moment they see the white room behind the packet, the packet loses its romance. It looks crude. It looks loud. It looks like what it is — a tuned dose, not food, not love, not rest.

Your body knows food. It knows hunger that arrives honestly and satisfaction that stays steady and light. No dial in a lab can improve that signal. It can only shout over it. Stop feeding the shout and the quiet signal returns on its own. No new discipline. No new diet. Just the old elegance coming back because you stopped jamming it.

You were never weak for living in the Sugar Trap. You were a person with a trusting appetite in a market that learned exactly how to recruit that trust. Be warm to that person. Be harsh to the trap and to the room that builds it.

And keep the anger clean. Not at yourself. Not at the tired cashier. At the manufacture of desire that put a tuned hit where your hand falls when you are tired and waiting. Let that anger be dignified, not bitter — the anger of someone who sees they were outgunned and is glad to be awake now.

Tomorrow the wrapper will still crinkle. The ad will still glow. The aisle will still be at eye level. The bells will still try to ring. You will hear them differently. Not as your sweet tooth. As engineering. Not as pleasure. As recruitment. Not as a treat you are missing. As a crude fix you are free not to feed.

See who built this trap and the trap loses its personality. It stops being your story and becomes a product. Products can be left on the shelf.

**SUMMARY**

- The wanting you felt was not a personal defect; it was learned inside a market built to recruit that wanting at every turn.
- Products are tuned — bliss point, fat with sugar, crunch and melt — to keep the next bite wanted before the last is finished and to quiet satiety.
- Each dose lights the brain's wanting circuit and guarantees a dip afterward, so the relief is the cause of the next need.
- Cues such as sight, sound, and time of day can fire wanting before a bite; that firing is learned circuitry, not character and not a command.
- Whether this picture should be called an addiction in a clinical sense is contested among scientists; what is not contested is that intermittent rules amplify wanting and that engineered desirability is real.
- Seeing the engineering as external reassigns the craving to its maker and leaves the body’s own hunger and satisfaction as the steady authority.
