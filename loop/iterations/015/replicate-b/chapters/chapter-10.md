Chapter 10
HOW YOU REALLY EAT

IN THIS CHAPTER — early light in the kitchen, a bowl left half-full, the honest hollow before lunch, an apple that snaps clean, taste coming back over quiet days

*You already know how to eat — BAD SUGAR only drowned out the signal, and when the noise stops the steady returns on its own.*

### THE NOISE YOU CALLED NORMAL

We did not lose our appetite. We lost the quiet underneath it.

For years we lived inside a hum. A hit, a lift, a dip, a reach. A biscuit to be good, a bar to get through, a bowl to finish the evening. We called that eating. We called the fidget hunger. We called the slump need. And because the hum never fully stopped, we forgot what silence felt like and assumed the hum was us.

You know the feel I mean. Not true hunger that arrives clean and leaves clean. The other one — the edgy, nagging, never-quite-settled state that kept you scanning. That is not character. That is the Sugar Trap running in the background, feeding you a little, dropping you a little, keeping you in the loop so you would feed it again.

I ask you plainly: when was the last time you felt genuinely hungry and then genuinely satisfied, without a shadow behind it?

Most trapped eaters cannot answer at first. Not because they never felt it, but because BAD SUGAR had been answering the question for them, loudly, for years. It taught us to read every flicker as a command to dose, and then it called the brief relief it gave a pleasure. We were tired, wired and craving — not for an afternoon, not for a bad week, but as the steady weather of the trap — and we thought that weather was normal. The boiling frog thinks warm water is just how water is until it remembers what cool feels like.

Here is the fact that changes the frame without changing a single rule about food: your body makes the glucose it needs from the food you already eat. You do not need to import BAD SUGAR to run. Fruit, vegetables, ordinary meals — the GOOD SUGAR that is simply food — is not the target and never was. BAD SUGAR is the engineered dose, the hit, the fix that learned to ride on top of eating and call itself eating. Remove the dose and you remove nothing your body requires. You remove the noise.

That is why this chapter has no plan to give you. No counting, no points, no forbidden lists beyond the one line you already own: BAD SUGAR is not food to you any more. Everything else is food. Your body is the authority, not a number. My job is not to teach you a new way to eat. It is to let you hear, again, the way you already know.

If you have felt a faint restlessness in the first days without the hit — a little empty echo that comes and goes in under a minute — know this: that is a small, time-limited hump, not a sentence. It tends to flicker most in the first two to five days, then it fades. You do not need to fight it or wean it. You only need to not feed it with belief. The Big Confection says, "That flicker proves you need the hit." The truth is the other way around. The flicker proves the little creature is going quiet.

### WHEN HUNGER KNOCKS

We were taught to fear hunger. The trap taught us that first.

Hunger, in the trap, meant danger — the I-need-to-eat-something-RIGHT-NOW feeling that had to be answered with something quick and sweet. That feeling was not hunger. It was the dip talking. Real hunger does not shout. It knocks.

Let me show you what the knock sounds like when the noise is off.

You wake. The kitchen is cold. The kettle ticks. You are not hungry yet and that is fine. You do not manufacture hunger with a rule. You wait. You move through the morning, you drink water because you are thirsty, not because a book told you to. Then, about three or four hours after you rose, a gentle hollow arrives low in the belly. Not edgy. Not panicked. A friendly hollow, clean, a little empty, slightly expectant. It does not demand a specific packet. It does not argue. It simply says, I am ready.

I remember the first morning I heard it again. I had spent years eating before it could arrive — tea and biscuits at ten because it was ten, a bar at eleven because the drawer was there. That morning I was busy and I forgot to perform the ritual. At about eleven-thirty the hollow came, unmistakable, and I laughed because I recognised it as an old friend from childhood. My body had not forgotten. I had just not been listening.

You do not need to chase that signal. You only need to stop covering it with hits. When it comes, you answer it as you would answer a knock — you open the door and you eat.

And you will notice something else that the trap hid: hunger is not continuous. It comes in a wave. If you are not yet able to eat, it rises, it crests, it passes, and it comes again later. It does not grow into an emergency. That wave is not a dose-clock ticking. It is your body saying, when you are ready, food will be good. The trap turned that wave into dread so the packet could sell itself as rescue. Without the packet, the wave is simply information. You can trust it for the rest of your life because it was built to be trusted.

Ask yourself now, not as theory but as memory: can you recall the difference between the panicky reach and the quiet hollow? You have felt both. One drives you to the packet. One invites you to a plate. The trap needed you to confuse them. Freedom is keeping them distinct.

### A SIMPLE PLATE, EATEN SLOWLY

We believed satisfaction was hard to find. The trap taught us that too.

Satisfaction in the trap was brief, coating, then restless. The hit was sweet, then cloying, then gone, leaving the mouth searching and the blood busy. We called that "moreish" as if it were a virtue. It is not a virtue. It is engineered craveability — the bliss-point learned how to keep the hand moving by never letting the mouth feel finished.

Real satisfaction is different. It is steady. It stays.

I want you to live one ordinary meal with me, slowly, without a single instruction about what must be on the plate. Choose plain, real food you like — eggs and toast, soup and bread, rice and vegetables, fish, meat, cheese, fruit — GOOD SUGAR as food, not as a project. Nothing exotic, nothing to buy specially. Your normal food, without the packet added.

You sit. You look at it for a second longer than you usually do. You smell it. You take a first bite and you chew. Not meditatively, not as homework — simply not fast. You taste salt, you taste warmth, you taste bite. You put the spoon down between mouthfuls without deciding to. Somewhere in the middle your shoulders drop.

And then, a few bites before the plate is empty, something quiet happens. The food stops pulling. Not because you told yourself to stop. Not because you ran out of will. Because your body says, enough. It is not a dramatic fullness. It is a level, even, light fullness that sits easy. You could eat more, but you do not want to. The plate can stay half-full and you feel no tug-of-war about it. That is satiety. That is what enough feels like when no hit is shouting over it.

We spent years overriding that signal with sweetness. We poured a dose on top of enough and called the overshoot pleasure. Then we spent the afternoon tired, wired and craving and called that personality. It was not personality. It was the after-effect of the overshoot.

Try this with me once, in memory, then once in life. Bowl half-full. No commentary. No praise. Just the plain fact: you noticed enough because enough was there to be noticed. Do you see? You did not need to be taught how much is enough. You needed the volume turned down.

You may ask, "But will this hold? Will I feel steady?"

The fact is, without BAD SUGAR your blood does not have to do the spike-and-clean-up work that made the afternoon feel like a roller coaster. Meals become islands of steady energy, three or so a day, with clear hunger and clear satisfaction between them. That is not a promise I need you to believe. It is what happens when you stop feeding the spike. Your body returns to its clean baseline on its own. You change nothing else. You just let the baseline be heard again.

### TASTE WAKES UP

We thought BAD SUGAR made food taste better. It is the other way around.

BAD SUGAR coats. It masks. A plain biscuit that tastes of little becomes "nice" only because sugar shouts over it. That is not liking. That is brilliance being borrowed. The same tongue, washed clean, finds the real food more vivid, not less.

Over the next days without the dose, taste does a small, marvellous thing. It brightens. Not in a fireworks way. In a clean way.

On day two, water tastes like something. On day three, bread smells like fields and butter. An apple — a simple, cold apple from the bag — snaps when you bite it, and the juice is sharp and sweet enough to make you close your eyes. You think, "That is sweet enough." That thought is not a consolation. It is the truth the packet hid: GOOD SUGAR as food was always sweet enough because it was not engineered to be craveable. It was grown to be eaten.

I had a peach that week, late summer, soft at the seam. I had told myself for years I did not really like fruit beyond the hits I allowed myself after being "good." The peach proved me wrong. It was fragrant, it dripped, it was too much and exactly enough, and I ate it standing at the sink with my sleeves pushed up and laughed at the years I had spent reaching past it for a wrapper.

Your tongue is not dull. Your nose is not broken. The trap dulled them by keeping them coated and busy. When you stop feeding the confection, the senses come back without effort, the way hearing comes back when a loud engine stops. You do not train this. You allow it.

And with it comes a small freedom you may not have expected: food becomes favourite again without being a problem. Not a treat you deserve. Not a sin you manage. Favourite, as in everyday favourite — the favourite you come home to because it is yours and it is steady and it likes you back. Real food likes you back. The dose never did.

You worry that without BAD SUGAR eating will be grey. Watch instead. Colour returns. The market stall smells stronger. The soup you made tastes of the soup you made. The pleasure you thought lived in the packet was always in the meal, in the hunger that made the meal welcome, and in the body that could finally hear itself.

This is not restriction. Naming BAD SUGAR is not forbidding food. It is freeing the rest of food to be food again — GOOD SUGAR included, without counting, without fear, without a name for it beyond nourishment you enjoy.

### STEADY IS THE FEELING OF FREE

We thought steadiness was beyond us.

We thought we were the kind of people who rode the roller coaster, who needed sugary somethings to get through, who would always be negotiating — "just a treat," "once a week is fine," "I've managed to be good this week." That negotiation was not evidence of who you are. It was evidence of the trap's script running, feeding the Big Confection the thought that BAD SUGAR gives something you will miss.

Inhabit, for a minute, the ordinary day without it.

Morning light, kettle, the honest hollow arriving later than the packet used to demand. A plate eaten slowly, left half-full without a story. The afternoon where the drawer used to open — now the drawer stays shut not because you are being good but because there is no call to answer. You stand at three o'clock and you are clear. Not buzzing, not heavy. Steady. Light. Clean. The evening where the bowl used to call — now you are satisfied from supper and you go for a walk a little further, you call someone back, you make something with the hands that used to unwrap.

That steadiness is not a reward you earn after weeks of perfect behaviour. It is the baseline that returns when the noise stops. You do not climb to it. You drop into it the moment you stop feeding the trap. Any faint flicker that visits in the first days is the little Nibbler going quiet, delighted to starve, not a reason to dose. You do not fight that flicker. You smile at it. It passes.

You may still hear the packet talk. A wrapper crinkles and an old bell rings — a cue that once learned to fire before the bite. That bell is not a command. It is circuitry. You hear it, you name it joyfully as the trap's old tune, and you watch it pass while your mouth tastes clean. Each time you do, the bell rings a little softer. Not because you resisted — because you did not feed the belief that kept it loud.

This is how you really eat. Not by rule. Not by system. By signal. Hunger arrives as a friendly hollow. Food is chosen from real food you like. Taste wakes up. Enough arrives as a steady, level fullness that stays for hours. Energy is even, mood is even, because you are no longer asking your body to clean up a hit it never needed. You get on with enjoying your life and eating is part of it, not the war with it.

You do not need to prove strength where there is nothing to resist. You need to keep the signal and leave the packet. The signal was there before the trap and it is there after. Change nothing else. Inhabit the old contexts confidently — morning, shop, desk, kitchen — with the new belief that BAD SUGAR added nothing and the body already knows. That belief, guarded, is what makes the steady last for the rest of your life.

You are not giving anything up. You are escaping a theft. The food stays. The sweetness that belongs to food stays. The hunger that tells you when and the satisfaction that tells you enough — that was always yours. BAD SUGAR only ever sneaked a ride on it. Leave the packet on the table and you keep the whole ride.

SUMMARY

- BAD SUGAR is not needed to run your body; your body makes the glucose it needs, so leaving BAD SUGAR is not deprivation.
- Hunger is a clean, friendly hollow that comes in waves; the panicky reach was the dip, not hunger, and you can trust the real signal.
- Satisfaction is a steady, level fullness that stays; it was drowned out by hits that never let enough be felt.
- Taste brightens when the coating stops; real food becomes favourite again without being a problem.
- Any faint restlessness in the first days is brief and passes; the steady baseline returns on its own when you stop feeding the trap, and you change nothing else to live it.
