Chapter 8

THE MORNING HOLDS WITHOUT A LIGHT

IN THIS CHAPTER: the bed waking without reaching, the stairs taken clear, the kitchen cup tasted to the end, the short walk and the pause with nothing lit, and the patio kit remembered without longing.

*The morning was whole before the match was struck, and it stays whole with empty hands.*

WAKING WITHOUT REACHING

You know the old waking because your hands have lived it for years, and you can see it clearly now without defending it.

The alarm sounded in the dark room and the hand left the warm sheet at once to find the pack on the nightstand, and the fingers knew the place by touch in a way they knew no other object in the house. The lighter clicked before the eyes were fully open, and the first draw was taken sitting on the edge of the bed with the shoulders hunched and the throat still thick with sleep. You told yourself the day could not start until the nerves were settled, and you took it as given that the body needed its signal before the feet could meet the floor.

We all lived that reaching for years, and I speak of it with warmth because I lived inside it and I know how necessary it felt from the inside. We coughed through the last hours of the night and woke with the mouth stale and the chest heavy, and we read that heaviness as proof that the day needed its starter. We lit in the half dark and felt the stir quiet for a few minutes, and we credited the paper with giving the morning back to us. The room stayed the same small room with the curtains closed and the air thick, and only the interval since feeding moved, closing briefly and already counting upward again.

Watch one near morning with clear eyes and let the reaching stay undone, because your own breath will teach faster than any argument.

You wake at the same hour in the same bed with the same light coming thin around the curtain edge, and the hand lifts from the sheet and finds nothing, and stays empty. You sit on the edge of the bed with the feet flat on the cool floor, and you take the first full breath of the day with nothing burning beside it. The air moves clean to the bottom of the chest, and the shoulders drop on the exhale without any help from the tip. You stand and cross to the window and draw the curtain, and the street stands in its early quiet with bins out and a cat on a wall and the sky pale above the roofs. Nothing in you leans toward the nightstand, because nothing there ever started the day.

Ask the waking its own plain questions and listen for the only honest answers your life allows. Did the eyes open clearer because smoke crossed the throat before water touched it. Did the shoulders loosen deeper through a chest tightened with the first dose of the day, or through a chest filling clean. Did the mind grow readier for the hours ahead through a mouth coated stale, or through a mouth rinsed clean at the sink. You know the coating dulled and the breath cleared and the light steadied, and you know the cigarette only timed its own closing to coincide with all three, the way a lodger takes credit for the sunrise because he happened to be awake for it.

I lay that morning here in full because the trap taught for years that the first minutes cannot be met bare, and bare hands meet them whole. You wash the face and feel the cold water wake the skin. You dress with the window open and the air moving across the room. You hear the house tick into its day with pipes warming and a neighbour door closing below. Each small act lands in its own rightness, and each carries its own quiet signal that the body has begun. No paper signs for any of them, and none asks for a signature.

Here is the verdict that closes this room, stated flat because your own lungs have already spoken it. You started the day. The dose only delayed it.

STAIRS TAKEN CLEAR

You know the stairs because your legs have counted them every morning for years, and you know the bargain that was made on them without ever being spoken aloud.

You came down with the cup in one hand and the pack heavy in the dressing-gown pocket, and you paused on the landing where the chest pulled tight halfway down. The breath came short near the bottom, and a small dry cough shook out before the kitchen door. You read the cough as weather and the stairs as age, and you took the body to be much the same as any other body that climbs before coffee. You lit at the back door with the kitchen still cool behind you, and you read the loosening after the draw as proof that the air outside had helped.

We all paid that toll for years while calling it ordinary, and I name it kindly because shame never taught a lung to open. We climbed slowly and rested the hand on the rail near the top, and we learned which step to pause on so the pause would look like thought. We kept the handkerchief near and cleared the throat before speaking to anyone, and we believed the morning chest was simply the price of being awake in the world. The price never showed on any bill except the breath itself, and we signed for it daily without reading the total.

Take the same stairs with nothing in your hands and feel what your own body already knows how to do.

You come down with both feet sure on the boards and the hand light on the rail, and the breath reaches to the bottom of the chest without catching halfway. You cross the kitchen and set the kettle on with the window cracked, and the cool air moves across the face without any sting behind it. You step out to the kerb with the bag for the bins and draw one deep breath at the gate, and the air goes down clean and comes back clean. The chest stays quiet after, and the throat stays clear for speech. No cough follows you back inside, and no pause needs to be dressed as thought.

Did the stair grow shorter because smoke rose at the back door after it. Did the chest grow looser through lungs lined with the night, or through lungs washed with clean air. Did the legs grow lighter because a dose was taken at the bottom, or because the blood moved as legs moved. You already know the true answers when both sides stand together in daylight. The climb cleared through movement and air, and the smoke arrived after and signed for work it never carried.

You may tell me here that the morning cough proves little and that many throats catch in cold air, and I hear the fairness in that answer and I do not dismiss it. Cold air does catch many throats, and stairs do test many legs. Yet you watched the difference in your own house across weeks when smoking paused for illness or travel, and breath came easier by the second day without any new training. You watched children run the same stairs two at a time with nothing carried and never pause to settle nerves. The tightness wore the clothes of weather and age to get itself fed, and the clean descent now shows the stitching.

So the stairs never asked for smoke. The lungs asked for air, and the legs asked for movement, and the morning lent both without asking a price.

The verdict lands short, as true verdicts do. The climb was yours. The cough was the toll.

THE CUP TASTED TO THE END

You know the kitchen cup because your tongue has met it beside smoke for so long that the two tastes married in memory, and you can watch the marriage dissolve without losing either party.

The kettle boiled and the coffee stood steaming on the counter, dark and hot with its bitter edge rising, and the paper was lit before the first sip had cooled enough to taste. The first draw rose with the first sip and the two met on the tongue, and the shoulders dropped as the warmth went down. You took the coffee to need its partner to be complete, and you read the morning as richer for the plume above the cup. You carried both to the table and drank and drew together until the cup cooled and the mouth coated, and you called the whole pairing comfort.

We all drank that wedding for years, and I lay it here gently because the cup itself deserves better than to be used as evidence against itself. We praised the heat while dulling the tongue that received it. We praised the aroma while filling the nose with a stronger smoke that covered it. We left the last third of every cup to cool beside a full ashtray, and we remembered the cup as rich while tasting less of it with every year. The cup stayed faithful through all of it, and lent its warmth to cover a transaction it never made.

Take the same cup at the same counter with the same morning light across the lino, and take only the paper out of the picture, because your own tongue will settle what argument cannot.

You pour and the steam rises clean with nothing crossing it, and the first sip meets a mouth rinsed and ready. The bitterness lands true and then softens, and the heat spreads without any coating over it. You taste the middle of the cup as fully as the start, and you taste the end without any sour turn from ash. You set the empty cup down and the tongue stays clean for what follows, for toast and fruit and the cold air at the door. Nothing in the hour leans toward the step outside, because nothing in the cup asked for company of that kind.

Did the cup grow deeper because ash fell beside it. Did the aroma grow fuller through a nose filled with smoke, or through a nose open to the steam alone. Did the morning grow kinder because the tongue coated early, or because the tongue stayed clean to the end. You know nothing in the cup moved except the gap since the last feed, closed for minutes and already counting upward again. The heat warmed through its own nature. The bitter pleased through its own edge. The quiet held through tasks ending and light widening. What moved was the thin stir, dressed in coffee clothes to get itself fed.

Think of tables you have truly owned, and watch how differently they hold in memory. You love the meal closed with the taste staying bright to the last bite. You love the kitchen cleared with the window open and no cloud hanging under the lamp. You love the cup finished and the mouth fresh for talk after. True savour never needs hourly feeding to remain, and never washes off with soap after, and never airs off the coat before company. What must be fed to stay is not savour. It is a hold wearing savour clothes.

You may answer me here with the strongest objection taste can raise on this ground, and I want it spoken plain between us. You say coffee and smoke belong together by nature the way bread belongs with butter, and that taking one without the other leaves the hour thin. I hear the honesty in that answer and I do not mock it, because years of always together can feel like nature. Yet you drank coffee clean in rooms where lighting was barred, in hospitals and trains and long morning meetings, and the coffee did not fail there. You saw company drink to the last sip at tables where no paper burned, and praise the brew to the host without any sense of lack. If smoke completed coffee the way milk completes it, the room would divide clean between those who lit and savoured and those who went without and found all flat. You never saw that division. You saw feeders leave the table to feed and return with dulled tongues, and company stay and taste to the end.

I speak of food and drink here only as themselves, and I lay no new rule about what to hold in the hand. No cup is assigned as a prop to carry, and no bite is set as a price for the hour. The cup does not carry you through, and the hour does not need carrying. The tongue clears because feeding stopped, not because another flavour covered it. When that difference settles, the old wedding loses its command. They met because we always met them together. They never belonged by nature.

Here is the inversion the whole table turns on, stated flat because it is settled. The cup gave the warmth. The dose took the thanks.

THE SHORT WALK AND THE BENCH IN SUN

You know the short walk because your feet have measured it in smokes for years, and you can walk it clean and feel the measure change without losing the ground.

The street to the shop runs seven minutes each way with a low wall and a corner tree and a bench where the sun lands mid morning. You walked it with a lit end cupped against the wind, and you counted the way in draws, lighting at the gate and tapping out near the shutter. The air moved cool on the face and the legs loosened with use, and the mind unknotted from the desk. You took the walk to need its companion to be restful, and you read the pause on the bench as complete only with its glow. You came home with the paper finished and the hands smelling of it, and you washed before touching the cupboard handles.

We all walked that measure for years, and I walk it with you now in memory without any blame for the counting. We learned which gate shelters a light and which corner turns the wind. We learned how many draws fit a seven minute errand and how to time the butt for the bin by the shop. We believed the legs moved easier for the company, and believed the mind cleared faster for the plume. The legs moved because legs move, and the mind cleared because strain stopped and air moved and eyes had somewhere kind to land.

Walk the same street at the same hour with your hands free, and let each step teach in its own order.

You leave the gate with the hands in pockets and the face open to the air, and the first hundred paces loosen the back without any signal from the tip. You pass the low wall and hear a radio from an open window and smell cut grass from a small front plot. You reach the bench where the sun lands and you sit for three minutes with nothing to tend, and the breath slows because sitting slows it. You watch the clouds gather over the roofs and watch faces pass with bags and dogs, and the minutes pass whole without any counting of draws to the butt. You rise when ready and walk home with the legs warm and the head clear, and the door opens on the same kitchen with the same light, held steady without any seal of ash.

Did the street grow brighter because smoke rose above it. Did the legs grow looser through a chest fed on schedule, or through a body moving in clean air. Did the bench grow kinder because the tip glowed beside it, or because the sun landed and the breath slowed. You already know the true answers when both questions stand together. The walk restored through movement and air and the kind landing of eyes. The pause pleased through rest itself. The paper only arrived at the same hour and claimed the room, the way a stranger steps into your photograph at the viewpoint and is later credited with the view.

You remember the recliner and the ashtray and the lighter waiting in their places on the patio, and you remember the carton kept full in the drawer, and you see them now without the old pull, because the night music played from the speaker and not from the tip. You remember days when teaching a class and finishing a paragraph and driving to the store each closed with a light, and you see those closings now as finishes that glowed on their own, because the work lent the glow and the pause lent the rest. Neither memory needs chasing away, and neither needs obeying. They sit where old timetables sit when the trains no longer run.

You may tell me here that empty minutes on a bench feel edgy and crawl, and that feeling itself proves the pause needs its filler, and I want that thought heard fully before it falls. Look at your own edginess and see training wearing nature clothes. The edginess marks the interval since feeding, not the nature of sitting still. Children sit on the same wall with free hands and find whole worlds in gutters and signs. Old men sit on the same bench with clean breath and watch the street to its end. The stir comes in a light wave and then passes, and comes again lighter, and the bench stays kind through all of it. It never filled your pause. It filled its own lack and called the closing a filling, and the pause stayed whole underneath throughout.

So the walk never needed paper. The legs taught. The air cleared. Distance ordered what the desk had tangled.

The verdict lands short, as true verdicts do. The pause held you. The dose only timed it.

THE ORDINARY HOURS ARE FAVOURITE

You came to this morning knowing each old gift was empty, and fearing the hours themselves would feel thin once the lights went out of them. That fear deserves an honest hearing before it leaves, because it is the last edge the old story holds.

“But ordinary hours feel thin with nothing in hand, do they not. The morning coffee tastes flat without its partner. The break drags without its marker. Something is missing, and missing will always ache.”

I greet that voice kindly before answering it, because I spoke in it for years and I know how reasonable it sounds from the inside. The voice mistakes the stir for the hour, and mistakes the closing of the stir for the fullness of the hour. It calls the quiet after feeding proof that feeding fills, and calls the edginess before feeding proof that the hour is thin. It has the picture back to front, and the morning just lived shows the right way round.

Hold the fear where it belongs and its object stands clear in daylight.

Think of the bed left calm without reaching, and know whose work the calm crowns. Think of the stairs taken without cough, and see whose legs carried the climb. Think of the cup finished clean on a clean tongue, and credit whose heat warmed the mouth. Think of the walk taken and the bench sat in sun with nothing tended, and name whose rest restored the mind. Each hour kept whole returns its thanks to its owners, to breath and taste and movement and rest. None returns thanks to burning paper, because none ever borrowed from it.

Ask the whole morning its own plain questions, and listen for the only honest answers. Did any hour this day move because paper burned beside it. Did any breath come deeper through smoke than through air. Did any taste grow richer through coating than through cleanness. You know the day stayed complete before any lighting, and you know the lights only ever closed a lack they had opened. The morning did not thin when feeding stopped. The lack thinned, and the morning showed through.

I speak to you now as one who has stood where you stand, between reasons cleared and hours not yet trusted, and I tell you what your own feet have already begun to learn. The body returns to its own measure without any new system to learn and without any new prop to carry. The stir that visits in the early days comes and goes like weather over roofs, and does not climb forever, and does not prove the hour needs its old seal. You meet it by living the hour straight through, by washing and climbing and tasting and walking with attention on the doing itself. Your part is to teach the class and taste its closing clean, to write the paragraph and hear it land, to drive the errand and carry it home whole, to close the meal with the taste staying bright, to take the deep breath at the kerb and know whose work it crowns. The hour kept whole is proof spoken without a word.

Watch one near week with these eyes and feel the difference settle before any new doctrine arrives. You wake and the hand stays empty and the breath comes clean. You climb and the chest stays quiet. You pour and the cup holds to the end. You walk and the bench holds without counting. You work and the finish crowns itself. You sit at dusk with the table cleared and the air clear under the lamp, and the day closes without any small ceremony of butt and wash. Nothing in you leans to the step. That week is not thin. It is whole, and growing dearer as the coating leaves the tongue and the stairs lose their toll.

Here is the verdict the whole morning turns on, stated flat because it is settled. Ordinary hours were yours all along.

SUMMARY

- The bed waking stayed whole with empty hands, and breath came cleaner without any starter before it.
- The stairs were climbed on legs and air, while the cough belonged to the toll and not to weather or age.
- The cup pleased through its own heat and edge on a clean tongue, and no drink needs a lit partner by nature.
- The short walk restored through legs moving in clean air and kind sights, while the pause pleased through rest itself.
- The thin stir comes and goes and does not own the hour, and hours lived straight through prove sufficiency.
- Ordinary moments return their thanks to breath and taste and movement and rest, and the day holds favourite with nothing lit.
