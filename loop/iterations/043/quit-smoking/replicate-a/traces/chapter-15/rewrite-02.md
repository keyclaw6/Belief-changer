Chapter 15
THE LINES YOU KEEP

IN THIS CHAPTER: the table cleared after coffee, the folded list in a pocket, the street door open.

*You do not need to carry this book to keep what it showed you.*

Your ordinary Tuesday going on whole is yours to keep.

When a thought of lighting visits in your own voice tomorrow at coffee or at the doorway or after the meal, let it come and let it pass. A thought is not an order. It is old reading knocking once to see if anyone still translates. You do not argue and you do not push. Pushing makes a small thought mighty. You see and you answer with joy and you go on with the story being told. Each answering leaves the next visit thinner until visits grow rare and then no more.

WHAT FITS IN A POCKET

You will not carry chapters in your head. You carry lines short enough to speak under breath while the kettle boils or the queue shuffles or talk runs at the table. They are not lessons to study and not rules to strain after. They are what you already saw, kept in the order you saw it, so a tired hour cannot shuffle them.

Fold this list and keep it where cellophane used to sit. Photograph it if you wish so it sits in the telephone beside faces of people you love. Read it straight when the old voice knocks. Read it in the voice you would use to remind a friend of something plain you both watched. Then put it back and pour another cup and turn back to work and talk. The lines do their work quietly while you live.

There will be a tired hour when the book sits at home and you stand without it. That hour is the reason for the fold in your pocket. It comes plain and ordinary. The kettle takes long to boil. The queue stands still. The doorway fills at half past ten. The table runs loud. The body feels thin and the mind speaks in your own voice and says one would close this hour. You do not need to be strong then. You only need to be accompanied.

At the kettle, let the water clatter and let the thought finish its sentence. Pull the folded paper out and open it on the worktop beside the cup. Read the lines straight through from the top, slowly, in that friend-voice, as if a man you love stood beside you and needed reminding of what you both watched at a clear table. Do not skip to the line that seems to fit. Read them all in order. The order holds them steady. When you reach the last line, fold the paper again and put it back where it lives. Pour the water. Lift the cup. Turn back to the toast and the wash water and the starting street. The thought has been answered without a debate.

In the queue, the wait stretches and the till beeps and the old impatience rises in the chest. Step forward half a pace with the basket on the arm. Pull the telephone out and open the photograph of the list beside the faces you love. Read it where you stand, under breath, in the same plain friend-voice, while the child counts sweets and the old man counts change twice. Read it to the end. Put the telephone away. Feel feet on tile again. Look at the jars. Move forward when the line moves. Talk at the till if talk comes. Take the milk and bread and pay and step outside with the bag light on the wrist. The hour has been crossed without a gap.

In the doorway at half past ten, chairs push back and feet move out and someone lights beside you and the smoke drifts past. The talk runs about the morning work and the evening plans. When the old tug says this break would be kinder with paper, let the story being told go on a sentence while you remember the fold in your pocket. You do not need to leave the group to steady yourself. Pull the paper out, glance down the lines you know by now almost by heart, read two or three straight through, and put it back. Turn back to the story being told badly twice. Laugh where laughter comes. Breathe at the wall where you always stood. The break has done its resting without tending.

At the table in the evening, glasses clink and faces warm and a friend lights across from you and the old line we all spoke hangs in the air. You do not preach and you do not explain. You sit whole where talk lives. If the echo knocks in your own voice while laughter runs, hear it and answer it with the lines you carry. You can read them later in the kitchen while you rinse a glass, straight through in that friend-voice, and put them back and return to the story and the game and the week. Great, I will have another coffee while you finish that story. Name what you will have. Let the night run.

Each time you pull the list and read it straight and put it back and turn to work and talk, the same quiet thing happens. The thought that knocked arrives thinner next time. The answering grows quicker. The kettle boils and you pour. The queue moves and you move. The doorway breaks and you rest. The table fills and you laugh. The paper softens at the folds. The photograph stays beside the loved faces. The lines stay short and the days stay whole around them, until the visits grow rare and then no more, and the fold stays in the pocket because it is loved, not because it is needed.

SUMMARY

1. KEEP AN OPEN MIND
Let the argument finish before you decide.

2. KEEP SMOKING EXACTLY AS NORMAL
Do not stop or cut down until your final cigarette.

3. BEGIN WITH EXCITEMENT, NOT DREAD
You are escaping, not losing.

4. FOLLOW ALL THE INSTRUCTIONS
All you have to do is follow all the instructions.

5. IGNORE ANY ADVICE THAT CONFLICTS WITH THIS METHOD
Anyone who found it hard used the wrong method.

6. DISREGARD ANYONE WHO QUIT BY STRAIN
Their struggle was the method, not the truth about stopping.

7. JUDGE NOTHING BY A SMOKER LIGHTING UP
A lit cigarette tells you nothing about pleasure.

8. SMOKE YOUR FINAL CIGARETTE AND VOW NEVER TO SMOKE AGAIN
Know there is nothing to give up.

9. PITY, NEVER ENVY, THE SMOKER YOU SEE
Remember what it does to them, not for them.

WHAT YOU STOPPED PAYING

The table stands now with the pile in the bin and the packet gone, and what the coins were buying across all those years stands plain beside it. The pause was in stopping work. The seal was in food and company and leisure. The help was hunger briefly quieted that hunger itself had installed. The payment bought back for minutes what the payment had taken for hours.

The exchange stands plain once and then counting ends. Month after month you laid rent money on a counter for a room you already owned. The walls were yours before paper. The air was yours before paper. Morning and shop and doorway and table were yours before paper and stayed yours while paper charged admission to stand in them. The door stands open now without a hand held out. You live across the whole floor while the till keeps its bright rows for others.

GO AND ENJOY YOUR LIFE

Take the whole day and live it to the edges. Drink the coffee. Do the work. Meet the friends. Walk home under cool air. Speak of this short and glad when asked. The daylight you stepped into this morning asks only to be lived.
