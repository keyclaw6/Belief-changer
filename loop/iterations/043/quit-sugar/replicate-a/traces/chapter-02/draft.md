Chapter 2
WHO IS EATING WHOM

It is two o'clock in the afternoon and the breakroom smells of warm plastic and cold coffee.

Someone left cake on the table by the kettle. A square of sponge with white icing, half covered by clear plastic that has fogged a little from the heat. The plate beneath it is smeared. Crumbs lead off the edge. A plastic fork lies beside it with icing on the tines. No one brought a plate for themselves. No one will admit to wanting it. And yet every few minutes the door swings, someone comes in for water or for air, and a hand goes out.

You know that hand. You have watched it from inside your own sleeve.

You walk in tired after lunch. Your stomach is full. You tell yourself you want nothing. You fill the kettle. You wait. Your eyes rest on the cake because there is nowhere else to rest them. You lift the corner of the plastic. You tell yourself you are only looking. Then your fingers break a corner off, small, hardly worth mentioning, and you eat it standing up. It is a little dry at the edge and very sweet in the middle. You chew and swallow and put the plastic back as you found it. You rinse your cup. You walk back to your desk. Ten minutes later you are thinking about the rest of it. Not with hunger. With a thin attention that keeps sliding back to the table by the kettle, to the exact shape under the plastic, to how much might be left and whether someone else has taken it.

I have stood in that exact spot a hundred times. I know the small mathematics that runs without permission. If I take another sliver now, no one will notice. If I wait until the kitchen empties, I can take a proper piece. If I leave it, someone else gets it, and then it is gone, and then I missed it. None of those sentences feels like a decision. They feel like weather passing through. And still the feet turn back.

*You never needed a book to enjoy something — the fact you are holding one proves something else has been steering your hand.*

I want you to look at that moment honestly, because your freedom lives inside it. Not in the mornings we spoke of. Not in the mirror. In that small reach that felt like nothing.

You will say you chose it. Everyone says that. I said it for years. I chose the biscuit with tea. I chose the bar at the station. I chose the spoon from the tub while the dinner cooked. Choosing was my story and I held it tight, because the alternative felt insulting. Who wants to be told they are not in charge of their own hand.

I am telling you now, gently and without a flicker of blame, that the hand was not following your orders. We were conned, you and I, and the con runs on making us defend it as choice.

Think of what choosing really looks like. When you choose a coat, you weigh and compare. You look at the price. You try it on. You walk away and come back. When you choose a holiday, you talk it over. You picture the beach and the flight and the money. Choosing is slow and open and yours. Did the breakroom look like that. Did your hand weigh and compare. Did you picture anything. Or did you reach while another part of you watched and said, I thought I did not want this.

Ask yourself, as an investigator would, not as a defendant. If you truly choose it freely, why do you eat any of it standing up, quickly, before anyone comes in. If you truly like it so much, why does the liking need hiding. And if you could stop whenever you wanted, why do you hold a book about stopping.

There is only one honest answer. Liking never needed help. What needs a book is not liking. It is steering.

The Cake Nobody Wanted

Stay in that breakroom with me a little longer, because it teaches what lectures never teach.

Watch the queue. Half past two. A woman comes in for tea and peels the plastic back and takes a sliver and says, I should not, and laughs. A man comes in for a glass of water and takes a square with his fingers and eats it over the bin. Someone else cuts a proper slice and carries it back to the desk on a paper towel, then leaves half of it beside the keyboard. By four the plate holds only smears and one dry corner. No one enjoyed a cake together. No one sat down and said, this is delicious, let us have some more. The cake disappeared the way a puddle disappears in sun. No one chose. Everyone reached.

We all know that disappearing. We have watched packets empty in our own kitchens the same way. The biscuits bought for visitors. The chocolates bought for the children. The tub bought to last the week. We open with ceremony. We close with crumbs. Between those two moments there is a stretch where we are not tasting at all, only repeating the reach. The same hand opens the cupboard at nine in the evening while the news plays. The same hand smooths the surface of the tub after, so the scoop does not show. Bare feet on cold tiles. Cupboard glow on the face. The quiet closing of the door so no one wakes. Then the standing eat, quickly, by the open fridge, and the burying of the wrapper deep under peelings. Warmth toward that figure, always. I have been that figure. You have been that figure. We deserve tenderness, not exposure. I describe it only so you recognise the reach knows your address.

Tell me, when did you decide to finish it. Name the minute. You cannot, because there was no minute. The first bite had a taste. The tenth had only motion. The hand went on after the mouth stopped caring. Is that what you call liking something. Is that what you call choice.

You will say, but that is the office, that is other people, that is boredom. At home I am in control. I want to meet that voice with respect, because it protected you for years. It kept you feeling like a competent adult in a world that calls sweet eaters weak. Hold its hand while I show you what it guards.

I Don't Keep It in the House

I don't keep it in the house. I'm fine, really.

I hear that sentence with love, because I said it myself with such pride. The cupboards held rice and beans and good bread. The fridge held vegetables and plain yoghurt and fruit. No bars. No tubs. No cans. I told friends I simply did not buy it, and they nodded, and I felt clean and sensible and a little superior. Then I want you to follow me through one ordinary day of that clean life.

Morning at home, nothing sweet, coffee plain, fruit with breakfast, virtuous to the last spoon. Mid-morning at work, someone's birthday, cake under plastic, a sliver, only politeness. Lunch, a proper meal, nothing wrong, then something small from the shop with coffee, only to get through the afternoon. Afternoon, the drawer, one bar, only today, only because the morning was hard. Evening, tired, the station kiosk, something for the train, only for the journey. Night, home again to the clean cupboards, a spoon of honey in tea, only honey, only small. Count the day with me. Seven small onlys. Seven doses, hits, shots of sweetness, none of them bought for the house, none of them counted in the house rule. The house stayed pure. The mouth did not.

Add the week around that day. Monday the polite sliver. Tuesday the meeting plate passed hand to hand and refusal felt rude. Wednesday the till bar while the queue waited behind. Thursday the child's leftover eaten over the sink so it would not go to waste. Friday the petrol station packet on the long drive. Each one too small to mention. Each one dressed as circumstance. Together they are a river. The rule measured the cart. The river ran around it.

Do you see. The control was located in the shopping cart, and the loop stayed intact everywhere else. We moved the trap from the shelf to the street and called the move freedom. The breakroom, the kiosk, the petrol station, the desk drawer, the meeting plate, the child's leftover, the taste while cooking, the tiny pay at the till that never reaches the receipt — the trap does not need your cupboard when it owns the whole town.

Ask yourself two plain questions. If the house rule worked, why does the rule need remaking every Monday. And if you are fine, really, why does fine need announcing.

The cart never governed the craving. It only governed where the craving was fed. A reader who prides herself on an empty cupboard and then eats cake standing in a cold kitchen at midnight has not proved control. She has proved the reach follows her out of the house. That is not a flaw in her. It is the signature of the con. The con removes choice and then sells you a small choice of location to make you feel in charge.

I am not asking you to stock the house or to empty it. Leave your shelves exactly as they are. I am asking you to stop grading yourself on the shelf and to start watching the hand. The hand tells the truth the shelf hides. The hand reached at two o'clock on a full stomach for cake no one wanted. The shelf had nothing to do with it.

Just One Until the Box Is Empty

There is a second sentence that sounds like control and acts like a leash. We all spoke it.

Just one a day. One sweet thing a day. Only at weekends. Only on weekdays. I don't overdo it.

Listen to how reasonable it sounds. One small pleasure, measured, adult, civilised. Now watch what one does in real life. You buy the box to last the week. You take one on Monday and close the packet with care. You take one on Tuesday and fold the packet a little less carefully. On Wednesday you take one after lunch and one with evening tea, because Wednesday was long, and the box is still nearly full, and one extra changes nothing. On Thursday you take two together to get it over with. On Friday the last three go in one evening in front of something glowing, hardly tasted. On Saturday you buy no more, proud and cross at once, and on Sunday you borrow from the children's stash. Monday you start again with a fresh box and a fresh rule.

Is that memory yours. It was mine, detail for detail, and it was the memory of everyone I have ever helped. The rule governs until the box is empty. Always. Without exception. The rule never governed the box. The box governed the rule.

Do not blame yourself for that slide. Blame the mechanism honestly seen. The first dose re-arms the wanting. The wanting negotiates the second. The second silences the negotiation. By evening the rule sounds like someone else's voice, faint and priggish, easy to dismiss. You were not weak on Friday night. You were dosed on Monday morning. The Monday dose wrote the Friday night. I know how the Friday night sounds from the inside, because I lived it. The packet open on the lap. The first two eaten slowly, almost with attention. The next three eaten mechanically, eyes elsewhere, jaw working while the mind scrolls. Then the sudden wakefulness at the bottom of the box, the rustle shaken to check, the stare at nothing, the hot rush of self-reproach. That reproach never belonged to you. It belonged to the loop talking in your voice. The loop runs past liking and then charges you for the running.

Ask yourself, when did a rule about apples ever behave like that. When did you eat six apples standing up because the first apple unhinged you. When did bread whisper from the bin at midnight. Real food never negotiates. Real food is eaten and done. Only BAD SUGAR makes rules, and then makes sure the rules fail, and then lets you call the failure yours.

That is why every moderation scheme ends the same way. Not because you lack character. You do not lack character. You lack an opponent you can see. The rule fights the packet. The packet is only paper. The fight is elsewhere.

Who Is in Charge

If you wanted to quit a hobby you enjoy, would you need a book to stop it.

Read that again slowly, because it settles the whole matter. People do not buy books to stop eating cucumbers. They do not join groups to stop walking in woods. They do not hide apple cores under rubbish. The mere fact this book is in your hands, the mere fact your eyes reached this page, proves the choice left the room some time ago. A free liking needs no rescue. A steered hand does.

We were brainwashed, you and I, and I use that word carefully and without drama. From childhood we watched adults we trusted unwrap and pour and smile. We learned before we could spell that sweetness means love and celebration and rest and reward. Then the wanting machinery did the rest. The brain keeps a great loud system for wanting and a small quiet one for liking, and the loud one learns fast. A taste followed by a rush teaches the loud system to shout do that again, even when the quiet one reports the last bite was sickly and the stomach is full. The shout grows stronger each time it is answered and fades only when it is left unanswered. Answer the cue and you feed it. Leave it and it quiets. That shout is not pleasure. It is instruction. It says reach, not enjoy. We mistook the shout for our own voice. I mistook it for years, and every time I obeyed it I taught it to shout louder next time, until an ordinary afternoon without a packet felt oddly empty, not because I needed anything, but because the shout had become the wallpaper of my days.

Have you lived this split. The last bite of the bar did not even taste good, dry and too sweet at once, and still the fingers searched the wrapper for crumbs. The can was warm by the end and you drank it anyway. The cake at two was stale at the edge and you ate the edge too. When liking ended, wanting walked on. That gap between liking and wanting is the whole trap in one picture. You did not enjoy the last bite and still wanted it. What does that tell you about who was eating whom.

There is a picture I want you to keep, because it names what you saw today. Call it the Sugar Trap. Not as a lesson. As a name for what you saw today. A designed enclosure with sweet bait at the centre. You walked in years ago on false information, the way we all did, told it was a treat and a lift and a comfort. The door swung behind you without a sound. Now the reaching happens inside walls you cannot see, and you call the walls yourself.

Inside that enclosure live two small residents. One is a trivial physical craver, the Sugar Mite, a tiny restless tug that complains when unfed. The other is the belief-system that feeds it, the Treat Spell, the old voice that dresses every tug as need and every packet as reward. You do not need to study them. You only need their names, so that next time the hand moves you can see clearly what moved it. Not you. The tug and the story about the tug. The Mite and the Spell. The Trap talking through your sleeve.

Do you feel how blame leaves the room when the picture enters. You were not foolish. You were conned. A con does not work on the stupid. It works on the trusting, the busy, the tired, the kind. It worked on me. It worked on millions of clever, hard-working people who run companies and raise children and mend roofs. I think of a friend who negotiates contracts all day without blinking and then stands in a garage forecourt at midnight arguing with a vending machine. I think of a nurse who holds steady through night shifts and then eats biscuits in her car before going indoors. Sharp minds. Steady hands. Same reach. Being conned never proved weakness. It proved exposure. And a con, once seen, loses its power without a fight. You do not wrestle a mirage. You see the sand.

That is the belief we change in this chapter and no other. You came in believing you could stop because you simply like it. You leave knowing liking never needed a book. The book in your hands is the proof someone else has been steering.

The Body Already Knows

There is a second witness I want to call, quieter than the breakroom and older than any packet. Your body.

Watch yourself eat a real meal when BAD SUGAR is nowhere near it. Not a feast. A Tuesday table. Something warm in a bowl. Bread. Vegetables soft from the pot. Perhaps meat or beans. Perhaps cheese. You sit because you are hungry. That hunger arrived as a clean hollow, a simple message, easy to read. You lift the spoon. The first mouthfuls taste of everything. Steam on the face. Salt and warmth spreading. You chew without hurry. Halfway through the hollow fills and the taste changes, still good but quieter, asking less. Near the end you put the spoon down between mouthfuls. You finish and sit a moment with your hands around the cup. Satisfied is not a word then. It is a state in the limbs.

When did that meal negotiate. When did it shout from the cupboard at midnight. When did one bowl demand another and another until the pot was scraped standing up. Never. Real food is eaten and done because the body governs it. Hunger opens the meal. Satisfaction closes it. The signals are old and exact and they work without lessons.

Now set beside that table the breakroom cake. No hollow before it. No satisfaction after it. A full stomach at two o'clock and a hand reaching anyway. A dry mouth after and a louder want an hour later. The difference is not character. It is authorship. One meal was ordered by hunger. The other was ordered by the Trap.

Your body already knows the portion. It knew before any packet taught it otherwise. It knows heat and fullness and enough. It knows the clean hollow and the quiet close. Hunger is not an emergency. It is an invitation. Satisfaction is not a struggle. It is a landing. You do not need me to teach your stomach its work. You need me to show you what interrupted it. Watch hunger arrive on a morning you slept well. It comes as lightness with an edge, a clear call, not a panic. You eat. The edge softens. Strength returns without fanfare. No drama. No bargaining. Compare that clean sequence with two o'clock at the breakroom, full stomach, dry mouth, hand moving. One sequence begins in the belly and ends in the limbs. The other begins in the eyes and ends in the jaw. Learn to tell them apart and the con loses its costume.

Think of a child offered plain food when truly hungry. She eats with her whole face. Rice on the cheek. Eyes bright. Then she turns away mid-spoon, mouth closed, head shaking, already running back to play. No counting. No remorse. No negotiation with the bowl. The body spoke and she obeyed without knowing she obeyed. We all ate like that once. The machinery still sits inside you, intact beneath years of shouting. It waits for quiet the way a garden waits under snow.

I am not asking you to diet or to measure or to earn your food. Eat when you are hungry and stop when you are satisfied — take those words as a standing kindness, not as a rule to strain after. For now only notice that the kindness is possible. Notice that bread never called you back. Notice that soup never whispered. Notice that only BAD SUGAR raises its voice after dark, and that the voice you called yours was the Spell talking through a tiny tug.

Hold that table beside the breakroom and the picture completes itself. Two hungers. One clean and closable. One loud and unclosable. One belongs to you. One belongs to the enclosure. A reader who sees both can never again confuse them. The confusion was the con. The seeing is the start of escape. Keep both pictures with you in the days ahead. When the hand moves at two o'clock, ask which hunger ordered it. When the evening cupboard glows, ask which hunger stands before it. When the till bar gleams, ask which hunger pays. You already know the answers. You knew them before this page. I only gave you words for the knowing.

Do not change anything yet. I mean that exactly as I say it. Do not clear the drawer. Do not swear off the cake. Do not eat less tomorrow to prove you understood today. The belief must change first, and belief changes by seeing, not by straining. Keep eating as you ate. Keep reaching as you reached. Only watch the reach with new eyes. Watch whose orders it follows. Watch how the rule slides. Watch how the house stays clean while the mouth travels. Watch how the last bite disappoints and the hand goes on. Each watching loosens one bar. The door has been open all along. You only needed to see the walls.

I make you one request in this chapter, and I make it with a smile and with total seriousness. I ask it now so the rest can do its work.

2. DON'T STOP OR CUT DOWN YET.
The belief must change first.

Keep that agreement with me. Bring your doubt and your raised eyebrow and your full sweet life exactly as it is. Argue on the page. Test every sentence against your own afternoons. Only do not try to prove freedom by force before you see the enclosure. A reader who stops from fear or from grit still believes in the treat and merely flees it. A reader who sees has nothing to flee. The seeing does the walking.

SUMMARY

- Liking never needed a book, the book in your hands proves the reach was steered, not chosen.
- A clean cupboard never stopped the loop, the trap feeds everywhere the hand travels.
- One always became the box, the rule governed until the packet emptied, never the other way round.
- Wanting outlived liking by hours, the shout to reach was instruction, not pleasure.
- Hunger opens and satisfaction closes a real meal, the body already knows its work beneath the noise.
- The enclosure has a name and two small residents, and seeing them removes blame without removing truth.
