Chapter 6
THE MOUTH AND THE BODY

IN THIS CHAPTER: the kitchen table at six with an empty stomach, one biscuit eaten slowly, the pack that starts heavenly and ends mechanical, and the body that never called for sweets.

*The mouth stopped enjoying long before the hand stopped, and hunger never called for sweets.*

ONE BISCUIT SLOWLY

Supper is an hour away and the stomach sits empty.

You have not eaten since lunch. The afternoon has taken its cut. The kitchen holds the plain smell of cooking from next door and the table holds a pack of biscuits you bought for the week. The foil crackles. The top biscuit shows its pattern and its sugar shine. You lift one. You tell yourself you will have one while the pot heats because the body asks and the mouth knows.

We have all stood at that table. I stood there for years with the same pack and the same story about listening to the body.

Do this whole and watch what the body actually says.

Sit down. Eat nothing else first. Take one biscuit and eat it slowly against that empty stomach. Notice the first bite. The snap gives way. Salt and fat and sugar arrive together. The mouth wakes. The second bite still carries. The third still holds shape. Chew to the end. Put the pack down. Wait a minute with the empty stomach underneath.

Ask what that one biscuit did.

Did it fill the empty place? Or did it tease the empty place and sharpen it? Did the stomach settle the way it settles after bread and cheese and soup? Or did the mouth light up while the stomach stayed exactly as empty and now louder? Did you finish the biscuit and forget the pack till supper? Or did you finish the biscuit and picture the second before you had swallowed the first?

There is only one honest reading and you already know it from a hundred evenings. The biscuit did not answer hunger. It woke the mouth and left hunger standing beside it, talking louder.

Hunger is a hollow ache low down that any plain meal settles. What you feel after one biscuit is not that hollow gone. It is a bright pinch up in the mouth and the thoughts, a pull toward the pack. We learned to call both by one name. We called the mouth-pull hunger because everyone around us called it hunger and because the pack taught us to answer every pinch with sugar. The body never taught that lesson. The body asks for food and stops asking when food arrives. The pack asks for another pack and never stops asking while packs remain.

Keep the biscuit on the tongue a moment longer in memory because the whole taste story stands in it. Real hunger grows slowly and waits for supper and takes bread gladly. The biscuit-hunger arrives sudden and names a brand and will not take bread. Real hunger ends with a meal and leaves the mind quiet for hours. The biscuit-hunger ends with the pack empty and leaves the mind busy for hours. One is the body speaking. The other is the loop speaking through the mouth and borrowing the body's voice.

The fact is one biscuit against real hunger proves the split. The mouth sang. The stomach stayed empty. The hand wanted more before the stomach had received anything it could use.

WHEN HEAVEN TURNS MECHANICAL

No one denies the first bites sing. I do not deny it. The first bites sing because warmth and fat and sugar and crunch arrive together after hours without, and the mouth was built to notice.

Stay with the pack past those bites because the pack tells the truth about taste if you stay to the end.

Open the pack you meant to keep for the week. Eat the first biscuit slowly. Heavenly. Eat the second. Good, still bright, already less startling. Eat the third while the kettle boils. Pleasant, familiar, the edge gone. Eat the fourth standing up. Eat the fifth while you scroll the messages. By the sixth you no longer taste shape, only sweet and chew. By the eighth you lick crumbs from the foil and read the pack for what you just ate, as if through a haze, and wonder where the heavenly part went.

We all know that slide. I lived it with bars and rolls and cereal eaten dry from the box. The beginning belongs to taste. The end belongs to motion.

Ask of that slide what any fair judge would ask of any pleasure.

If the pack tastes heavenly, why does heavenly last three biscuits and not thirty? If the tenth bite carries the same pleasure as the first, why do you eat the tenth faster and with less attention and with no more delight? Did you finish the pack and sit back fuller and calmer the way you sit back after a meal you liked? Or did you finish the pack and feel coated and thirsty and vaguely cheated, already replaying the first bites as if they belonged to a different food?

You know the answer because you have lived it in every room where BAD SUGAR sits. The birthday chocolates no one counts. The office tin passed hand to hand. The late film with the bag on the lap. The first handful stops talk. The last handful fills silence while attention has already left. The hand keeps travelling after the mouth has clocked off.

Think of the evenings that prove it without argument. The soup you ate hungry to the last spoon and put down satisfied. The bread you tore and chewed slowly and stopped when enough had arrived. Plain meals hold their taste from start to finish and then finish. BAD SUGAR opens loud and thins fast and never finishes till the container stands empty. That thinning is not your fault and not your lack of appreciation. That thinning is the design showing.

The mouth was never built to call a whole pack heavenly. It calls the first contrast heavenly and then tells the truth by going dull. The hand that keeps going after the dullness is not following taste. It follows something else that outlives taste.

IT TASTES TOO GOOD TO REFUSE

You say it tastes too good to refuse. I hear you because we all said it in the same tone we use for music and for people we love.

You say Dessert is the best part of every meal. You say the sweet at the end makes the meal and without it the table feels unfinished. You say It tastes too good to refuse and you mean the tongue has voted and the vote is final.

We took it for a genuine pleasure or treat because the first bites sang and because everyone at the table sang with them.

Ask what the vote was actually about.

Was it the taste of BAD SUGAR itself, or was it the contrast after salt and the pause and the company and the hour? Take the same dessert alone at the sink at midnight, tired and already full, with no talk and no music. Does it sing the same? Take the same cake after three biscuits and a fizzy drink, when the mouth already swims. Does it still lift? You know it does not. The same spoon that sang at eight lies flat at midnight. Taste did not change its chemistry between those hours. What changed was the mouth around it and the hour around the mouth.

Think how strangely narrow the too-good list is. When people are asked which foods they lose control over and rank them, the same names crowd the top every time. They say they lose control over fizzy drinks and chocolate and biscuits and pastries. They say they keep wanting sweetened cereals and flavoured yoghurts and ice cream even after the mouth stopped liking them. They say they want the rolls that vanish to nothing in the mouth and they keep reaching after liking has thinned. The foods that rise are the processed ones built from sugar with fat, tuned sweet and soft to hit fast. At the bottom sit apples and strawberries and broccoli and plain staple foods eaten as food. No one writes that they emptied a bowl of apples till they felt sick and hid the cores. No one says the strawberry kept them up picturing more.

The fact is it was never the strawberry. The fact is the trouble concentrates where sugar rides with fat and fast melt, the very foods the loop feeds on.

That ranking matters because it breaks the taste story open. If taste alone proved benefit, the strawberry would trap you hardest. It tastes bright and sweet and whole. Yet the strawberry never started the slide. The pack did. The difference is not deliciousness. The difference is construction. One was grown to be eaten and to finish. The other was tuned to pique without telling the brain to stop, sweetness with melt that tells the mouth no weight arrived, so the mouth asks again.

You never refused the strawberry because there was nothing to refuse. You keep answering the pack because the pack was built to keep the question open.

Taste the difference plainly and the too-good vote loses its force. The mouth likes sweet. The loop sells you BAD SUGAR as sweet and then keeps the hand moving after liking has thinned. What you called too good to refuse was too engineered to stop at one, and those are opposite things.

HUNGER NEVER ASKED FOR SWEETS

Hunger feels like proof. The stomach growls. The head thins. The hands get a little shaky. You eat something sweet and minutes later you can think again. You say hunger proves I need it. You say the body asked and BAD SUGAR answered.

Watch one hungry hour whole and the proof fails its own test.

You skip lunch for work. By four the hollow sits low and wide. You buy a bun and a sweet drink and eat them at the desk. For twenty minutes the hollow softens and the head steadies. You call that answering hunger. By five the hollow returns with a sharper edge and a mouth-pull on top. You feel emptier than at four and more fixed on food than at four. By six you picture supper plus something sweet after supper, though the bun was meant to see you through. If the bun answered hunger, why did hunger return louder and earlier than if you had eaten bread and cheese? If the drink filled a need, why did the need multiply within the hour?

There is only one honest answer. The sweet paused the noticing of hunger for minutes and then re-armed it.

Real food behaves otherwise. A plate of meat and potatoes and greens, a bowl of beans and rice, fish with bread, eggs with vegetables, leaves hunger settled for hours and leaves the mind off food. No one finishes such a plate and immediately pictures a second plate while still chewing. The stomach has received weight and the brain has received the signal. BAD SUGAR behaves otherwise because it arrives as a fast hit with no weight to report. The mouth fires. The stomach waits. The thoughts stay pegged to the next hit. We called that hunger returning. It was the hit wearing off and leaving the original hunger underneath plus a new mouth-pull on top.

Think of children before the loop takes hold. They run hungry to the table and eat plain food with appetite and stop and run again. They do not ask for the chemistry of the icing. They ask for food and for rest and for the day to resume. Think of yourself on days built from meals. You ate breakfast with appetite. You ate lunch till satisfied. The afternoon held. You did not timetable chocolate like medicine. The days you timetabled chocolate were the days built from grazes and sips, sweet on sweet, each hit buying the next hollow.

Ask yourself three plain questions and answer as if plain speaking mattered more than defending a habit.

When did a sweet taken for hunger ever keep you off food till the next meal the way a meal keeps you off food? When did the shaky hollow ever stay gone after BAD SUGAR and not return with interest before the hour ended? When did you finish a pack hungry and feel hunger done rather than hunger plus thirst plus motion plus self-reproach?

You know the pattern because you timetable around it. The roller coaster you ride most afternoons is not the body failing. It is the fast hit lifting for minutes what the last fast hit dropped, then dropping it again. The body manufactures the steady supply it needs from the food you eat across the day. It does not run on spoonfuls dropped from above. What runs on spoonfuls is the loop, which borrows hunger's voice to order its own refills.

Hunger never ordered BAD SUGAR. The loop ordered BAD SUGAR and signed hunger's name.

WANTING OUTLIVES LIKING

There is a split inside you and once you see it you cannot unsee it.

There is liking, the brief brightness when the first bites sing. There is wanting, the pull that keeps the hand travelling to the pack after the brightness has thinned. They feel like one thing because they arrive together at the start. They are not one thing. Wanting runs on one wiring and liking runs on another, and wanting can roar for hours after liking has gone dull.

The fact is you did not empty packs because every bite stayed heavenly. You emptied packs because wanting outlived liking.

Watch any pack from the inside and you can time the split.

Bites one to three belong to liking. The mouth wakes. Talk pauses. You shut your eyes without meaning to. Bites four to six belong to habit. The brightness thins. You start reading while you chew. Bites seven to the bottom belong to wanting alone. You no longer taste shape. You taste sweet and chew and reach. You tell yourself you will stop at the next one and reach past the next one. If liking drove the pack, the pack would end when liking ended, around bite three. The pack ends when the container ends or shame ends it, long after liking left the room.

Ask what drove those last bites if liking had already left.

Did you lift the seventh biscuit because the sixth sang louder? Or because the hand felt restless till it lifted? Did you pour the second bowl of cereal because the first still sang on the tongue? Or because the mouth felt coated and the thoughts felt busy and finishing felt harder than continuing? Did you drink the last third of the bottle because it tasted better cold, or because the bottle stood open and the pull stood open with it?

There is only one honest reading. The last bites were wanted without being enjoyed.

I know this is hard to accept if you built years around taste. I built years around it too. I told myself I had a sweet tooth the way other men have blue eyes, inborn and final. I collected the bars I liked and learned their wrappers the way other men learn teams. The tooth explained everything and excused everything. Then I timed one pack whole and the tooth story broke. The tooth liked three biscuits. Something else ate the other nine.

That something else is not character. It is the wanting wiring lit by fast hits stacked on fast hits. Each hit lights wanting for the next. Liking fades with repetition the way any bright signal fades. Wanting grows with repetition the way any trained pull grows. That is why one is too much because it is never enough among ourselves became a joke that stopped being funny. One lights wanting more than it satisfies liking. The second chases the first. The pack ends the chase by running out, not by satisfying.

See how different that is from the old story. The old story said you lack appreciation and control. You do not. You appreciated exactly what there was to appreciate in three bites. The rest was not appreciation. It was motion after brightness, the hand obeying a pull the mouth no longer endorsed.

Once you time the split you stop trusting the pull as taste. You hear the mouth go dull and you know the hand has no taster behind it now, only motion. The pack loses its vote because its best witnesses, your own first three bites, testify against the last nine.

IF TASTE PROVED ANYTHING

Stand taste up for its last test and let it speak for itself.

If BAD SUGAR tastes too good to be doubted, why does it taste best in the first minutes and dullest when you have had most? If dessert crowns the meal, why does the meal feel lighter and the night feel heavier after the crown? If hunger called for sweets, why are you hungrier an hour after sweets than an hour after a meal?

Answer plainly and the taste story fails its own test. What passed as proof of benefit was proof of construction. The fast hit sings at the start because contrast sings. It dulls by the middle because the mouth tells the truth. It pulls at the end because wanting outlives liking and the container stays open.

Think of the tables that prove it without argument. The dinner eaten hungry where bread and soup and meat satisfied and the talk carried the hour and no one missed a capstone. The afternoon built from lunch that held till supper with no timetable of sips. The fruit eaten after weeks of plain meals that tasted bright because the mouth had washed clean. You already own hours where taste arrived whole with no pack to thank. You lived them. Taste did not need BAD SUGAR. BAD SUGAR needed taste to look like more than a fast hit that dulls as it pulls.

Put liking and wanting side by side once and the tie breaks.

Liking stays honest. It sings for bites and then goes dull and leaves you satisfied. It never asks for a larger dose next time to feel the same. It never leaves wrappers and a busy mind and a vow to be good on Monday. Wanting does the opposite. It grows with feeding. It asks for more next time to chase the first minutes. It leaves the mouth coated and the mind picturing the shop. You know the aftertaste of a tasted pack. The stomach still hollow. The head foggy. The inner voice turning. That turning is not the mark of pleasure received. It is the mark of wanting fed and liking starved.

The body never lied to you about hunger. The mouth never lied to you about the first bites. The lie arrived between them when the loop taught you to read wanting as liking and to read both as need. Untie those wires and the pack stands alone, loud at the start and empty at the end, with nothing it can still prove.

All you have to do is follow all the instructions.

SUMMARY
- One biscuit against real hunger woke the mouth and left the stomach empty, the mouth-pull is not hunger.
- The pack sang for three bites and turned mechanical by the bottom, the hand kept moving after the mouth went dull.
- The trouble crowds into processed fast hits and leaves apples and strawberries alone, it was never the strawberry.
- Hunger taken to BAD SUGAR returned louder within the hour, sweets pause the noticing and re-arm the hollow.
- Wanting runs apart from liking and outlives it, the last bites were wanted without being enjoyed.
- Dessert never crowned the meal, the meal and the company and the hour carried every table you remember.
- Taste proved construction and not benefit, contrast sings and then dulls while the pull keeps the hand travelling.
