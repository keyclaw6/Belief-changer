WHO MADE YOU WANT IT

IN THIS CHAPTER: the white room with spoons and warm trays, the melt that vanishes on the tongue, the Saturday aisle with carts high with colour, the cartoon break between shows, and the small back label that once told half the truth.

*You never had a weak mouth. You had strong makers.*

Come with me to the room where the wanting was built, because seeing it proves the craving never started in you.

It is not a kitchen. It is a white room with long tables and small white dishes in a row. On each dish a spoon of sweet waits for a score. Men and women in coats taste and spit and mark papers. They add a little more sweet that promises deeper pleasure and that promise shows the liking was tuned. They try a little less sweet that costs them the peak and that cost shows the peak was hunted. They hide a little salt under it that means the tongue sings louder and that meaning shows the tongue was played. They are not eating for hunger. They are hunting a number.

We need to see this room because we have blamed the wrong person for years. We told ourselves the craving started in our character. We told ourselves other people can take one and walk away while we cannot. We told ourselves the hand in the packet proves something soft in us. That story kept us ashamed and kept us buying while the white room tells another story where the hand proves skill in them.

THE NUMBER THEY HUNT

“I just like sweet things. That is just how I am.” We all said it because it sounds like honesty about ourselves. It sounds like knowing yourself while it hides the white room, and that hiding proves liking cannot prove need. Look at where that liking was aimed before you ever chose it.

In the white room they hunt what they call the optimum where too little leaves the mouth flat and too much pushes it away. In the middle there is a peak where the mouth lights and asks for more. They test it with panels and scores. They move the sweet up a grain and down a grain until the scores climb highest. That peak has a trade name in papers, and I speak it plainly because you must know they call it the bliss point.

Ask what a bliss point does to a person who believes liking proves need.

You taste the peak and the tongue sings with bright pleasure. You think the singing means strength and love, and that thinking shows the Treat Story reads tuning as need, for the singing only means a dial was turned. Pianos sing sweetly when the tuner knows the strings well through practice.

When did your tongue ever get a fair test. When did liking ever prove benefit once the liking was tuned on purpose.

You know the answer because you lived the rest of the day. The tuned bite never closed hunger but opened it wider within the hour. The song never fed the body but summoned the hand toward the next packet.

The fact proves the liking was manufactured before you arrived.

There is a second dial beside the sweet and it matters more. They build the bite to melt. It goes crisp and then gone before the jaw has work, while it goes creamy and then air while the mouth stays pleased. Chew finds nothing to chew and swallow finds nothing that sits, and in papers this trick has a plain name they call vanishing density. Watch what that vanishing does in the mouth of a person who trusts the body, because watching proves fullness never had a fair chance.

The body knows weight and chew and stay as signals that food arrived. Soup sits warm and heavy while bread sits and asks the jaw to work, and an apple sits and asks the jaw to work with juice and fibre. The tuned bite gives none of those readings. It floods sweet across the tongue and then absents itself without a trace. The tongue reports a party while the belly reports that nothing arrived. The head hears the party and waits for the bill that never comes, so it orders again to finish a meal that never started. Is that your weakness. Did you fail to chew what was built to need no chewing. Would you blame your eyes for not seeing through a trick of light built by men who study light.

The tuned bite proves exactly what tuned bites do. It piqued without telling the brain to stop.

They gain when you eat more and the gain shows a plain name in ledgers they call stomach share. How much of your stomach belongs to them today and how much of tomorrow can be taken when every peak takes more share. Every vanishing melt takes it faster because nothing tells you to put the packet down.

That proves the whole of their business in one plain line that clears you, for you were not weak in character but outmatched by panels.

THE MELT THAT TEACHES MORE

“But I keep going after the first bite. Other people stop.” We said that in the kitchen at night with the packet open. We watched ourselves take the second and the third while telling ourselves to stop at one, and we read the second as proof of a flaw. Look at the second through the white room and the flaw changes address at once, because looking there proves the recipe taught the hand.

Take one tuned biscuit that arrives sweet and fat and fast with a melt that leaves. It arrives sweet and fast while the mouth lights up at once. It melts and leaves before the belly can answer. The mouth stays awake with the signal ringing while the belly stays un-told with no closing signal. The head holds the sweet signal with no closing signal beside it. Of course the hand moves again when the first bite taught the mouth to expect the peak. The vanishing taught the belly to report nothing while the tongue sang. The second bite is not a new choice. It is the first bite finishing its lesson.

I have stood with eaters who could leave plain food half eaten without a thought. They leave soup sitting in the bowl because it tells the body that food arrived and hunger heard it. They leave potatoes on the plate because the body says enough and asks for nothing more. They leave apples to brown on the desk because no tuned peak taught them to want again. The same eaters could not leave the tuned packet half eaten without hearing it call from the cupboard. Ask what that difference proves.

When did plain food ever call from the cupboard. When did soup ever need a clip and a high shelf and a promise to be good. You know because you lived both kinds of evenings. The call lives in the tuned packet that was built to pique. The quiet lives in real food that chews and closes.

The loop was never about your character. It was about density that vanishes and sweet that peaks. One trick teaches want while the other removes stop, and together they teach the exact night you blame yourself for. The box empties not because one is too much because it is never enough. It empties because the food was built to make enough never arrive.

That is why counting the bites never touched it. You counted what they tuned to peak and timed what they timed to return. You know you blamed the eater for the recipe that was written in a lab.

THE AISLE

Walk the aisle with me on a Saturday morning while the shop hums, because walking proves choosing starts before hunger.

The lights stay bright over wide floors that fit two carts side by side. The shelves rise higher than a man. At the entrance the carts wait in a row and the children sit in the seats with their legs swinging. We push in with a list for supper and by the second turn the list is gone from the hand. Look at what the aisle does before any hunger speaks, because looking proves want was stocked before need woke.

Colour calls first with gold and red shining. Tubes and bars carry windows that show the melt, which proves packets summon. At child eye height the boxes wear faces that smile at small children. The tiger roars and the bear waves while the spoon dives into a bowl that never empties on the cardboard. Higher up adult boxes promise comfort about quiet evenings and rewards. One box promises a quiet evening in after a long day, which proves choosing was staged for tired adults. Another box promises a reward after a hard day, which proves the Treat Story was printed on cardboard. Another box promises something warm that will get you through the week, which proves want was stocked before hunger spoke. The words change with the shelf height while the sweet inside stays the same.

We walk and we collect. We take a packet that promises company for the film and that promise proves choosing was staged in the lab. We take a box that claims to give us the morning and that claim proves the list never stood a chance. None of it was on the list. All of it felt like choosing. By the third aisle the cart holds a week of tuned peaks and the head is already tasting them in order. At the checkout the line looks the same in every direction. Every cart stands high with the same colour while every buyer looks calm as if this were nature. Nobody looks trapped in that line while everybody holds the same dose.

That sameness fooled us for years. We told ourselves everyone does it, so it must be normal, so the trouble must be our portion and not the packet. Look again at the line and ask who made the sameness. When did a field ever stack itself in cardboard at child eye height. When did broccoli ever buy the end of the aisle and the cartoon break beside it. You know the sameness was shipped before you woke, and normal was delivered by lorry.

I do not blame the shoppers in that line and you must not. The mother with the crying child is not weak when colour presses on them. The man with the late shift is not weak when the shelf offers lift at night. The student with the thin wallet is not weak when cheap peaks fill the shelves. Each is a lone eater against armies of formulators with panels and scores and shelf plans. Each hears the Treat Story from every side and thinks the voice is their own. Pity that line instead of envying or judging it. We stood in it for years. We know how kind the people in it are and how hard they try.

The fact proves the aisle is a machine for making want look like choice, and that fact takes the gaze off you.

Take the stash we all seeded after that walk, because seeing proves the aisle now lives in our kitchen. We stock the high cupboard with sweet that promises rescue and that promise teaches the aisle now lives in our kitchen. We fill the drawer under the tea towels with sweet that claims to give calm and that claim teaches hiding was learned, not born. We told ourselves the stash proves we are secretive and strange. The stash proves we learned the aisle lesson well. Buy when calm and hide when ashamed and eat when the house sleeps became the drill we learned. It waits with the same patience as the shelf.

Ask who taught that patience to a wrapper, because asking proves learning has an author. Did you teach yourself to hear a wrapper at midnight. Did the pinch arrive because you are built wrong, or because tuned peaks on every shelf trained the pinch to arrive on time.

The pinch proves training by mornings of bright hits. The training had a budget with panels and ads.

THE CARTOON BREAK

“I should have taught my children better. I should have known better.”

That voice hurts most because it sounds like love failing. Know it with care because it carries blame that never belonged on you.

Sit on a sofa at half past seven with a child beside you where a child with milk teeth sits cross-legged and the show breaks for ads. The screen fills with a bowl that pours itself and never spills. The milk turns brown and smiles. A song about mornings plays while crumbs dance. The child laughs and lifts the empty bowl to the light to see if the picture is inside. By Monday the child names the box by its animal. By Friday the child reads the aisle by colour before reading words at all.

Tell me where choice lives in that small sofa scene, because telling proves consent never entered it.

When did a four year old consent to a panel score. When did a tired parent at the till, with a queue behind and a child pulling the sleeve, freely choose against colour and song and animal and peer and price and placement all at once. You know the till. You lived the till. The box went in the cart while the child cheered and you told yourself one box cannot hurt and everyone else buys it.

The Treat Story starts there where sweet means love and morning and the hero likes you and good days come in cardboard, and by the time teeth ache the story is already grown. By the time the adult counts squares the story is already old. We did not write it. It was read to us before we could read.

Look at your own first sweet memory and separate what rode together, because memory proves warmth came from people. You remember the birthday room where cousins shouted around the table. You remember the cinema where lights went low while friends leaned close. You remember the grandmother who pressed a coin and a bar into your palm. The sugar did not make the room warm. The people made the room warm. The sugar rode the warmth and billed itself as the warmth after. That billing is brainwashing, plain and complete. Take the sweet out of the memory and the warmth stays. Take the people out and the sweet is a brown square in an empty room.

Would you call that square love. Would you call it comfort when the room is empty and the square sits alone on the table. You know you would not. The square was only ever sneaking a ride on love.

That proves why blame cannot stay on you, for you were aimed at before you could aim back while your children are aimed at now, and anger belongs with the aiming and not with the aimed-at.

THE NUMBER THAT HID THE SPOONS

“Why did I not know it was in that at all.”

We asked it over yoghurt that ate like pudding in the morning and over cereal that ate like cake with milk. We asked it over bread and sauce that never called themselves sweets in the head. The asking sounded like confession. It was evidence. Turn the packet over where the small busy back listed one number for all sweet together for years. The sweet that arrived in milk and fruit sat in the same number as the sweet poured in by the maker. The spoon you added at home sat beside the spoons added in the plant. You could not see which was which. You could read and still not know.

Only lately was the added part made visible on its own line. Before that the system hid the fuel in a single figure, so your “I did not know” was certain and a person cannot choose against what the label hides.

Ask what that hidden number did to every rule we tried, because asking proves the map was wrong.

When did counting ever work when the count was half blind. When did being good ever hold when good food arrived already dosed. You know the rules failed because the map was wrong, not because the walker was weak.

Hold one plain bottle to see the dose, because holding proves a sip can spend the day. One tall cola holds about thirty nine grams. That is almost ten teaspoons in one drink. Ten teaspoons gone before supper, before the biscuit, before the sauce on the evening meal. The day’s whole budget spent in minutes while the thirst stays and the belly stays open. I do not say one bottle fells every person, for I say the bottle shows the scale and you were never choosing a small treat. You were choosing most of the day at once while thinking you chose a sip.

Look down the label list and the spoons hide again in plain foods, because looking proves the loop feeds where we never look. ONE IN EIGHT

Look now at the scale and let it move the gaze off yourself, because looking proves a tilted floor beats weak feet.

Adult numbers more than doubled since nineteen ninety while young numbers quadrupled, and I show those numbers flat because they matter. A hurt of that size does not arrive because millions of separate characters failed in separate kitchens in the same decades. It arrives with a change in the whole food around them. Lorries and aisles and labs and breaks and labels all moved one way while will was blamed for not holding the other way.

I do not say BAD SUGAR alone wrote every pound. Many drivers move weight. Drink and portions and hours and sleep and streets without pavements all play their part. I know the contour clears you. When a whole population slides together, look for the floor that tilted, not the feet that slipped.
