Chapter 15
THE YEAR THE TRANCE BROKE

IN THIS CHAPTER: a lined notebook left by the kettle, the first tired week and vivid nights, plain food tasting too sweet, the smell from a wrapper months later, and one last binge that ended the argument.

*Stopping does not wear thin. It beds in.*

October. Tuesday night. The kitchen light still on.

A notebook lies open by the kettle with the pen clipped to the spine. The handwriting leans and hurries. Day one. Day two. Day three. No lecture in it. No table of numbers. Only what the mouth did and what the afternoon asked and what the night showed. I want you to read over that shoulder with me because you have told yourself a story I hear every week.

You have told yourself it works for others, not for me.

I do not blame you for that thought. We all stood where you stand. We watched someone else walk out light and we said she must be built different. We felt the afternoon call and the evening argue and we said our case must be heavier. Let me give you company rather than counsel. One ordinary eater kept the record you never kept. She was tired and doubtful and dosed every day like you. She wrote it down while it happened. Read her year and tell me where you differ.

She did not write to prove anything. She wrote to stay honest.

DAY ONE TO DAY FIVE: THE TIRED START

Monday she ate breakfast and lunch as food and left BAD SUGAR out of both. By three the head felt thick. By five the hands felt empty. She wrote, “tired, short, snapping at nothing.” Tuesday brought the same fog with a headache behind the eyes. Wednesday night she dreamed of a shop aisle and woke with her jaw tight. Thursday she felt grouchy before noon and ate a large plate of eggs and potatoes at one and felt the grouch pass by two.

None of that frightened her once she understood it. I do not want you to change from fear, and the method takes care of it.

The fact is the body protests for a few days when a daily rhythm stops. We dosed it sweet at the same hours for years. We taught it to expect the lift at three and the cap after dinner. When the hour comes without the dose, the hour pinches. That pinch is small and brief. In her notebook it peaks at two to five days and then it passes. Hers peaked on day three. By day six the mornings came back plain.

Ask the only honest questions those five days allow.

When did a few grouchy afternoons ever prove you need the thing that made the afternoons grouchy. When did a strange dream ever prove hunger. You know the answers because you have lived both sides. The headache did not arrive from lack of food. It arrived from lack of the rhythm. Food removed it. Time removed the rest.

That is the first entry in the proof. The start feels rough for days, not for life. What feels like inability is only unfamiliar hours.

Friday she writes longer because the night tested her. The house went still after ten and the cupboard door seemed louder than usual. She made toast with butter and ate it at the table and went to bed. She writes, “dreamed of pouring cola into a plant pot.” Saturday she shopped with a list and cooked a pot of stew and ate two bowls when hunger called and left the pot. Her husband said she seemed less edgy at dinner. She writes, “ate and talked and did not think of pudding until the plates were washed.” That line matters more than it looks. The pudding thought arrived after satisfaction had already answered, and it had nowhere to land.

THE WEEKS THE LIGHT CAME BACK

By the second week her pen changes.

She writes shorter entries because there is less to report. “Ate at seven. Worked through. No three o’clock search.” “Soup and bread at noon. Full at one. Forgot to look up.” “Walked home without stopping at the kiosk.” The afternoons that once bossed her lose their voice. The evenings that once bargained grow dull.

We know that dullness. We lived years where the day was a timetable of lifts. Bright for an hour after the sweet, heavy two hours later, bright again after the next, heavy again by night. Each lift felt like help. Each drop felt like need. We never saw the help and the need were one motion. We thought BAD SUGAR carried us through the slump. The notebook shows the slump was the payment for the carry.

She notices what her body already knows how to do. Hunger calls before meals and goes still after them. Thirst feels like thirst and not like a call for cola. Sleep comes sooner without the late bowl. She writes on day eighteen, “I woke before the alarm and made porridge and ate it standing by the window.” There is no sermon in that line. There is a woman at a window with a bowl, and the day starting without negotiation.

Does that sound like someone white-knuckling through loss. Does that sound like someone missing a pleasure. You know it does not. The entries grow boring, and boring here means the day has stopped shouting orders.

Day twenty-four she stays late at the office. The tin on the meeting table opens at four and travels hand to hand. She takes coffee without sugar and keeps typing. She writes, “watched the tin go round twice and felt nothing sharp.” Day thirty she walks through the market at noon with coins in her pocket and buys apples and cheese and bread for lunch. She eats on a bench and watches pigeons and returns to work without the old fog. The notebook does not call those afternoons victories. It calls them ordinary. That ordinariness is the point the doubter misses. We imagine walking out must feel dramatic every day. Her pages show drama drains out and plain hours take its place.

That is the second entry in the proof. Energy does not fall without the dose. It steadies. The lift you called help was only a brief pause from a low the last dose dug. Remove the digging and the ground holds.

THE GRANOLA THAT TASTED LIKE SYRUP

Week six brings the page I want you to read twice.

She visits her sister. Breakfast is a bowl of honeyed granola from a box that calls itself wholesome. She eats three spoonfuls to be polite and stops. She writes, “sickly, coated, too sweet to finish.” The same food she once ate by the large bowl without pausing now stops her after minutes. She adds, “plain yoghurt with apple tasted sweeter after.”

Read that plainly and let it do its work.

We all remember eating to the bottom without enjoyment. The first two bites lit. The next ten moved. The last of the pack was wanted without being liked. The hand finished what the mouth had left minutes before. We called that a sweet tooth. The notebook calls it what it was. The liking died early. The wanting carried on.

Ask what changed in six weeks.

Did her tongue grow new parts. Did she train herself to dislike joy. No. She stopped dosing daily and the filter fell. While we dosed daily we tasted through comparison with the last bar. Everything mild seemed flat. Everything strong seemed normal. Give the mouth weeks without the daily hit and plain food speaks louder. Shop treats shout. That is not a promise that every tongue changes on the same Tuesday. It is one eater’s breakfast telling the truth about tolerance. She needed the family size to chase what two bites once gave. Now two bites give too much. She writes, “like a haze lifting off plain food.”

The verdict on that bowl is short. You did not enjoy it like you thought. You wanted it after enjoyment ended.

That is how you tell wanting from liking. Liking ends and thanks you. Wanting ends and orders more.

She turns back pages and finds the old math. October last year: a sharing pack bought for a film, finished before the opening titles ended. November: two bars kept in a drawer for guests, eaten standing before guests arrived. December: a tin received as a gift, opened to taste one and emptied by night. Each entry ends with the same sour note. “Did not even like the last half.” “Stomach sore, head dull.” We all know that sequence. The mouth lights and then coats. The belly stays untold because the engineered bite melts and vanishes and tells the brain no real food arrived. So the hand orders again to finish a meal that never started. Her June bowl breaks that sequence because liking now ends the meal.

THE SMELL MONTHS LATER

Autumn passes. Winter passes. The notebook thins to a line a week.

Then March. She writes, “corner shop, four o’clock, smell of warm pastry through the door, pull so strong I stopped.” April. “Found an old wrapper in a coat pocket. Mouth watered before I knew what it was.” May. “Television dessert, late, the old tug for an hour.”

If you have ever feared that pull means you failed, read her next lines.

She does nothing. She pays and walks. She drops the wrapper in the bin. She watches the programme to the end and washes the cup. She writes, “gone by bedtime.” “gone by the stairs.” “gone before the kettle boiled.”

We need to understand that pull without giving it power it does not own.

After months without daily dosing, a smell or a packet or an hour can still fire the old signal. The cue learned its trade over years. It fires before the bite, not during it. The mouth waters at the door, not at the table. That does not mean the trap still holds you. It means the bell still hangs where it hung. A bell can ring in an empty house.

Ask what those three pulls prove.

Do they prove the dose still gives something. She stood in the smell and ate nothing and the evening stayed whole. People owned the walk home. Work owned the afternoon. Hunger owned the dinner and satisfaction answered. The sweet added nothing to any of those rooms. Do they prove she must answer every ring. She answered none and each ring faded faster than the last. The pull came. The pull passed. The days kept their shape.

That is the third entry in the proof. Cues linger and then thin. Their strength after time off is a learned echo, not a verdict on your future. Hear it and keep walking and it teaches you how little it owns.

June she queues behind a boy with a frosted drink the colour of a traffic light. The straw squeaks. The old tug rises for a minute. She watches him stir and sip and stir and notes what she once missed. He sips and frowns and sips again, chasing the first sip. She buys water and leaves. She writes, “he looked busy, not happy.” July she sits through a birthday speech while cake travels round. She takes fruit and cheese and listens to the speeches. People owned the laughter that night. The room owned the warmth. The cake rode along and took the praise. She writes, “sang and clapped and forgot the plate.” Those lines are not boasts. They are field notes from someone learning that joy stays where it always lived.

THE BINGE THAT ENDED THE ARGUMENT

Summer brings the page that makes this chapter necessary.

A wedding. A hot garden. Tables of cold drinks and white plates. She eats cake and sweet drinks through the afternoon and writes the next day, “fun, fizzy, then flat.” The day after she eats more, alone this time, biscuits and ice cream to the bottom. She writes, “fun for a day, then unwanted.”

Here is where you expect collapse. Here is where the old voice says one slip proves the dungeon still owns you.

Read what she actually does.

She wakes thick-mouthed on the second morning. She eats eggs and fish and greens when hunger calls and stops when satisfaction answers. By noon the tight gnaw under the ribs has gone. By evening she writes, “glad it is not daily.” The next week she writes nothing about sweets at all. The week after she writes, “thought of cake at a birthday, felt glad to be out.”

We have all lived the opposite story under rules. One bite became the box. The box became the week. The week became the confession that we lack character. That was not character. That was the yo-yo schedule at work. Hold back by counting, then release by weekend, and the release rushes through the open gate in the first hour. Each restrained week rehearsed the raid.

She was under no tally. She held no rule about being good. She met the old dose as an experiment without brainwashing to feed it, and the dose could not hold her. The first afternoon borrowed amusement from the garden and the company and the sun. The second day borrowed nothing and showed the bare mechanics.-lit mouth, coated mouth, heavy head, evening bargain. She saw the bargain from outside because she had lived months outside it.

Ask the questions that binge allows.

When did two days ever rebuild a daily rhythm she had left a year before. When did a brief amusement ever prove daily dosing gives anything. You know a summer cold does not give you winter. A remembered song does not move you back into the flat you left. The dose visited. The life did not reverse.

Monday she shops without a list of rules. She buys fish and potatoes and greens and oranges. She cooks when hunger calls and eats sitting down and stops when satisfaction answers. Tuesday she wakes with a plain head and works through the morning without searching drawers. Wednesday she writes, “yesterday’s binge feels distant, like a film I walked out of.” The notebook does not scold. It records. No tally restarts. No penance meal follows. That absence of penance is what makes the experiment self-correcting. Under the old counting weeks a binge proved the rule had failed and the rule was tightened and the next binge was rehearsed. Here a binge proves the dose has nowhere to live now, and life resumes its shape by the next shop.

That is the turn this whole book bends toward. Leaving compounds. The first month she stood past cues by noticing. The sixth month she laughed past them without pausing. The twelfth month she tested the old room and walked out with the door banging behind her. Each passage made the next passage less interesting. Not because she grew harder. Because she saw more plainly that there was nothing to keep.

WHAT THIS PROVES ABOUT YOU

“But she is not me.”

Say it aloud with me because I want no corner left where doubt can breed.

“But I dosed longer. But I dosed more at night. But I work beside a tin. But I raised children on it. But my evenings are lonelier than hers.”

I meet every version of that sentence with the same answer. The notebook is not about her stomach. It is about the object. The object does nothing for any stomach. It lifts by relieving a low it dug. It rewards by riding love that was already present. It tastes by lighting two bites and then driving the hand after liking ended. Change the eater and the object stays empty. Put a heavier craver or a lighter craver at the same table and the cake still borrows its warmth from people and occasion and real food. The sweet only ever sneaked a ride.

When did your length of dosing ever make a false benefit true. When did your night pantry ever make the coaster climb higher than normal. When did your lonely hour ever receive comfort from a packet that left the problem staring back after twenty minutes. You know the only honest answers because you lived the same hours she lived. The lift fell back by evening on the same day it lifted. The comfort soured into regret before sleep. The pack ended without thanks.

It is the other way around from what the doubt says. You are not the exception that proves the method fails. You are the rule it was built for. The doubter who needs a companion gets one here, dated and tired and ordinary, and the companion hands you a proven record. Her first week was grouchy and brief. Her sixth week turned the sweet sickly. Her spring cues rang and faded. Her summer test ended unwanted. Each stage asked less than the last.

Take that order and feel its weight. If quitting were a trait, her year would show strain stretching. It shows the opposite. Strain shrank. Attention returned to work and meals and walks. The thought of BAD SUGAR crossed her mind at a birthday table and arrived already answered.

Say the settled sentence plainly and let it settle. The year did not make her strong. The seeing made the year light.

SUMMARY
- The first days pinch and pass within days, which proves unfamiliar hours rather than need.
- Steady weeks replace the lift-and-drop timetable once daily dosing stops digging the low.
- Plain food tastes louder after weeks out, which shows wanting long outlived liking.
- Old smells and wrappers can still ring months later and fade when left unanswered.
- A brief return to the old dose after long time out feels fun for a day then unwanted, which proves daily life was the benefit all along.
- Her arc answers the not-me doubt because the object is empty for every eater, and each passage asks less than the last.
