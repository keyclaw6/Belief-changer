Chapter 2

WHO IS SMOKING WHOM

IN THIS CHAPTER: the bedroom before standing, the stairs at work, the hourly reach, the long drive and the petrol station sink.

*You did not choose the timetable. The timetable chose you.*

Count Your Yesterday

Let us count one ordinary day together, not an extreme day and not a story about someone else.

You woke and you wanted one before your feet found the floor. You had one with coffee and you told yourself the coffee called for it. You had one after food and you told yourself the meal called for it. You had one when the phone rang badly and you told yourself the nerves called for it. You had one when you waited for the bus and you told yourself the waiting called for it. You had one on the break and you told yourself the break called for it. You had one in the evening when nothing was wrong and you told yourself the quiet called for it.

How many was that. Twenty. Thirty. You know your number better than I do.

I am not asking you to be ashamed of the number. I am asking you to look at it with me the way we would look at any timetable that runs a life. We fell for a story that says we smoke because we decide to in each separate moment for a separate reason. The day you just counted tells a different story. The reasons change every hour but the hour never changes.

If each cigarette were a free decision made for its own reason, your reasons would vary the way free decisions vary. Some days you would have three. Some days you would have none. Some stressful days you would forget entirely because you were too busy surviving to remember a luxury. Some easy days you would remember often because you had time to enjoy a luxury. That is how free pleasures behave. They wait until they are wanted.

Your smoking does not wait. It arrives on schedule whether the day is hard or soft, whether you are alone or with friends, whether you are happy or sad. The excuse changes so the schedule can stay the same. That is not how a free choice behaves. That is how obedience behaves.

You say to me, and I hear the dignity in it, that you choose this and nobody forces you. I respect the dignity. I want to protect it. So let me ask you three plain questions and let you answer them honestly against your own yesterday.

If you choose freely, why do you smoke the ones you do not even want. Why do you light one and then leave it burning in the ashtray while you do something else, and then light another. Why do you smoke in the rain under a doorway when you hate the cold and hate the wet and hate standing apart. Would you freely choose a pleasure that requires you to stand in the rain to enjoy it.

If you choose freely, why does the want arrive without your permission. You sit in a meeting where you cannot smoke and you do not decide to want one. The want decides for you. It taps your shoulder at its own hour. You look at the clock. You calculate how long until you can get out. Who taught you to watch the clock that way.

If smoking were truly your decision, why do you need this book in your hands. A man who freely chooses to collect stamps does not need a book to stop collecting stamps. He stops when he prefers to stop. You hold this book because stopping has not felt like that. That fact is not an insult. It is evidence.

There is only one answer that fits all three without calling you foolish. Something else has been deciding. You were never foolish to fall for it. You were taught to misread obedience as choice.

That enclosure has a name. The nicotine trap.

Inside it live two residents you will come to know well, and I mention them only in passing now because you have already met them every day without knowing their names. The Nipper. The Con. One tugs at your body. One talks to your mind. Between them they keep the timetable running while you carry the blame for a schedule you did not write.

The Morning Before Standing

Think of this morning, because the morning tells the truth before the day has time to dress it up.

The alarm goes. Your mouth is dry. Your chest feels heavy. You cough. Before you stand, before you wash, before you speak to anyone, your hand is already reaching for the pack on the table beside the bed. You light up sitting on the edge of the bed with your feet barely on the floor. The room is still dim. The house is still quiet. No stress has happened yet. No pleasure has been offered. No friend has invited you. No work has pressed you. Yet you smoke.

Why.

You believe you smoke that first one because you enjoy it. Look closer with me. You coughed as you reached. You did not think how wonderful the smoke tastes. You thought only that you must have it to start. A free enjoyment does not begin with a cough and a compulsion before the day has begun. A need begins that way.

What you felt in those first seconds was not a day calling for a pleasure. It was a body calling for the next dose after a night without one. During sleep you went hours without nicotine. The level fell. The want grew while you slept. You woke into the want. The cigarette did not start your morning. It answered the want that the last cigarette yesterday evening had guaranteed would be waiting this morning.

I want you to notice the order, because the order is everything. You did not wake restless because life is hard in the first minute of the day. You woke restless because the dose wore off in the night. You believe the cigarette calmed you into the day. The truth is closer to this. The cigarette created the unrest it then briefly quieted.

That is why the morning cigarette feels so important and tastes so harsh at the same time. You credit it with getting you going while your throat protests the smoke and your chest tightens against it. Both feelings are true. The relief is true. The harshness is true. You misread what the relief proves. It proves replenishment, not support.

When smoke enters the lungs, nicotine reaches the brain within seconds and the level peaks within seconds of the puff. The fact is that the speed is the whole trick. You feel something change almost at once, and you call that change help. But the acute effect fades quickly as well, along with the brief sense of reward, and the fade guarantees the next reach. You never rise above the calm of a man who never smoked at all. You only climb briefly out of the hollow that the previous dose dug, and then you slide back toward it.

I do not tell you this to make you study chemistry while you drink your coffee. I tell you because your morning already taught it to you. You smoke before standing, and an hour later you need another, and the need proves nothing about your character and everything about the timetable.

We all lived that morning. We all believed it proved we loved to smoke. We woke coughing and reached before standing and still said we chose it. We said we chose it because to say otherwise felt like admitting weakness. It was not weakness. It was a designed want arriving on time.

The Clock That Rings Every Hour

Watch the rest of the day with me the way you would watch a reliable timekeeper you never hired.

You smoke after the meal. You smoke with the coffee. You smoke on the break. You smoke when you wait. You smoke when you drive. You smoke when the phone brings bad news and you smoke when the phone brings good news. The occasions look different. The interval does not. The interval stays near an hour, whatever the occasion claims to be.

That regularity should make us curious. True pleasures do not ring like a bell. You do not eat cake every hour. You do not listen to a favourite song every hour. You eat cake when cake is there and you are hungry for it. You hear the song when you choose it. Only a want that fades on a fixed curve returns on a fixed curve. Only a master rings.

Think of nicotine as that clock. Not a friend who visits when invited. A clock that rings every hour whether you invite it or not. You hear the ring as a thought. You call the thought mine. You say I fancy one. You say I need a break. You say I cannot think until I have one. But notice how the thought arrives. It does not arrive as a clear preference after calm weighing. It arrives as a faint tug that grows louder until you obey it and call the obedience a decision.

What does that tug feel like in the body. Learn its true size now, because you have believed for years that it is large. A faint, empty, slightly edgy tug. That is all it ever was in the flesh. A faint emptiness behind the ribs. A slight edge on the nerves. A sense that something is missing that a puff will settle. You know it well. You have felt it a hundred thousand times between cigarettes.

You were taught to read that tug as proof that smoking helps you. You felt edgy and you smoked and the edge softened for a while, so you credited the cigarette with calming you. But the edge was the fading of the last dose. The cigarette did not calm life. It fed the tug that the previous cigarette had planted. The relief you felt was real, and the credit you gave was mistaken. We all made that mistake because the timing hides the cause. The puff and the quiet arrive together, so we believe the puff fixed the day. The puff fixed the tug.

Now look at the stairs you climb at work or at the shops. You know how they leave you winded. You know the cough and the phlegm that greet you in the morning sink. You know how the hourly clock interrupts a film and a meal and a conversation to send you outside. You know how smell and taste have dulled, so food is less vivid and coffee needs the smoke to feel complete. You know the cold in your hands and feet on winter evenings. You know the smell you carry on clothes and hair and breath, and the small daily buy and the money handed over without thinking.

You pay all of that already, every day, while you believe you choose. I do not list it to frighten you into stopping. Fear cannot show you that leaving costs nothing. Only clear seeing can do that. The method takes care of it. I list it so you see the price of obedience clearly. A free pleasure does not charge you breath on the stairs and taste at the table and money at the till and time outside in the cold for the right to enjoy it. A master charges exactly that.

Ask yourself again with the clock in view. If you are free, why can you not skip the hourly ring without feeling the tug. Why does the free man check how long until the next one. Why does the free man stand outside away from people he loves to feed a want he says he enjoys. Does that look like enjoyment to you, honestly, or does it look like service.

You are not weak for serving it. You were conned into service and then told the service was your choice. There is a difference, and the difference saves your dignity instead of costing it.

A Hug That Will Not Let Go

Let me give you a picture for what the inhale has become, because you still think of it as a companion.

You think of the cigarette as something that holds you when life shakes. A pause. A friend. A hand on the shoulder. I understand why. The first puff after the tug feels like being held. The shoulders drop. The hands stop buzzing. The mind settles enough to go on.

Look at the hold more closely. It is a hug that will not let go.

A true comfort holds you and then releases you stronger. This hold releases you weaker. It holds you for minutes and then it tightens the need for the next hold. It comforts the tug and guarantees the tug will return. It borrows calm from the next hour to pay for calm now, and it charges interest in breath and money and time. You believe you are being held. You are being kept.

We all believed the hug helped because we felt the shoulders drop. We mistook the softening of withdrawal for the softening of life. The shoulders dropped because the tug quieted, not because the problem moved. The problem waited exactly where we left it, and now we had less breath and less time to meet it, and an hour less until the next interruption.

If a friend held you that way, you would not call him a friend for long. If every hug from a man left you more restless an hour later and sent you back for another hug on a fixed schedule, you would stop calling it comfort and start calling it keeping. You would see the kindness was the grip. That is what the inhale has become. A clinging embrace that sells itself as support while it keeps you returning.

You say you love to smoke and hate being a smoker, that the moment you light up feels like heaven and afterwards you feel low about yourself. I believe you on both halves. The heaven is the brief quiet of the tug. The low afterwards is the knowledge, buried but present, that the quiet was rented and the rent is due again in an hour. You are not lying about the heaven and you are not weak about the low. You are describing a hug that comforts and captures in the same act.

Once you see the hug clearly, you cannot unsee it. Every future puff will carry the picture with it. You will feel the arms close and you will know they will not open unless you return. That knowing is not gloom. It is the beginning of freedom, because no man keeps paying for a hug once he sees it is a hold.

The Long Drive and the Petrol Station Sink

Now drive with me, because the long drive shows the slavery and the shame in one errand without any lecture from me.

You set out for hours on the road. You smoke as you drive. One after another. The car fills. Your eyes sting. Your throat dries. You open the window to clear the air and the cold rushes in and you close it again. You tell yourself the cigarettes keep you alert and keep you company on the empty miles. You believe they help the drive.

Halfway there you stop for petrol. You get out. You see yourself in the glass doors. You smell your own coat. You are ashamed of the smell, not because you are a shameful person but because you know what non-smokers will smell when you walk back into the world. So you do what we all did. You dump the ashtray and tie the bag tight. You change the top layer if you can. You wash your hands and face in the petrol station sink. You rinse your mouth. You spray something sweet over the smoke. You chew something strong. You stand outside a moment to air yourself like a man airing a room.

Then you get back in the car and light another.

Look at that errand honestly with me. Would a free enjoyment require a deodorizing ritual halfway through. Would a true helper on a long drive require you to wash it off yourself before you meet other people. You do not wash cake off yourself after you enjoy cake. You do not spray perfume over music after you enjoy music. You clean smoke off yourself because part of you already knows it soils even while another part believes it helps.

I tell this with comic warmth because we have all stood at that sink. The dance with the soap and the spray is almost funny when you see it clearly. The man who swore he chose freedom spends ten minutes trying not to smell like his choice. He scrubs his hands that will hold another one in twenty miles. He airs the coat that will fill again before the next town. He does it carefully, even proudly, as if cleanliness could make obedience respectable.

Who is smoking whom on that drive. You tell yourself you smoke to enjoy the road. The road would be there without the smoke. The company you credit to the cigarette is the radio and the moving land and your own thoughts keeping you awake. The alertness you credit to the cigarette is your body doing its work while the hourly tug interrupts it and then briefly quiets itself so you can drive on. The cigarette did not drive with you as a friend. It rode you as a master and made you wash off the ride before you would let anyone see you.

And notice the number. On a long drive you smoke more, not because the road is more stressful mile by mile, but because the clock keeps ringing while the hands have nothing else to do. The timetable fills empty time because empty time lets you hear the ring clearly. That is why waiting smokes and driving smokes and breaks smoke. Not because those moments need a cigarette. Because those moments leave room for the tug to be heard.

You were never foolish to misread that. Every smoker misreads it. We were taught that smoking fills pauses, so we filled pauses with smoke and called the filling a choice. The pause was already full. The air and the road and the waiting were already there. The smoke only charged admission to what was already yours.

Two Small Residents and One Large Bill

We have counted the day and the morning and the clock and the hug and the drive. Now let us name what we found, plainly and without blame.

You live inside a designed enclosure that runs on a simple loop. A dose fades. A tug arrives. You feed the tug with another dose. The feed guarantees the next fade. Round and round on an hourly ring while you pay the daily bill and call the running a choice.

The nicotine trap.

Say it to yourself once, slowly, not as a slogan but as the shape of your yesterday. The nicotine trap. Every reach you counted belonged to it. Every excuse you gave belonged to it. The morning reach before standing belonged to it. The stairs and the sink and the petrol station wash belonged to it. You did not invent that shape. You stepped into it the way we all stepped in, one puff at a time on false information, and then the loop closed and called itself your will.

Inside that enclosure work the two residents I mentioned in passing. Let me introduce them properly now, because you will need their names for the rest of our work together.

The first is small and almost pitiable when you see him clearly. The Nipper.

He is the faint physical tug itself, given a name so you can see him outside yourself instead of mistaking him for yourself. He lives on nicotine and nothing else. He asks for his feed every hour with his small empty gnawing. A faint, empty, slightly edgy tug. That is his whole voice. He has no argument. He has no charm. He cannot make you smoke by strength. He can only tug and wait for the other resident to talk you into obeying.

Notice how small that makes the physical part. You were taught to fear withdrawal as days of torture that only a strong will could survive. The fact is that the body tug is faint and empty and slightly edgy, and it peaks and passes on its own in days while you get on with your life. I do not ask you to test that yet. I ask only that you stop inflating the Nipper into a monster that requires courage. He is a small feeder who dies quickly when he is not fed, and he is already starving between every cigarette you smoke. Every fade you feel is his hunger. Every puff is his brief feed. He never grows stronger by being obeyed. He only lives to tug again.

The second resident is large and loud and lives entirely in what you believe. The Con.

He is the voice that turns the small tug into a reason. He says the cigarette calms you. He says it helps you think. He says it rewards you after work. He says it keeps you company while you wait. He says you choose it freely. He says you cannot enjoy coffee or a meal or a break or a drive without it. He says one will not hurt when the time comes. He is not your character. He is your conditioning, installed over years by everyone who smoked around you and everything that told you smoking helped. He feeds the Nipper by sending you to the pack, and the Nipper feeds him back by tugging again an hour later so his lies sound true.

Once you see the pair, the day you counted rearranges itself. The hourly ring is the Nipper tugging. The changing excuse is the Con talking. The hand reaching is you obeying both while believing you chose. You did not choose. You were talked. That is good news, because talk can be seen through while a need must be endured. Kill the talk and the tug starves on its own.

And the bill for feeding both, day after day, is a fixed state you already know in your bones. Restless, dulled, and enslaved.

Read those three words against your own body. Restless from the hourly tug that never lets the nerves settle. Dulled in smell and taste and breath, on the stairs and in the morning sink, in the eyes that sting on the long drive. Enslaved to the pack and the clock and the doorway in the rain and the petrol station wash. Restless, dulled, and enslaved. That is not a sentence I pass on you. It is the receipt you already carry. You pay it every day for the privilege of staying where the Con tells you that you are free.

Do you see now why the question who is in charge answers itself. A free man does not time his day by a tug. A free man does not wash his choice off his hands before meeting people he loves. A free man does not cough as he reaches before standing and call the cough a pleasure. We did all of that and called it choice because the Con supplied the word choice at the exact moment the Nipper supplied the tug. Two small events, fused a thousand times, felt like a will.

You are not weak for fusing them. You are not stupid. You are a capable person who was conned out of choice and then blamed for lacking choice. I have sat with heavy smokers who ran firms and raised families and carried heavy loads without flinching, and they all believed they were weak because they could not stop obeying a timetable they thought was themselves. Their strength was never the problem. Their belief was the problem. Once the belief changed, the strength they already had was more than enough, because no strength is needed to stop feeding a small tug once the loud voice stops lying about what the feed gives you.

So here is the only command I give you in this chapter, and I give it warmly and without apology because a guide who sees the timetable owes you a clear direction.

4. NOTICE WHO IS IN CHARGE

Watch your own hand for one day the way we counted together. Notice each reach and name its true owner. That is the Nipper tugging. That is the Con talking. That is me obeying a schedule I did not write. Do it without changing anything and without judging yourself. You are investigating, not performing. When the tug comes, feel its true size. When the excuse comes, hear whose voice it uses. When you reach before standing and when you check the clock in the meeting and when you step out into the rain and when you wash at the sink, ask who decided that. Your honest answers will do more work than any effort, because they move the blame from you to the pair that earned it.

You came to this chapter believing you smoked because you decide to. Leave it with the timetable testifying against that belief. The day runs on a ring. The morning begins before choice. The stairs and the sink charge a daily price. The hug holds too long. The drive requires washing. The small tug and the loud talk keep the loop closed. None of that looks like freedom. All of it looks like service. And service can end the moment the servant sees the master clearly.

SUMMARY

- Smoking follows a timetable, not a free decision in each moment.
- The morning reach shows need arriving before any reason has happened.
- The hourly want is a fading dose calling for its next feed.
- The inhale comforts briefly and then guarantees the next unrest.
- The daily price is already paid in breath, taste, time, and money.
- A small physical tug and a loud learned voice keep the loop running together.
- Seeing who decides removes blame from you without excusing the loop.
