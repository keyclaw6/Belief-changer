Chapter 1

GOOD NEWS YOU WILL NOT BELIEVE YET

You can stop smoking easily, permanently, without willpower, and you find that hard to believe — so read on, with your cigarettes beside you and the shelf of your past attempts behind you.

Look at that shelf for a moment. You still keep the drawer with the half-used patches in their foil. You remember the gum that burned your throat. You see the lighter you swore you would throw away. You washed the ashtray and then you needed it again. You bought the carton because it was cheaper that way. You know every object on it. You know the chair you sat in when you said this time would be different. You know the morning you lasted until eleven and the evening you started again. I believe you about how hard that felt. There is no lecture in this book. There is an open chair, a lamp, an unhurried hour, and a reader who has earned the right to be tired of suffering.

IN THIS CHAPTER: the shelf, the chair, the lamp, and an easy read that asks nothing painful of you.

*Your past failures were not your fault, and getting free does not have to hurt.*

The Shelf Does Not Lie

You have not come to me fresh. You have come after years of attempts, and I want you to feel believed about that from the first page.

I have sat with smokers who had attempted at least twenty times before and used most of the replacements and meds on the market with no success. I do not tell you that to shame you. I tell you that because you think your record proves something is wrong with you, and it proves the opposite. When the same ending happens to strong, bright, determined people again and again, across patches and gum and sheer resolve and white-knuckle afternoons, the honest question changes. What if it was never weak people? What if it was a weak way of stopping?

Think of what those attempts asked of you. They asked you to feel you were losing something precious and then to be brave about the loss for the rest of your life. They asked you to count hours, to watch the clock, to sit with an empty feeling and call that feeling proof that cigarettes had been wonderful. They told you that freedom would feel like deprivation with a better name. No wonder you went back. Anyone with a life full of stress and meals and coffee and breaks and waiting would go back to something they still believed helped them cope.

You say to me, even if you say it kindly, that quitting always means suffering and this book cannot be different. I hear you. You have proof. The shelf is your proof.

So let me ask you plainly, as one smoker to another who has been exactly where you sit now. If you are so weak, how do you hold down a job and pay bills and look after people who count on you? If stopping is simply a matter of character, why did the misery arrive in the same shape every time, whatever product you used and whatever promise you made yourself on the first morning? And if cigarettes truly calm you and help you and please you, why did you need this book at all?

There is only one honest answer that fits all three. You are not weak. You used a method that makes strong people feel weak. It was never you. It was the method.

I call it by its true name so you never blame yourself again. The Willpower Method.

That method tells you that cigarettes relax you and help you cope and are your pleasure, and that stopping means you must be brave without them forever. It tells you that failure is personal. It keeps every relapse private and every success a matter of endurance. It is a cruel story, and it is false from the first line. Once you see that the story was the problem, the shelf behind you stops being a record of your weakness and becomes what it always was. Evidence against the method.

I know how strange that sounds. I smoked heavily myself, and I failed in all the usual ways, and I believed all the usual things about myself. I believed I loved to smoke. I believed I needed it for pressure and for pleasure. I believed life without it would be grey and that I simply lacked the spine that other ex-smokers seemed to have. I was wrong on every count, and I was not stupid to be wrong. I had been taught to be wrong by everyone around me who smoked and everyone around me who told me how to stop. When I finally saw the trick, I stopped, and it was easy, and it has stayed easy. I tell you that not to boast but to put my authority where you can see it. I escaped, I have watched thousands escape the same way, and I know why your attempts hurt.

You do not have to take my word for any of this yet. That is the whole point of how we begin. You keep smoking while you read. You bring your doubts with you. You check everything I say against your own life. If I am wrong, you will know soon enough. If I am right, you will feel the ground move under an old belief long before you smoke your last cigarette.

I want to be clear about the chair you sit in while we do this work together. You sit as a smoker, not as a patient. You light up when you want to. You drink your coffee as you always do. You take your breaks as you always do. Nothing in your day has to change while your mind is changing, and that is why your mind can change. When no one threatens the cigarette, you can look at it straight. You can notice how often you smoke without hunger for it and how often you smoke while you tell yourself you enjoy it. You can notice how the thought of never smoking again tightens the chest, and how that tightness proves the fear rather than proving a pleasure. I ask only that you notice honestly and keep turning pages.

Many smokers tell me they feel two things at once on the first evening with this book. They feel hope because someone at last describes their attempts without contempt, and they feel suspicion because hope has been sold to them before. I welcome both feelings. Keep the hope warm and keep the suspicion sharp. Let the hope keep you reading and let the suspicion test every sentence. A clear idea survives sharp testing. A false idea does not, and the false idea about smoking has survived only because we were never allowed to test it calmly while we still smoked.

Why Strong People Fail

Let me show you why even the aided attempts so often end the same way, without asking you to study anything or to trust any slogan.

In trials, the licensed products can raise the odds against a dummy over six months and more. That is true in its own narrow room. It is also true that most smokers who try still try on their own, with only a handful still free at six months. Those two facts answer different questions, and neither fact is an order about what you must buy or what you must do. Neither fact means you cannot stop. Taken together they mean something simpler and kinder, something your own shelf already told you. Whether aided or unaided, attempts built on the same idea fail in the same way for the same reason. The idea is that you are losing a prop and must endure the loss.

Do you see the trap inside that idea? If you believe cigarettes calm you in stress and reward you after work and keep you company in waiting, then every hour without one feels like proof of the belief. The empty feeling arrives, you call it need, you call the need pleasure missed, and the missing grows teeth. The products and the promises change. The belief stays intact. And while the belief stays intact, the ending stays the same.

That is why I do not want your resolve. Resolve is what you use when you believe you are being deprived of something good. I do not want you deprived of anything good. I want you to see that there was never anything good to be deprived of. That seeing is not hard. It is a relief. It happens while you sit in the chair and turn pages, not while you stare at a clock and ache.

You may be thinking that this sounds too good to be true. Good. Bring that thought with you. I would rather you argued with me on the page than nodded politely and changed nothing. Question what you think you know about your own smoking. Notice when you reach for one and what you tell yourself it will do. You are not being converted. You are investigating your own life with a guide who has walked out of the same room.

And there is no hurry in the investigation. We have time. The book is not long, the steps are few, and the door stays open the whole way. You do not have to be brave tonight. You only have to keep reading with an open mind and let each page do its work.

This is the contract I make with every smoker who reads me, and I make it with you now in plain words. You stop in one clear act and you stay free. Easily, at once, and for good.

Read those six words again slowly. Easily means no ache to admire yourself for bearing. At once means the whole habit ends in one clear act, not a long fading that keeps the want alive. For good means you do not spend life looking back. I know that sounds impossible from where you sit. It sounded impossible to me. It sounds impossible to every smoker before the belief changes, because we were all taught impossibility by The Willpower Method. It is not normal. It is the method talking.

I am not asking you to believe the contract yet. I am asking you to let it sit beside you while we test it together. If stopping were really a matter of character, the bravest people I know would have found it easiest, and they did not. If stopping were really about finding the right product, the shelf would be empty by now. Something else is going on, and that something else is good news. The need you feel is not a verdict on your nature. It is a misunderstanding that can be cleared up by thinking clearly for a few hours. When the misunderstanding goes, the want goes with it. That is why the promise can be easy. Not because you are about to become stronger, but because you are about to see straighter.

The Price You Already Know

I will not keep you from the hard part of the truth, because you already live with it and you deserve to hear it stated plainly.

The fact is that tobacco kills more than eight million people each year, including more than a million non-smokers from second-hand smoke. The fact is that smoking causes about four hundred and eighty thousand premature deaths each year in the United States alone. The fact is that smoke exposes the smoker to thousands of chemicals, including dozens that can cause cancer. The fact is that even one cigarette a day over a lifetime can cause smoking-related cancers. There is no safe level.

Sit with those sentences for a moment, not as a lecture but as the weight you already carry to the stairs and to the sink in the morning. You know the cough and the phlegm that greet you. You know how the stairs leave you winded. You believe the clock rings every hour for another dose. You feel the cold in your hands and feet and you know your smell and taste have dulled. You know the smell you carry on clothes and hair. You know the daily buy and the money handed over and the time spent stepping outside away from people you love to feed a want you did not choose. You pay all of that already, every day, for something you credit with calming you.

I state it at full weight because it is true and because softening it would insult you. You are an adult. You know the packet warnings are not decoration. You have felt the morning chest and told yourself it was nothing, and then felt it again the next morning. I will not pretend those costs are small to keep you comfortable. They are not small. They are the price of staying where you are.

Do Not Be Frightened Into This

And now hear me just as plainly on what those facts mean for how we do this.

I do not want you to stop because you are frightened. Fear fades, and what is done from fear feels like obedience, and obedience breeds the very sense of loss that sends smokers back. If those facts sit on your chest like a sentence, put them down here with me. The method takes care of it.

What do I mean by that? I mean that fear tells you what staying costs, but it cannot show you that leaving costs nothing. Only clear seeing can do that. A smoker who stops from fright while still believing cigarettes calmed him and pleased him will feel deprived and brave, and deprivation plus bravery is exactly The Willpower Method. A smoker who sees that cigarettes never truly calmed anything and never truly gave any pleasure has nothing to be brave about. He walks away glad, not scared. That gladness is what keeps him free when the fear would have worn off by spring.

So keep the facts where they belong. They remove any idea that staying is neutral. They do not have to be your fuel. Your fuel will be relief. Each chapter from here will show you, piece by piece, that the calm and the help and the pleasure you credit to the cigarette belong to you and to your life, not to the smoke. When that is clear, you will not need to push yourself out of the burning room. You will see the door was never locked.

I know you believe the damage is already done and wonder whether seeing clearly can still help someone who has smoked as long as you have. Let that worry sit beside you without driving you. The body you live in knows how to breathe and taste and move when smoke stops getting in the way, and you will feel that return in ordinary mornings long before any doctor could measure it. You do not need to earn that return by suffering first. You need only to stop feeding the want once the belief that fed it has gone. That is why I keep bringing you back to gladness. Gladness opens the mind that fear closes, and an open mind finishes this work.

There is a freedom in reading this way that smokers rarely allow themselves. You are not sneaking and you are not hiding. You sit in lamplight with the pack on the table and you study the habit the way a kind engineer studies a machine that never worked. You note the hour it calls and the meal it follows and the pause it interrupts. You see how much of your day bends around it. The seeing itself lightens you, because every honest observation loosens one more thread of the old story that you loved it.

Ask yourself now, with kindness, what you actually want. Do you want to spend the rest of your life paying daily for the privilege of staying restless? Or do you want to find out whether the relief you feel at the first puff was ever anything more than the brief quiet of a want that the last puff created? You do not have to answer yet. Keep reading, keep smoking if you want to, and watch your own answers change as the belief loosens. That loosening is the whole work. Everything else follows from it like morning follows night.

I want you excited about this read, not gloomy about it. Think how proud you will feel to understand your own smoking at last. Think how light the shoulders feel when a heavy confusion lifts. That lightness is already on its way, and it arrives long before the last cigarette. It arrives the moment you allow that perhaps the failures were the method and reading on costs nothing.

So here is how I see you, and I ask you to let yourself be seen this way for the length of this book. You are not a weak person with a shameful habit. You are a capable person caught by a clever confusion that millions of capable people share. You fell in without meaning to, you stayed in because the confusion fed itself, and you can walk out by seeing it clearly. There is warmth for you on every page and contempt only for the confusion and for the method that blamed you. I am on your side against both.

Read On Under Three Agreements

Everything hard in this book has now been said for this chapter. What remains is simple, and it is the only thing I will ever command of you.

You have already felt the first agreement forming while we talked about the shelf. Bring your doubt, bring your cigarettes, but do not close your mind before you have checked. Test me against your own mornings and breaks and meals. Notice where I am right about your life and where you think I am wrong, and keep reading past the wrong places until the pattern shows itself. An open mind is not belief. It is the willingness to look. You have nothing to lose and everything to gain by reading on.

Read that sentence again and let it sit as a fact about this week, not as a slogan. You have nothing to lose and everything to gain by reading on. You lose no pleasure by turning pages. You lose no prop. You pay nothing, you suffer nothing, you change nothing yet in your day except that you read and watch. What you gain, if I am right, is the beginning of the end of a confusion that has cost you money and breath and peace for years. That is a bargain only a closed mind would refuse, and yours is not closed. You have read this far.

The second agreement protects the first. While you read, change nothing about your smoking unless the book tells you to at the proper time. Smoke as before. Do not smoke more to test me and do not smoke less to prove yourself. Let the belief change first, then stopping is easy. Stopping now, by effort, would only bring back The Willpower Method. It would make you watch the clock instead of watching your own mind. It would make you feel deprived before you have seen there is nothing to be deprived of. I want your attention on the argument, not on the ache. There will be a clear moment to stop, and you will know it when it comes, and you will meet it glad.

The third agreement sets the tone for all of it. Come at this with gladness, not gloom. There is no doom in what we do here. This is not a punishment course. It is an escape being planned in daylight. Bring a little curiosity. Smile when you catch yourself reaching at the old times. You are learning how a trick works while the trick is still in front of you, and there is pleasure in seeing through a trick. The smoker who reads gloomily reads with the old belief that something precious is about to be taken. Nothing precious is about to be taken. Something heavy is about to be put down.

All you have to do is follow all the instructions.

That is the whole ease of the method in one line. All you have to do is follow all the instructions. Not most of them when you feel like it. All of them, exactly as given, in order, with the same care you would give a recipe that you wanted to work. The instructions are few and they are small. Keep an open mind. Do not stop yet. Begin with gladness. Later there will be a few more, each at its proper place, each with its own short reason. None of them will ask you to ache. Each of them makes the next one easier, because each protects the belief change while it happens. Follow them all and the ending takes care of itself.

So I put them now as commands, warmly and without apology, because a guide who knows the way owes you clear direction.

1. KEEP AN OPEN MIND
You cannot be conned twice if you check everything.

2. DO NOT STOP YET
Let the belief change first, then stopping is easy.

3. BEGIN WITH GLADNESS
There is no doom in what we do here.

Keep those three while you read. Smoke if you want to while you read. Watch yourself with interest, not with judgment. When the next chapter counts your day with you, let it count honestly. When a later chapter asks what good the cigarette does at all, answer honestly. Honesty is the only talent this book requires, and you already have it. You proved it by picking up a book that promised ease after everything hurt, and by reading past the first page when every past attempt told you not to hope.

You came here believing that stopping means misery and that failure is personal. Leave this chapter with a different possibility sitting beside you in the chair. Perhaps the failures were the method. Perhaps reading on costs nothing. Perhaps ease is not a lie told to beginners but the natural result of seeing straight. You do not have to be sure yet. You only have to be willing to turn the page.

SUMMARY

- Stopping does not have to mean suffering to work.
- Past failures can mean the method failed, not the person.
- Reading on costs nothing and risks nothing.
- Fear states the price of staying but glad seeing is what frees.
- A clear mind checks everything before it decides.
- Smoking stays unchanged while belief is allowed to change first.
- Gladness is the right tone for an escape.
