Chapter 5
REWARD THAT NEVER LOVED BACK

IN THIS CHAPTER: the bakery on Saturday morning, the smile-face cookies in the white bag, tonight's pay packet on the kitchen counter, the love that was always in people.

*The chocolate never paid you and never loved you — the people did, and the packet only ever billed you after.*

### The Bakery That Loved You

It is Saturday morning and you are eight years old again, and the bell over the bakery door rings the way only that door rang. The air is warm flour and butter and sugar, and the glass case runs low to the ground so a child can press a nose to it. Your mother bends beside you and lets you choose, truly choose, and you point to the smile-face cookies with the pink icing mouths, two currant eyes, the edges gone crisp in the oven. She laughs and buys them, and the woman behind the counter drops them into a white paper bag that goes soft at the corners from the warmth. You carry that bag home like treasure, swinging it careful so the faces do not smudge.

I honour that morning with you, because I am not here to mock it or to take one crumb of warmth from it. Your mother loved you in that bakery. The day was kind. The street outside was bright. You were proud to be taken and proud to be allowed. Hold that whole picture still for a moment, because this chapter stands or falls on where you place the love in it.

Tell me honestly, as one adult looking back with another, where did the love live in that bag? Did the flour love you? Did the icing know your name? Did the currant eyes see you? The love lived in her hand on your shoulder while you chose. It lived in being taken, being noticed, being allowed a Saturday ceremony that said you matter. The cookie only ever sneaked a ride on that ceremony. It sat in the bag while love did the work, then claimed the credit after.

We all carry one such morning. For some of us it is grandmother's biscotti brought out with permission when visitors came, the tin lid lifted like a small festival. For some it is payday candy from father's coat pocket on Friday, the crackle of the wrapper, the being remembered after a long week. For some it is holiday chocolate eaten on the rug while the tree lights blinked, the house full, everyone home. The details change. The structure never changes. Sweet plus people plus ceremony, and the child inside us writes the wrong name on the gift tag.

Did you love the sweet, or did you love being loved while sweet happened to be there? Put the question to your own memory and answer from inside the room, not from theory. When you remember the bakery now, what warms you first — the chemical taste on the tongue, which you could not describe to save your life, or her smile, the bell, the swung bag, the walk home? You know the answer. You have always known it. The warmth was people, leisure, safety, Saturday itself. The packet covered that sweetness for an hour the way cloud covers sun, then took praise for the light.

That mislabelling was never your fault. You were a child in a kind conspiracy. Every advert, every film, every well-meaning adult told you sweet means love, sweet means pay, sweet means celebration. The brainwashing went in before you had words to refuse it. By the time you were grown you no longer needed the bakery. The spell translated every hard day and every happy day into the same order. Tired means sweet. Kind means sweet. Lonely means sweet. Proud means sweet. The translation felt like your own voice because you had heard it since the white bag.

### Tonight's Pay Packet

Come forward thirty years and stand with me in tonight's kitchen. The day has taken plenty. Work ran long. The inbox never closed. Someone needed you four times before lunch and twice after. You kept going. You were patient when patience was thin. Now the house is half quiet, the bag is down, the keys are in the bowl, and on the counter sits the pay packet — a bar of chocolate, a tub of ice cream, the leftover cake from Sunday, whatever your house stocks for this hour. You can already feel the evening softening at the thought of it.

We speak this hour in your own dialect, so I print your dialect back and answer it with warmth. You say you want something sweet because you have been good, because the day was hard, because celebrating calls for chocolate, because the pictures call for sweets, because a hard week deserves a soft evening. You called it a genuine pleasure, comfort or lift. I hear the tired giver in that line, because we have all been that giver.

Let us watch what the pay packet actually pays.

You break the seal meaning a little, and the first two bites arrive bright. The shoulders drop. The noise of the day thins. For three minutes the evening feels kinder. Then what? Does the kindness stay? You know this film. The brightness thins. The hand keeps moving past taste. The third square tastes less than the first. The sixth tastes of little beyond sweet and chew. You eat standing, then sitting, then scrolling, and the packet empties faster than the tiredness empties. An hour later the mouth is coated, the gut is turning over, the head is a touch thicker, the patience you meant to buy is shorter than before you paid.

Which part of that hour comforted you? The first bright minutes, or the flat coated hour that followed? If a friend paid your wages on Friday and then took back twice the sum on Saturday, would you call that friend generous? If a blanket warmed for three minutes and then left you damper for the evening, would you fold it with thanks? Only honest answers count. A pay packet that guarantees a bill is not pay.

Ask the next plain question while the wrappers are still in front of you. Who earned the evening? You did. You carried the day. You answered the mail. You held your temper. You did the school run through rain or the shift through noise. The credit belongs to you, your effort, your patience, your stamina. The packet did none of that work. It arrived at the end, watched you do it all, then stepped forward to be thanked. That is the oldest move in the trap. It sneaks a ride on your virtue, then presents your own evening back to you as its gift.

Many of us learned to speak of this hour as self-payment after stress or victory. That frame keeps the loop fed, because every hard day then orders sweet and every sweet then guarantees another low that tomorrow will call hard. I do not say every sweet thought in every reader follows this one script. Some evenings run on habit, some on tiredness, some on the little creature hunger we have named. I speak only of the pay story where it lives in you, and where it lives it rules. Notice how the story never closes its account. A hard day earns sweet. Sweet makes tomorrow heavier. Heavier tomorrow earns more sweet. The wage never settles. The debt only rolls forward.

Think of the evening you actually wanted when you reached. Quiet. Warm. Yours. Feet up. Talk unhurried. Mind slowing without fog. Nothing calling from the cupboard. That evening never came from the packet. It came from stopping, sitting, being done, letting the day end. The packet only ever interrupted that ending, then offered itself as the ending. Remove the interruption and the evening you wanted is what remains. It was underneath all along, covered, waiting.

### Three Sentences From The Pay Desk

You keep this belief alive in sentences you have spoken or heard in kind voices, so I take three of them in turn and test each against the kitchen you just watched. Read each slowly. Answer as investigator, not as defendant. No scorn here. We were all conned in the same kindly way.

"find something non-food as a treat for when you feel you deserve one"

I hear the kindness in this advice, because it tries to spare you. It sees the bill and wants a cheaper pay. But watch what it leaves intact. It leaves the whole pay office open. It still says virtue needs payment, evenings need prize, goodness needs wage. Tonight it is a bath, a magazine, a new candle. Tomorrow, when the bath runs cold and the day runs harder, the old till reopens, because the belief that you are owed something for being good never closed. The question was never what currency pays virtue best. The question is why virtue sends a bill at all.

Did the bakery morning need a prize to be warm? Your mother did not pay you for being good that Saturday. She took you because it was Saturday and you were hers. The walk home needed no wage. The evening you want tonight needs no wage either. You do not need a different treat. You need to see that no treat was ever owed, because nothing was taken from you today that sweet must repay. You carried the day. The day is done. Let it be done. That single correction closes the office the advice keeps open.

"If I was to ever reject their offer to buy food/cook or not eat the food they prepared, it would be as if I rejected their love."

I hear the fear in this line, because love matters and no decent person wants to refuse it. Picture the scene as it truly runs. Grandmother sets out the plate. Mother pushes the tin toward you. A friend has cooked and watches your face for the first bite. The room holds its breath. To leave the sweet feels, for a second, like leaving them.

Hold that second beside the bakery and test it. Did your mother love you through the cookie, or through taking you, choosing with you, walking home with you? If the cookie had burned that morning and the bag had come home empty, would the walk have been loveless? You know it would not. You would still have swung her hand. You would still have been taken. The love never lived in the starch and syrup. It lived in being seen, fed, remembered, included.

Refusing a dose of BAD SUGAR never refuses the cook. It refuses the packet that sneaked a ride on the cook. Eat the meal hungry. Praise the hand that made it. Sit longer. Talk more. Take seconds of the real food made with care. Thank the maker plainly. The people feel your love in your staying, your attention, your appetite for what truly feeds. What wounds them is never your leaving the sweet. What wounds them is the story that says their love can only travel by sweet, a story the trap taught you both. Return the credit to its true owners and the fear falls away. You keep the people. You leave the packet.

Stay at the table with me for the next twenty minutes, because this is where the old fear is answered, not by another reason but by what happens when you remain.

The plates from the meal are still out, the serving dish still warm in the middle, the cloth a little creased where elbows have rested. Nobody has jumped up. Nobody has carried the sweet in as if the evening needed rescuing. You are still there, cup refilled, chair pushed back an inch, listening while your father retells the story about the wrong turn on the holiday road, the one you have heard three times and still want to hear again because of the way he does the voices. Your sister laughs before the punch line. Your mother shakes her head and reaches to straighten your cuff without thinking, the way she has always done. The room is loud in the kind way rooms get when no one is watching the clock.

You take a second spoon of the roast potatoes, because they were made with care and you want more, and your uncle slides the dish toward you without comment and asks if you got enough gravy. You praise the crisp edges the way you would praise any good work, plainly, and he nods, pleased, and tells you he left them in ten minutes longer this time. No one measures your plate. No one praises your restraint or mourns your loss, because there is no loss at the table to mourn. The talk moves on to the neighbour's new dog, to the price of the train tickets, to who will drive grandmother home. You answer, you ask, you laugh in the right places, and the evening carries you without a single tug from the sideboard.

Ten minutes pass like this. The candles burn a little lower. The serving spoons rest. Your cousin tells the story of the flooded tent, hands wide, water up to the ankles, socks drying on the car mirror, and the whole table groans and laughs at once. You watch her face while she talks, the way her eyes go bright before the funny part, the way she looks round to make sure grandmother heard. You catch grandmother's eye and smile. She smiles back. Nobody is waiting for the sweet course to start the warmth. The warmth is already there, moving round the table like the dishes did, from hand to hand, and you are inside it, not outside it watching.

When the sweet is brought out you do not make a speech. Your aunt sets down the cake stand a little to the left, the same stand she always uses, and someone says what a beauty it is, and it is. You pass the plates along with the others, you hand the server, you pour more water for the child beside you who has spilled a little and is trying not to cry. When the plate comes to you, you shake your head with a small smile and say no thank you, I am good with my meal, and you turn back to the story. No gasp goes round. No silence falls. Your aunt nods and offers to the next person. The child tugs your sleeve to show you a drawing under the table, a house with too many windows, and you bend to look.

You stay. That is the whole of it, and it is everything the fear said you could not do. You stay through the cake being cut, through the oohs, through the second slice offered and taken by your brother who closes his eyes in mock bliss and makes everyone laugh. You laugh with them, truly, because he is funny and because you love him, not because you are pretending. The cake does not stare at you. It does not call your name from the stand. It sits where it sits while the talk runs over it and past it, the way talk runs over the salt and the pepper without stopping. You notice this without trying, the way you notice rain has stopped, not by deciding but by hearing the room.

Another ten minutes go. The tea comes, strong and hot, poured into the good cups that never match. You wrap your hands round yours. Your mother asks about your garden, about the leak under the sink, about whether you still walk the long way home through the park, and you tell her, fully, not in the clipped half answers you used to give while your mind ran to the cupboard. You ask about her knee, about the choir, about the neighbour she worries for, and she tells you, fully, glad to be asked. The talk is unhurried. Nobody is performing. The plates sit, the cups steam, the window goes dark outside and the room draws closer round the table.

You notice the small things because you are there to notice them. The way your father taps his spoon twice before he drinks. The way your sister tucks her hair behind her ear when she listens. The way grandmother holds her cup in both hands to warm them, though the room is warm. The way the child under the table has fallen quiet against your leg, drawing now on your shoe with one finger. None of this needed sweet to happen. All of it was happening every year while you thought sweet was the point. You were at those tables before, but part of you was always half elsewhere, counting, bargaining, waiting for the next course, planning the next packet. Tonight no part of you is elsewhere.

When the table is cleared you carry with the others. You stack, you scrape, you hand the bowls to your brother at the sink while he rinses and you dry with the striped cloth that has hung there since you were small. The talk does not stop at the sink. It follows you, about the match on Saturday, about who left their scarf last time, about whether the cake kept well last year. You laugh, you dry, you put the clean bowls down. The cake stand sits now at the end with two slices left under the cover. You look at it once, the way you look at the salt, and put the dried spoons beside it. Nothing pulls. Nothing whispers. The evening does not tilt toward the cupboard.

You return to the sitting room with tea still warm and sit beside grandmother while she tells the bit about the bus driver who waited, the bit she always tells when the family is together. You have heard it, you hear it again, and you are glad to hear it, because her voice does the funny pause in the same place and everyone waits for it. The child climbs up beside you with the drawing finished, house, sun, dog too large for the garden, and you pin it with a magnet to the fridge where everyone can see. The room talks on. The clock moves. Half an hour has gone since the sweet was passed, and you have not left the evening once, not in mind, not in body. The love has not thinned. It has thickened, minute by minute, while you stayed.

That is what refusing the packet gives, not takes. No one at the table felt refused. Your aunt felt helped with the plates. Your mother felt heard about the choir. Your father felt laughed with, not laughed at. Grandmother felt watched and answered. The child felt noticed. You felt, minute after minute, the plain human heat of being among your own, with nothing calling from the side to split you. The sweet sat where it sat. The people were where they always were, only now you were with them fully, and the evening proved what the bakery morning always meant.

Love stays exactly where it always lived.

"if sugar really were this addictive substance, there would not be so very many people who don't have sweet tooths, who can eat sugar easily in moderation, and who don't crave it."

I hear the sharpness in this objection, because it sounds like logic and it arrives in clever voices. Let us answer it with eyes open and without bluster.

Some people never fall into this loop. They pass the bakery without a pull. They leave half a bar without a second thought. They keep no drawer and hear no call at three. Their ease proves nothing about your evenings except that the trap does not catch every ankle. Not every sailor drowns. That never proved the sea dry.

Look instead at how many ankles it does catch. In pooled questionnaires across hundreds of studies in dozens of countries, about fourteen in every hundred adults and twelve in every hundred children score in the food-addiction range on the standard questionnaire family, at levels that sit beside alcohol and tobacco in scale. I state this once and plainly, not as a diagnosis of you and not as a formal disease label, which this is not, but as the honest answer to born-this-way. This is a recognised population-scale pattern measured the same way across countries, not a unique flaw in you. You are not weak. You are not strange. You are one of many caught by the same design.

Now turn the clever question back on itself and let it close the escape it tried to open. If ease in others proved safety, would your own drawer hour count for nothing? Would the wrappers you hid, the third square past taste, the coated mouth, the second reach till bedtime count for nothing beside someone else's indifference? Your own evenings are the evidence that matters in this book, and they have already testified. Others can take or leave sweet. You found you could not leave it alone once started, however firmly you meant a sliver. That difference never marked you as defective. It marked the loop doing its signature work in you while leaving your neighbour alone. Even if some bodies answer sweet harder than others, the way out stays the same because the pay story is what keeps the loop fed.

That is the whole of the objection, met head-on and left without a foothold. The existence of light users never disproved the trap. The scale of caught users disproves the shame.

### The Child You Think You Were

Under the pay story sits a deeper story, quieter and harder to speak. It says you were always this way. Chubby kid. Sweet tooth. Born with it. One of those who simply loves sweet while others simply do not. Identity feels heavier than habit, because habit can change while identity feels fixed for life.

Let us test that identity against your own years, because you lived the experiment even if you never named it.

Recall the yard at half past three when you were small, before the drawer taught the hour to call. You ran. You argued. You forgot food for hours when play was good. No sweet ran that play. Recall the first sweet you ever took for love or pay. You did not demand it from nature. It was given, praised, repeated, until the loop learned its timing. The tooth you call sweet was trained tooth by tooth, dose by dose, pay packet by pay packet. What feels born was built.

Watch how the supposed trait follows the loop rather than leads it. When the loop runs hot, the trait looks strong. You call yourself a sweet person in the very months you eat most sweet. When life for a stretch runs on real meals, sleep, movement, the trait goes quiet and you forget to mention it. A born trait does not go quiet when the schedule changes. A fed pattern does. The loop creates the very hunger it then points at as proof you were always hungry.

Ask the two questions that settle it. If chubby-kid fate wrote your evenings, why did the same child run hours without thought of sweet when play absorbed him? If a sweet tooth drove the packet, why does the tooth ache loudest in the exact hours the last dose set swinging, and lie silent on mornings after plain days? Only honest answers count. The pattern points one way. The loop comes first. The identity follows after, like smoke after fire.

I write this without a scrap of scorn for the child you were or the adult you are. That child was lovable and is still lovable. The packet never made you you. It only ever rented your evenings and then told you the rent was your character. Return the character to its owner. You are the giver who carried hard days, the walker home from the bakery holding a hand, the worker who closed the inbox. The rest was translation, and translations can be corrected.

### Wanting Is Not Liking

There remains one last knot in the pay story, and untying it sets many readers blinking. You say you like it. The trap needs that word. If you like it, pay feels fair. So let us test like against your own last packet, bite by bite, because your mouth already knows what your story denies.

Take the last ordinary evening packet you finished. Not the remembered bright first bite from years ago, but last Tuesday, last Friday, the one whose wrappers you remember. First square bright. Second square less. Fourth square chew and swallow. Eighth square standing at the counter, scrolling, hardly tasting, hand moving while mind is elsewhere. The final square eaten not for brightness but to finish, to close, to stop the packet calling from the counter. Tell me plainly. Which square did you like? The first two, perhaps. And the rest? You did not like them. You wanted them while barely tasting them.

That split is not imagination and it is not weakness. When the body's own small wanting receiver is blocked in experiments, people want high-calorie food less yet report liking it just the same. I state this in plain words and leave it there, with no suggestion about medicines and no claim beyond what it proves. Wanting and liking run on separate lines. The loop rides the wanting line. That is why you can finish a packet you stopped enjoying halfway, then open the cupboard again. The hand wants what the mouth no longer loves.

Run that finding back through every pay evening and watch the story thin. A genuine pay is liked while it is wanted. This pay is wanted after it stops being liked. A genuine comfort comforts while it lasts. This comfort coats while it lasts and thins patience after. A genuine lift lifts. This lift borrows, as you have already seen in the breakroom hour, and bills the same day. What you called pay was wanting wearing pay clothes.

Does that picture sadden you? Let it gladden you instead. If pay were real, leaving it would cost. Since wanting merely posed as liking, leaving costs nothing real. The packet never loved back. It only ever wanted back. You mistook its tug for your pleasure, the way a tired parent mistakes a child's midnight cry for hunger when the child only wants holding. Hold the packet to the light of that last joyless square and the verdict lands by itself. There is nothing to miss in a pleasure that stops halfway and drags you through the rest.

### SUMMARY

- The bakery morning was warm because people made it warm, not because the packet loved you.
- Tonight's pay packet never paid. Two bright bites borrowed the evening and billed you in coating and short patience.
- A different prize never closes the pay office. No wage was ever owed because nothing was taken that sweet must repay.
- Refusing the sweet never refuses the cook. Stay, eat real food hungry, praise the hand, and love stays where it always lived.
- Sweet-tooth identity follows the loop rather than leads it. The child ran hours without sweet when play absorbed him.
- Many share this loop on the same questionnaires across countries, so the pattern is shared, not a unique flaw.
- Wanting runs apart from liking. You finished packets you had stopped enjoying, which proves the tug was never pleasure.
