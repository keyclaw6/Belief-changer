Chapter 1
THE INVITATION YOU CAN TRUST

You can stop eating sugar in a way that is easy, permanent and takes no willpower at all — and you find that hard to believe — read on.

I understand why you doubt it. We have all been conned before by promises that melted by Tuesday. We have all paid for plans that blamed us when the plan failed. You hold this book because some part of you suspects the whole approach was wrong, not you. I promise you this is different. I will show you, step by step, why quitting BAD SUGAR is gain and not loss, and why the line around it is clear.

IN THIS CHAPTER: the kitchen shelf, the soup tin, the morning labels, the heavy afternoon, the aching teeth at midnight.

*Leaving BAD SUGAR behind means gain and not loss because BAD SUGAR never gave you anything.*

### The Shelf That Talks Back

We start where we live. Not in a clinic. Not in a lecture. In the kitchen, with the cupboard door open and the light on.

Do what I ask as an investigator, not as a believer. Pull out five ordinary things most of us keep without thinking: a tin of soup, a loaf of bread, a pot of yoghurt, a box of cereal, a bottle of ketchup. Turn them around. Look at the small print where the ingredients hide. We did this together with readers in kitchens up and down the country, and the same laugh came out, short and shocked. Sugar. Sugar. Sugar again. Under half a dozen sweet-sounding names, dose after dose, where no sweet taste was even promised.

Take the soup first. A plain tomato soup, nothing festive, nothing marked as a treat. Turn the tin. There, midway down, sits sugar, tucked between starch and flavouring. We bought it for lunch, not for a hit. Yet there is the dose, quiet and legal. Now the bread. Soft sliced bread that promises wholesomeness on the front. Turn the pack. Sugar again, plus a cousin with a clever name to split the amount across two lines. We toast two slices for breakfast and call it sensible. The loop calls it a start.

Lift the yoghurt pot sold as light and kind. The label shows more sugar per spoon than many puddings our grandmothers saved for Sundays. The cereal box shouts about whole grain on the front and whispers about syrup in the back. The ketchup we squirt without measuring carries spoon after spoon across a week. None of these were bought in a moment of weakness. They were bought on a Tuesday, by a responsible adult, with a list. That is the point I want you to hold. Doses do not only arrive in birthday cake. They ride inside ordinary foods, meal after meal, training the palate to expect a sweet note where none belongs.

Look closer and the same load wears different coats. Glucose. Fructose. Syrup. Dextrose. Concentrate. The syllables change; the effect on appetite stays. I am not asking you to memorise a dictionary. I am asking you to see the pattern once, with your own eyes, in your own kitchen. When five staples all carry it, the idea that we choose each dose on our own starts to wobble. What stands in its place is simpler and kinder: we were surrounded.

“How did I not see it?” That is the voice I hear. “I thought I liked sweet things. I did not know sweet things were put into everything.”

You did not miss it because you are careless. We missed it because we were conned. The trap works best when it looks normal. When every shelf carries it, the abnormal starts to feel like the norm. We call it groceries. We call it stocking up. We call it feeding the family. The words are warm. The contents are doses.

Stand there a moment longer. Count the packets, the bars, the bottles bought for “the children” that we finish ourselves at ten at night. Notice the strange arithmetic of our kitchens: the more we buy to be safe, the faster it goes. The box that should last a month lasts four days. The bottle that should sit in the door stands empty by Friday. We tell ourselves we lack character, but look at what we do at work, with money, with children, with trouble. We hold our lives together all day. Then a packet opens and something else seems to take the hand.

Look at the pattern honestly. Why do we hide the wrappers? Why do we eat the last biscuits standing up, fast, before anyone comes in? Why do we promise ourselves, while the taste is still in the mouth, that tomorrow will be different? We do not do that with real meals. We sit. We notice hunger. We eat. We stop. Nobody hides a plate of stew.

You will hear your own objection, and I want to hear it with you. “But I like it. Surely I get something from it.” Keep that thought. Hold it up to the light. I am not asking you to believe me yet. I am asking you to look with me, as one adult to another, at what is actually on the shelf and what it actually does.

Before we go further, we need clean words, because muddled words keep the trap alive. When people say sugar they mean everything from an apple to a frosted doughnut, and that blur is useful to the people who sell doses. We will not use blurred words here.

> BAD SUGAR means free and added sugars in any name plus the ultra-processed fat-sugar fast-absorbing foods built to retrigger the loop: candy, chocolate bars, ice cream, pastries, cookies, cake, doughnuts, sugary drinks, sweet snacks, sweetened yoghurts and cereals, sauces and breads where sugar rides hidden, white refined starch binges eaten as sweets. Whole fruit, vegetables, unrefined staples eaten as real meals are not BAD SUGAR and are free.
> When this book says sugar, take it to mean BAD SUGAR.

Read that box twice. It is the line we will hold for the whole book. Inside the line sits the stuff built to pull you back. Outside the line sits real food you can trust, including whole fruit and plain staples eaten as meals. The body can cope with an accidental crumb. What it cannot cope with is the belief back. Guard the belief and the rest follows. That image will return when you need it: a belt exists for accidents, not for driving erratically.

### What The Body Pays

Now look at what those doses actually do once they are inside us, because the shelf only tells half the story. The other half is written in the body by three o’clock.

We know the pattern too well. Mid-afternoon, a clamp tightens low in the stomach. Not honest hunger. A nagging pinch that says something must be eaten right now. We answer with something quick and sweet. For twenty minutes the fog lifts a little. Then a brick seems to pass through us, slow and heavy. The gut turns tense and twisty. The head goes dull. Hands feel a touch shaky on the keyboard. We reach again, because what else do we know? By evening the box is empty and pleasure left hours ago. We ate past taste, past comfort, into mechanical chewing while staring at nothing.

Then night. You know the night I mean. Lying awake after a day of sweet hits, mouth dry and sticky, teeth aching with a dull throb that makes you press the tongue against them to check they are still there. A fright runs through: what am I doing to my teeth? What am I doing to myself? In the morning the tongue feels coated, the stomach feels tender, the head feels wrapped in cotton. We promise to be good today. By three the clamp returns.

Watch a single Tuesday without judging it. Morning starts coated. The tongue feels thick. Water helps for minutes. By eleven the head feels a touch slow, the kind of slow we blame on sleep or age or weather. We answer with a biscuit and tea with two sugars. Lift, then dip. Lunch passes. At two the eyelids grow heavy. At three the drawer opens almost by itself. Hands move while the mind watches, half annoyed, half resigned. Another bar. Another packet. Another promise that this is the last one today.

Evening tells the rest without mercy and without lying. Supper ends, yet the meal does not close. Something calls from the cupboard, not as hunger but as a pull toward the bright packet. First bites taste vivid. Bites five to fifteen taste of nothing much, eaten fast, hardly chewed, while scrolling or watching. Wrappers gather. The stomach feels stretched and still unsatisfied. Sleep comes late and thin. At two in the morning the mouth is dry, the teeth throb dully, the gut churns. Morning returns coated again. Same shelf. Same drawer. Same promise.

Place those pictures beside the ceiling numbers for a moment. A tenth of daily energy. Around fifty grams. Aim lower for further gain. Ordinary intake at double and triple that. Seventeen teaspoons a day as a national average across the ocean. No single spoon caused the clamp or the brick or the midnight throb. The pattern caused them, repeated daily, year after year. Pressure creeps. The liver labours. Appetite static grows. Enamel softens. Each effect arrives quietly, then all at once in the mirror and the chair and the dentist’s lamp.

I describe this flatly because it is flat fact, not poetry. The same sweet hit that lifts for minutes taxes for hours. Public guidance draws a ceiling most of us never heard until too late: keep these sugars under a tenth of daily energy, around fifty grams, with further gain below half that. In Britain and America ordinary intake runs at twice to triple that ceiling. In America the average sits near seventeen teaspoons a day from added sugars alone. That is not a scolding. It is a measure of how far the normal shelf has drifted from what any body was built to meet.

What does the tax collect? Start with the mouth. Sugar on teeth feeds plaque acid that softens the surface toward decay. Dentists call decay the most common noncommunicable condition on earth, and any parent who has held a child through a filling knows why that matters. The same treat that feeds the loop feeds acid against enamel. The tooth gets no vote.

Move inward. The lift that feels like energy behaves in the body like deferred load. Added sugars in quantity arrive fast, shove blood pressure upward over time, overload the liver’s quiet work, and scramble the signals that tell us we have had enough. Appetite stops behaving like a messenger and starts behaving like static. We eat and remain unsatisfied. We finish and want the start again. That is why the afternoon needs a top-up and the top-up guarantees another afternoon just like it.

Do you see the shape? The hit borrows and charges. It never settles the account.

I know fear rises here. It should. Teeth, pressure, liver, appetite — these are not small things. Let me be plain with you as a guide must be: do not make this change because fear pushes you. Fear fades within days. Fear makes quitting feel like running from a stick. I want you to move because you see clearly and gain. The hard facts have done their job once you have seen them. Now let them step back. What carries you forward is not dread of what stays, but sight of what leaves and what returns: clear mornings, a quiet drawer at work, a meal that closes, a mouth that wakes clean.

Ask yourself three honest questions, and answer as you would to a friend you respect. When the afternoon clamp comes, does a sweet hit ever carry you through to supper satisfied, or does it call for another within the hour? When you eat the evening box, do the last bites taste as bright as the first, or do you finish past pleasure because stopping feels stranger than continuing? When you wake coated and foggy, do you feel restored, or do you feel like someone who paid out and got nothing back? Only honest answers count. You already know them. The hit borrows and charges. It never pays.

Here is my verdict on this one point, short so you can hold it: the cost is real, the lift is lent.

### Nothing To Lose

If the cost is real, why do we hesitate? Because we believe quitting means loss. We picture birthdays without cake, cinemas without chocolate, tired days with nothing to soften them. The trap’s cleverest lie is not that BAD SUGAR is harmless. It is that BAD SUGAR is precious. Take it away and life goes grey. Keep it and at least you have something.

That lie deserves a fair hearing, because you have lived inside it for years. So let us try it in court.

Suppose I told you there is a way to step away from BAD SUGAR that leaves birthdays bright, workdays steady and evenings calm. Suppose the method asks no strain, no counting, no white-knuckled afternoons. Suppose thousands of heavy daily eaters, people who hid wrappers just like we did, found they could stop and never miss it. Would you say, “No thank you, I prefer the clamp and the brick and the midnight teeth”? Of course you would not. You would say, “Show me.”

That is all I ask at this stage. Listen as a reader who has been conned before: you have nothing to lose and everything to gain.

Try the risk the other way round, as a practical adult. What happens if you read this book to the end and find nothing in it true for you? You lose a few evenings. Life goes on unchanged. No money beyond this copy. No humiliation. No promise broken, because no promise was asked too soon. Now turn it. What happens if the argument holds and you see the loop from the outside for the first time? Mornings change shape. Drawers go quiet. Meals close. Bills shrink. The clamp loosens its schedule. That asymmetry is why I press you to keep reading. The downside is tiny. The upside touches every day you have left.

You say, “I have read books before. I felt hope, then felt foolish.” I do not blame you for guarding hope. Guard it. Spend it carefully here, a chapter at a time. Test one claim tonight. Open the cupboard and count how many staples list a sweet alias. Tomorrow, watch the clock between a sweet hit and the next pull. Just watch and note. If the book is wrong, your notes will prove it. If the book is right, your notes will prove that too, and proof from your own kitchen outweighs any paragraph of mine.

Ask three plain questions while you watch. Does the thing you reach for at three ever let you forget food until supper, or does it remind you of food within the hour? Do the last bites of a night box ever match the first two for brightness, or do they fade into chewing for its own sake? Do you wake lighter after a heavy sweet evening, or heavier, duller, thirstier? You know the answers because you have lived them. I only arrange them in a row so they cannot hide behind each other. The verdict writes itself in your own hand: borrowed lift, certain bill.

Feel how light that sentence is when you test it. What do you risk by reading on? A few hours. What do you stand to gain if this is true? Every afternoon back. Every evening back. Money back. Teeth, pressure, liver, appetite no longer taxed for nothing. No loss is demanded of you in advance. You are not asked to throw a single packet away today. You are asked to follow an argument and watch your own experience against it.

“I’ve heard grand promises before,” you say. “Nothing about sugar is simple. I’ve failed diets. I know myself.” I honor that doubt. Doubt is intelligence when you have been sold cures that blamed you. Bring your doubt with you. Use it. Check every claim against your shelf, your afternoons, your nights. If a sentence does not match your life, mark it and keep reading. If it does match, let it stand.

There is a reason I speak with such confidence. The method in this book is counter-brainwashing, nothing more and nothing less. For years we were taught, sip by sip and ad by ad, that sweet hits belong in every part of the day. That teaching installed a belief, and the belief installed the want. Remove the teaching and the want collapses. No strain is needed to avoid something you no longer want. You do not need a strong character to leave a room you no longer wish to sit in.

This is why the contract sounds impossible until you try it. You can leave BAD SUGAR behind easily, immediately and permanently.

Read that sentence again slowly, as lived experience, not as slogan. The first word means no fight in the drawer, no hourly bargaining, no counting of days. The second means starting now, not after some distant milestone. The third means no reopening of the question, no “just for Christmas,” no lifelong tug. Three words that contradict everything diets taught us, yet every one of them follows once the benefit illusion dies. If there is nothing to lose, there is nothing to miss. If there is nothing to miss, there is nothing to fight.

There is no sacrifice here, only gain. I say that carefully, because sacrifice is the diet word that kept us trapped. Diets asked us to mourn a pleasure while still believing in it. This book asks the opposite: see that the pleasure was lent and taxed, then walk away without mourning. Mourning would prove the belief intact. Clear sight proves it gone.

### Dinner Without The Packet

What do we eat, then, if the packet goes? This fear stops many readers before they start. They picture empty plates, flat chewing, a life of boiled greens while others feast. Let that picture come, so we can replace it with a real dinner.

Picture your favorite real meal when you were truly hungry. Not a craving pinch. Honest hunger after work or a walk. You sit. The food is hot. You notice the first bites the way you notice music you love. You eat. At some point, without lessons or apps, you feel fullness arrive like a quiet hand on the shoulder. You stop. Satisfaction stays. Taste stays bright because no sugar film sits over it.

When did BAD SUGAR ever do that for you? It never closed a meal. It opened another. That is the difference between food that feeds and doses that loop.

I make you this set-piece promise now, and I will keep it for the whole book: Eat as much of your favorite real food as you want, whenever you want it, and leave BAD SUGAR alone.

Walk through a market in your head with me. Stalls of bread with crust that cracks. Fish on ice. Potatoes with earth still on them. Greens that snap. Cheese with a rind. Apples with a crunch that sprays. None of it needs a label paragraph to explain itself. You can picture a meal from it without a manual: soup that steams, a roast that fills the kitchen, a plate you finish and push away with a nod. That nod matters. It is the body saying enough, clearly, without static.

Compare that nod with the packet’s ending. Packets do not nod. They rustle. They show empty corners that accuse. They leave the mouth wanting water and the stomach wanting more at the same time, a contradiction no real meal produces. Real meals have a beginning, a middle and a close. Doses have a beginning and then a repeat. Once you feel that difference in your own evenings, you cannot unfeel it. The packet stops looking like food and starts looking like what it is: a loop in bright dress.

Bring your own history to this, not mine. Think of a meal you still remember with warmth — who cooked it, who sat near, what was said. Was the warmth in the sugar on top, or in the people, the hunger met, the hour unhurried? Think of a tired day made better by food. Was it the packet that steadied you, or the pause, the sitting, the hot plate? I do not answer for you. You answer, and your answers gather into one quiet line: food feeds, doses loop.

Take it as lived direction, not as diet talk. As much means as much. Whenever means whenever. Your favorite means your favorite — the stew, the roast, the fish, the potatoes, the cheese, the fruit after, cooked the way you like it, eaten until you are satisfied. No weighing. No window. No forbidden hour. Hunger makes sense. Meals come to a close. Taste brightens once the filter lifts. This is not a replacement system to fill a void. It is the baseline your body returns to on its own once the loop stops shouting over it.

“But fruit is sweet,” you say. “What about bread and potatoes?” Hold the line we boxed. Whole fruit, vegetables and plain staples eaten as real meals are outside the line. They come with water, fibre and bulk that slow them and let fullness speak. BAD SUGAR is the other thing: the added sugars in any name and the ultra-processed fat-sugar foods built to retrigger the loop. One closes meals. The other reopens them. Your own evenings prove it. When did an apple ever pull you to the cupboard at midnight for six more apples?

Our goal in this book is not mere holding back as a behavior to admire. Our goal is an emotion you can feel in advance and then live inside: to be happy to be free. Picture it without forcing it. A steady two o’clock with the drawer quiet. A kitchen aisle walked past without argument. A morning mouth clean. A meal closed. No count. No reopened decision. When a sweet thought crosses the mind, it arrives already labeled for what it is, and the feeling that follows is lightness that you no longer obey it.

You are the investigator here, not the pupil. I will not ask you to take my word for any of this. Weigh your own evidence this week while you read. Notice which foods pull for more and which let you stop. Notice when hunger feels clean and when it feels edgy and false. Notice the difference between the first two bites and the last ten. Bring me your findings as you turn the pages. The method only works if you own the conclusion inside a frame I hold steady for you.

### Three Agreements Before We Start

Before we look closer together, I want three simple agreements from you. They are small. They protect the work we are about to do. All you have to do is follow all the instructions.

Keep these three in sight as we go. When doubt knocks — and it will, because old teaching does not leave quietly — meet it with the first agreement. You need not settle the whole case today. You need only stay open one more chapter. When impatience knocks and says act now, prove now, meet it with the second. Observation first, movement second. Sight first, steps second. When gloom knocks and paints quitting as grey, meet it with the third. You are not marching toward loss. You are reading toward gain. Three small postures, repeated daily while you read, hold the door open for the larger sight to enter.

Write them on a card if that helps. Keep the card where you read. Glance at it when the old voice starts its tape. No other preparation is needed. No shopping. No clearing of cupboards. No announcement to anyone. The work happens in how you see, not in what you move in the kitchen. That is why these agreements come first and why they stay with you to the last page.

Read each one slowly. Let each land before you move to the next.

1. KEEP AN OPEN MIND
You do not have to believe it yet, just keep reading.

2. DON'T TRY TO QUIT BAD SUGAR YET
Keep eating exactly as normal while you read.

3. START WITH EXCITEMENT, NOT DREAD
You are escaping, not losing anything.

I know the second agreement surprises you. “Keep eating? While reading a book about stopping?” Yes. Deliberately. If you force a halt now, while the belief still stands, every page will feel like deprivation. You will read through clenched teeth. You will test yourself instead of testing the argument. I want your mind open and your habits untouched until sight does the work. When sight is complete, stopping will not feel like effort.

The third agreement matters most. Dread closes the mind I need open. We have been trained to meet any change in eating with gloom, as if a punishment were coming. No punishment is coming. What is coming is the removal of the clamp, the brick, the twisty gut, the midnight teeth. Meet the reading as you would meet good news. Sit a little straighter. Expect to gain.

Do you feel the pushback? “Instructions? I don’t follow instructions. I’m an adult.” I respect that. That is why these are not orders from above. They are the conditions under which clear thinking happens. An open mind lets new evidence in. An unchanged habit lets you observe without panic. A willing mood lets you feel the gain instead of rehearsing loss. All you have to do is follow all the instructions, and the rest unfolds.
