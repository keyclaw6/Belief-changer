Chapter 3

WHAT DID IT EVER DO FOR YOU

Last night. Not the worst cigarette, not the best one. Just the recent one, the one you can still taste if you try.

You had finished what you were doing. The plate was cleared or the work was done or the road had paused for a moment. You stepped to the door or the yard or the window, you lit, you drew, you stood a while. Then it was finished and you went back inside.

IN THIS CHAPTER: the last cigarette and its moment, the dinner table and the coffee, the scales tipped and emptied.

*Forget for a moment what it costs you and ask what it ever gave you.*

The Bargain You Think You Struck

I know what you are thinking, because we all thought it.

You are thinking, it must do something for me. I pay for it. I plan for it. I stand in rain for it. I hide the smell from people I love for it. I cough for it. No man does all that for nothing.

That is fair. It sounds like common sense. If you hand over money and time and breath, day after day, year after year, there must be something coming back.

We tell ourselves we would have stopped long ago if there were nothing in it. We say, I like it. It helps me. It belongs with my coffee, my meal, my break, my evening. Take away the health talk and the money talk and there is still this solid thing left. It does something for me.

Let me ask you plainly, as one smoker to another. What?

Not what does it cost. We know what it costs. Not whether the cost is larger than the pleasure. That is the wrong question, and it keeps you arguing forever, because you can always answer, yes, it harms me, but I enjoy it. Every smoker has said that. I said it.

If a shop sold you a stick that did nothing at all, would you queue for it in rain, leave warm rooms for it, and call the queue pleasure? If you stood in that queue yourself, would you call it choice or con?

The true question is smaller and much harder. Leave harm aside entirely. Leave money and cough and smell aside. Look only at the other side of the scales. What good is there at all?

If a man sold you a bottle and said it would make your food taste richer and your coffee deeper and your rest fuller, you would not ask whether the bottle also poisoned you. You would first ask, did it do what he promised? Did the food taste richer? Did the coffee go deeper? Did the rest restore you? If it did nothing of what he promised, you would not weigh harm against benefit. There would be no benefit to weigh.

That is where we are. We have seen the bars. Now we must look at the cell and ask what the jailer ever gave us for staying inside.

Take One And Weigh It

Do it now with that recent one. Not your first, not your worst, not some romantic memory from years ago. The recent ordinary one.

Bring back the moment around it. Where were you. What had just finished. What were you feeling before the hand moved. Full or tired or pleased or flat. Who was with you. What sounds were in the room.

Stay a little longer in the before, because the before tells the truth.

You had just put down the fork or the pen or the wheel. There was a small pause, the kind every life is full of. Work done leaves a loosening in the shoulders. A meal done leaves a warmth low in the body. An evening at rest leaves a quiet that needs nothing added. For a man who never smoked, that pause is complete as it stands. He breathes out, he looks around, he is ready for whatever comes next.

For you, something else entered the pause. A thin stirring. Not yet a thought. A faint dryness at the back of the throat, a lightness in the fingers, a sense that the pause was unfinished. The hand remembered before the mind chose. It patted the pocket, it felt the shape, it brought out pack and lighter in the old order. You may have told yourself you fancied one, you had earned one, you needed a moment. Under those words there was only the small signal: time since the last one had grown long enough for the want to be heard again.

Now follow the during, draw by draw, and watch honestly.

You stepped out or leaned to the window. The lighter flared. The tip glowed. The first draw scratched faintly, then settled. Shoulders dropped a fraction. The stirring quieted. For a minute the evening seemed kinder, the head a touch clearer, the body a touch more at rest. That minute is the whole case for smoking, held in one minute. Remember it well, because we will look at it closely.

Then watch what happened inside that same cigarette. By the middle draws the lift had already thinned. You were no longer tasting anything new. You were drawing because the stick was lit, because the pause was still open, because the hands needed something to do until the end. The smoke grew hotter, duller, faintly bitter at the back of the mouth. You may have looked at the burning length and wished it longer, or shorter, without knowing which. The last draws brought no fresh pleasure, only the smell strengthening on fingers and clothes, the faint heaviness in the chest, the wish to be finished and back inside.

Now stay for the after, because smokers rarely look at the after.

You pressed the end out, you breathed the last smoke away, you went back to the room. What were you carrying that you had not carried before you lit? A fuller stomach? No. A clearer head? No, the head was as it had been an hour before, only less stirred. A solved problem? A warmer friendship? Or only a mouth that needed rinsing, fingers that needed washing, clothes that carried the smell into the warm room, and a short flatness already gathering again somewhere low, promising that in an hour the hand would remember again?

Keep that shape in mind: faint stirring, brief quiet, dull finish, faint stirring again. That is one ordinary cigarette seen whole, without stories.

Now take the cigarette out of the picture and leave everything else exactly as it was. The finished work still finished. The evening still quiet. The pause still yours. What did the moment lack that only smoke could supply?

Did the air lack flavour until you filled it with smoke? Did the rest lack rest until you set fire to it? Did the finished thing lack finish until you paid for the finish with a light?

Ask it a second way. What did the cigarette add that a man who never smoked would have missed in that same moment? He would have stood in the same doorway. He would have breathed the same evening. He would have felt the same easing after work done. Would he have turned to you and said, something is missing here, if only I had smoke in my mouth?

You know he would not. We know it, because we were once that man. Before we ever learned to inhale, evenings finished without it, meals closed without it, breaks restored without it, and nothing felt absent.

Ask it a third way, the hardest. When the cigarette was finished, what were you left with that you did not have before you lit it? A fuller stomach? A clearer head? A solved problem? A warmer friendship? Or only the quieting of the want to light, which had not been there until the last cigarette began to fade?

Keep that recent one in mind. We are going to eat with it.

The Dinner Table

Supper is over.

The plates are pushed back. The food was good. You can still taste it. The kitchen is warm. Someone pours coffee. The cup steams. The smell rises, dark and full. Your hand moves a fraction toward the pocket where the pack sits, before thought has finished. After food. With coffee. The paired moment. The one smokers call impossible without.

Stay there with me. Do not argue yet. Look.

The meal closed. That pleasure is complete. Meat and bread and sauce did their work on tongue and stomach. No smoke helped them. You did not lift a cigarette between bites to make the bites taste more. The taste was the food's own. The fullness is the body's own. The ease of a hunger met is older than any pack.

The coffee is poured. That pleasure is complete. Hold the cup. Feel the heat through the china. Watch the steam. Take the first sip, bitter and hot, then mellow. Your mouth knows exactly what to do with it. Your nose knows its smell. No smoke taught coffee to taste like coffee. Children of smokers grow up loving the smell of coffee from the kitchen without ever lighting. Non-smokers finish the same meal, drink the same cup, sigh the same satisfied sigh, and never feel a gap where smoke should be.

So where does the hand's small movement come from? Not from the meal. The meal asks for nothing more. Not from the coffee. The coffee is whole as it stands. The movement comes from pairing. Meal, then coffee, then cigarette, repeated hundreds of times until the sequence feels like one thing. The hand expects because the hand remembers, not because the mouth needs.

Think how the pairing was learned, because seeing the learning takes away its authority.

No man was born wanting smoke after food. The first times you smoked after a meal, smoke laid on fullness felt heavy and dull. You persisted because others lit and to refuse felt like standing outside. The pleasure belonged to food and talk, and smoke only stood nearby while it happened.

Then repetition did its slow work. Plate pushed back, cup filled, lighter flicked, hundreds of evenings in the same order, until the third step felt promised by the first two. That lean is not hunger. It is memory of sequence.

That is what stirs in your fingers now as the coffee steams. Not a need that coffee cannot meet. A learned cue. The end of the plate has become a bell, and your hand moves toward the pocket at the ring of a bell you helped hang.

Watch it from inside and you can catch the bell ringing.

There is a half-second, before any words about wanting, when the eyes drop to the pocket, the fingers flex, the chest leans a fraction toward the door. Thought has not yet spoken. The body has already rehearsed. Then thought arrives late and dresses the rehearsal as reason. Coffee tastes better with one. I cannot finish without one. This is my moment. Those sentences feel like decisions. They are echoes after the bell. If the bell had never been hung, those sentences would never come.

Watch what happens if you leave the third part out. The meal still closes. The coffee still warms. The talk still runs. The chair still holds you. Nothing in the food turns grey. Nothing in the cup empties. The pleasure of eating and drinking stands exactly as it stood for every non-smoker at every table since food was cooked. The only thing that stirs is a faint expectation in the fingers, a learned cue that says now, and the cue is not proof of a gift. It is proof of a pairing.

I lived inside that pairing. We all did. Then one evening I drank the coffee without and found it had never needed the smoke. The smoke had only been sneaking a ride on the coffee's pleasure and claiming the credit.

Keep your pack where it is. I am not asking you to test yourself. I am only asking you to see the table clearly. The food fed you. The coffee pleased you. The cigarette added nothing the moment lacked.

Heaven For A Moment

You will tell me I am ignoring what you feel.

You will say, you do not understand, the moment I light up I am in heaven. I love smoking even while I hate being a smoker. I feel shitty afterwards, yes, the taste, the shame, the sense of being tied again, but the light itself, those first draws, that is real. You cannot tell me I do not feel what I feel.

I will not tell you that. I felt it too.

There is a lift when you light after hunger for it. Shoulders drop. The edge softens. For a minute the world feels kinder. Call it heaven if that is your honest word. I am not here to mock your honesty or to tell you that you lied about your own mouth.

I am here to ask what that heaven was.

Was it a gift laid on top of a neutral moment, lifting you above a non-smoker? Or was it the ending of a low that had been growing since the last one faded, lifting you briefly back toward where the non-smoker had stood all along?

Think of your recent one again. Before you lit, there was a thin want. You may have called it stress or hunger or need for a break, but under the name there was the same faint gnaw. Then you lit, the gnaw quieted, and the quiet felt like pleasure. Afterwards came the flat taste, the smell on fingers, the faint disgust with yourself for needing it again. Heaven, then shitty afterwards. Love the puff, hate the life. That is exactly how we all lived it.

Look closer at those first draws you call heaven, because heaven that lasts a minute deserves a close look.

The first draw after hours without feels kind only by contrast, the way taking off tight shoes feels like heaven in the evening. The relief is real as feeling and false as proof, because the discomfort was worn all day by choice.

And notice how narrow the heaven is. It lives in the first draws and then thins into mere smoking, hotter and more bitter, smoked to finish rather than to enjoy. A true pleasure does not need to be finished. Food enjoyed leaves fullness. Friendship enjoyed leaves warmth. This heaven leaves only a short quiet and a stale mouth, then the same want rebuilt.

What you felt at the light was real as feeling. It was not real as benefit. It was relief from a discomfort its predecessors had created, mistaken for a gift because the discomfort wore everyday names.

Strip away the names and what remains is simple. A momentary easing, credited to the thing that caused the need for easing. That is not plenty FOR you. That is plenty TO you while you called it heaven.

The Reward For Everything

There is a second heaven, quieter, and it holds many of us tighter than the first.

Cigarettes were my reward for well, almost everything. Teaching a class. Finishing a story. Finishing a paragraph. Driving five hundred miles. Driving to the shop. Washing the car. Paying the bills. Putting the children to bed. Good news and bad news. Work done and work avoided.

You know the list because you have your own. The little parties through the day. The full stop at the end of every sentence. Done that, now this. Earned it.

I do not doubt the feeling. The work ends, the hand reaches, the draw comes, and something in you says, well done, this is mine. The party is real as feeling. The question is the same. Who gave the party?

Did the cigarette celebrate the finished paragraph, or did the finished paragraph celebrate itself and the cigarette step in front to take the bow? The relief of a task done, the loosening after effort, the pride of a page complete, those belong to the work and the worker. A non-smoker who finishes the same paragraph feels the same loosening, breathes the same deeper breath, smiles the same small smile, without lighting. The joy was already in the moment before smoke touched it.

Watch the small festivals one by one and give each back to its owner.

You finish a paragraph that fought you for an hour. The sentence locks at last. There is a loosening between the shoulders, a wish to sit back a moment and feel the work hold. That loosening is the body's own applause. You lit into the middle of it for years and called the applause the cigarette.

Watch how widely the reward attaches and you will see it cannot be the reward's true source. The same stick rewards the long drive and the short errand, the hard class and the easy chore, boredom and excitement, hunger and fullness. No true reward fits everything. Food does not reward being full. Rest does not reward being rested. Drink does not reward thirst already quenched. Only a want that returns on its own clock can attach itself to every finished act, because every finished act leaves a pause, and every pause leaves room for the want to be heard. The bell rings, the hand moves, the pause is credited to the bell.

The party was your life finishing things well. The cigarette only charged admission to moments that were already yours.

You do not lose celebration by seeing this. Meals still close. Coffee still steams. Work still ends with its own satisfaction. The evening pause still holds its quiet. The non-smoker celebrates everything you celebrate, fully, without paying a fee at each small festival. Seeing the fee for what it is does not empty life. It returns the party to its rightful owner.

What Good Is There At All

We come to the switch. Turn it slowly, because everything after this turns on it.

Until now you have asked, does it do me more harm than good? That question keeps the trap alive, because harm can always be answered with, but I like it, but it helps me, but it is my reward. Harm argues with fear. Fear fades. Liking remains.

Ask instead, what good is there at all? Demand a positive benefit. Not less harm. Not milder damage, but a real relief or reward.

Name one thing it does FOR you that a non-smoker lacks without it. Not TO you. TO you it does plenty. It steals breath on stairs. It empties pockets. It chains hours to a clock. It sets the hand checking and the mind counting. It lays shame over pleasure. It turns evenings into supply arithmetic. It did plenty TO you. It did nothing FOR you.

Read those two sentences again, because they hold the whole turn. It did plenty TO you. It did nothing FOR you.

Look at the TO side without flinching, but without fear either, because we are only listing what you already live.

It does TO your breath what you feel on the stairs and in the morning cough. It does TO your money what you feel each week at the till. It does TO your time what you feel in hours spent stepping out to finish something you call enjoyment. It does TO your evenings what you know as checking: the tap of the pocket, the squeeze of the pack, the calculation of enough until morning. It does TO your pride what you feel hiding the smell from people you love.

Hold all that TO on one side. It is heavy. Every smoker knows its weight.

Now hold up the other side and ask for one solid thing. Not a lifted minute that ends where the non-smoker already stood. Not a bell you learned to answer. A real relief or reward that life without could not supply.

Is there a calm it gives that calm people lack? Look at calm people. They are calm without.

Is there a taste it adds that food and coffee lack? They taste fully without. The meal closed fully for you before you ever learned to light after it, and it closes fully for millions tonight who will never light.

Is there a friendship it builds that friends lack? Friends laugh without. The talk at your table ran before the lighter flicked and runs on after the ashtray is cleared.

Is there a thought it thinks that clear heads lack? Clear heads think without. The paragraph finished itself by your work, not by fire.

Is there a rest it gives that rested bodies lack? Rested bodies wake clear without smoke to start them.

Every claimed gift, held up to the moment itself, dissolves into the moment. The situation gave it. The body gave it. The pause gave it. The drug only ended for minutes the want the last dose had planted, then planted it again.

That is why the scales can be put away. There is nothing to weigh. The one side is heavy with cost. The other side is empty. An empty side does not need balancing. It needs seeing.

QUESTION WHAT YOU THINK YOU KNOW. Ask what good there is at all.

You owe yourself that honest weighing before you go further.

You came here believing you paid for something solid. Leave this seeing with the opposite certainty. We were conned into paying for the ending of a hunger we paid to create. You were never weak. I was never weak. We were only misled about where the pleasure came from, and a misled man who sees the trick does not need to fight himself. He needs only to keep looking.

Hold that question lightly and bring it with you. When the next want speaks in the voice of reward or relief, meet it with the same words. What good is there at all? You will hear the silence where the answer should be, and in that silence the trap loosens.

SUMMARY

- The right question is not whether harm outweighs benefit, but whether any positive benefit exists at all.
- A recent ordinary cigarette, weighed against its own moment, adds nothing the moment lacked.
- Before, during and after tell one shape: faint stirring, brief quiet, dull finish, then the same want rebuilding.
- Meal and coffee are fully pleasurable on their own; the hand's expectation after dinner is pairing, not proof of a gift.
- The pairing was learned by repetition until sequence felt like need; the bell ringing in the fingers is memory, not hunger.
- The heaven felt at lighting is a real feeling of brief easing, not a lasting gift laid on top of life.
- The reward felt after finished acts belongs to the act and the pause itself, not to the smoke that stepped in front of it.
- Seen clearly, smoking does plenty TO the smoker and nothing FOR the smoker, so there is no benefit left to weigh.
