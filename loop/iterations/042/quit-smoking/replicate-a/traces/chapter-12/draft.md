Chapter 12

THE SAME PATIO, A DIFFERENT MORNING

The chair sits in the same place it always sat. Left side of the small patio, back to the fence, facing the kitchen door so the morning sun comes over the right shoulder. The paint on the arm has worn where a hand rests. The coffee mug leaves its ring on the low table. The ashtray is gone. That is the only object missing, and after a while you stop noticing the bare circle where it stood.

I sit there now most mornings with nothing in my hands but the warm mug. The air smells of wet soil and toast from the kitchen. Sparrows argue in the hedge. The day starts without any ceremony around fire. It took me years to understand how strange and how plain that would feel. Strange for a week. Plain ever after.

You come to this page with your mind made up and your body still asking its question. You have seen through the hours we walked together. Coffee to the bottom and found cleaner. Break outside with nothing lit and found whole. Meal closed and found gentle. Night among lit ones and found the talk still yours. You have named the six sentences that sound like your own reason and heard who speaks them. The doors stand shut in your mind.

And still a fair question rises. It rises in good faith, without any wish to go back.

Yes, you say. I see it clear here, sitting warm, reading steady. But does it hold in a lived body across years. Does any person live this and find the mornings stay whole. Show me one life walked all the way out, with its counts and its senses intact, and let me stand inside it before I take my own last step.

I will show you mine. Not as argument. Not as lesson. As witness.

IN THIS CHAPTER: the patio as it was, the cough that started the day, the counts that ran the years, and the same patio as it is now.

*Freedom like this does not wear thin with years; it settles until you forget you ever needed anything else.*

You Ask For A Body, Not Another Line

I welcome the question. I would distrust any reader who did not ask it.

We have talked mind to mind for many chapters. I have asked you to look at hours you know and answer plain questions about them. You have done that work honestly. A convinced mind is a fine thing. It is not yet a proved life. You want to know what happens after the seeing, when winter comes twice and three times, when money passes through the hands, when food is eaten and sleep is slept and birthdays go by, and whether any old pull still climbs up uninvited in year three or year five.

Any man can promise you a fine week. I am offering you years you can check against your own senses.

So lay argument aside for this one chapter. I will not pull apart another excuse. I will not add another proof. I will tell you what my days held when I still lit, what the first weeks without held, and what year after year since has held, with the numbers left in and the tastes left in, so you can feel the weight of a body that walked out and stayed out.

Listen the way you would listen to a neighbour over the fence who has nothing to sell. If any line here sounds like boasting, forgive me in advance. I mean it only as relief made audible. I was where your feet are now, timed to a pack, clearing my throat each dawn. I sit now where your feet will be, in the same chair, with clean air in the chest and no argument running in the head.

Ask as you read the only questions that matter for witness.

Does this man describe hours I recognise from my own life. Do his counts match the shape of my counts. Do his mornings and meals and nights sound like mine, only with the fire taken out and everything else left whole. And if his years hold without any private ache, what reason remains to believe mine would hold any different.

You already know the answers. Let the life itself speak them.

Where I Sat Then

I began the way most of us begin, to belong and to look at ease, and I stayed the way most of us stay, by the clock.

For over twenty years I lit between fifteen and twenty-five on an ordinary day, more on nights out, more on long drives, more when drink was warm and talk ran late. A pack a day is close enough for truth. Twenty folds of paper and leaf a day. One hundred and forty a week. Over seven thousand a year. Year on year. I do the sum now without pride and without flinching, only to show you I am not describing a light acquaintance from far off. I lived inside the timetable.

My patio then looked much as it looks now, and that is the point. Same chair, left of the flags, back to the fence. Same low table with its ring. The differences were small objects that ran the hour. A heavy glass ashtray that I emptied each evening and filled again by noon. A lighter that lived in the right pocket and was patted for before keys. A carton on the kitchen shelf so Thursday never caught me short. Music on low from the kitchen door in summer. The first one with coffee before the house woke, taken as thinking fuel for the day.

I called that hour my own. I believed the chair needed the lit end to complete it. I would settle, sip, light, draw, and feel the shoulders drop as the day arranged itself in my head. What I did not see then, because no one had shown me, was that the shoulders were tight from the night without, and the drop was only the tightness pausing for a few minutes before it began to climb again toward the next one.

The cough started the day before the thought did.

Every autumn and then most of the year, I woke with a rattle low in the chest and cleared it sitting on the edge of the bed. Feet not yet on the rug and the hand already reaching to the bedside table. Light first, cough after, coffee after that to wash the mouth. On cold mornings the first draw burned and I coughed harder and lit again within the hour to smooth what the first had roughened. I told myself this was only morning throat, only weather, only age creeping. I did not tell the doctor, and I did not tell my wife how often I checked the handkerchief.

The stairs told their own plain story. Twelve steps from hall to landing. I took them two dozen times a day and breathed harder at the top than a man in his forties should. I rested my hand a second on the newel post and called it pausing to think. I knew the smell in my coat and worked around it with the yard air and the washroom soap. I knew the taste in the mouth at noon and worked around it with mints. I knew the till receipts and worked around them by not adding the column. Seven pounds then, nine pounds then, more after, a day. Fifty a week. Two hundred and more a month. Thousands a year for the privilege of staying timed.

Evenings had their own timetable. After the meal, coffee poured, hand expecting its pair. Out to the patio while others stayed at the table, or out to the yard at other people's houses while the talk went on inside. I missed the end of stories. I came back smelling of the yard and smiled a little too bright to cover where I had been. On nights out I stood with the lit crowd under the heaters and believed the best talk lived out there. On long drives I timed the miles by burns and aired the car at the station with shirt shaken and spray used, then lit again before the slip road.

I do not write this to darken you. I write it because you lived some version of it and I want you to know my witness starts from your ground, not from some easy height.

Ask your own memory as you read mine.

Did your chair have its objects too. Did your morning begin with clearing before thinking. Did your pocket get patted before keys. Did your stairs ask more breath than they should. Did your evenings split in two, half inside with people you loved and half outside with a burning end.

If yes, then we sat in the same chair. Keep that likeness in mind when I tell you where that chair sits now.

The Weeks The Clock Ran Down

I stopped the way this book will ask you to stop, when I saw there was nothing to keep. No slow taper that made each one sacred. No props in the drawer. No counting of days on the kitchen calendar. One ordinary one with full attention on what it was, then none. I do not dwell on that hour here because your own hour stands very close and deserves its own room. I tell you only what the body did after, so you need not fear it.

The first three mornings asked for attention. Not pain. Not illness. Attention.

I woke at the usual hour and the hand moved toward the bedside table out of habit before thought caught it. The chest felt light and a little hollow, as if a meal had been missed. By mid-morning a thin edginess came and went, then came again after lunch, then in the evening when coffee was poured. It never rose above a background hum. It never stopped me working, eating, talking, sleeping. It asked to be noticed, the way a clock ticks louder in a quiet house the first night, then fades into the walls.

I met it the way I now ask you to meet any such hum. I drank my coffee to the bottom and tasted it more, not less. I took my break outside with nothing in the hands and felt the ten minutes whole from edge to edge. I ate my meals and found the close gentle, the mouth cleaner after than before. I went to bed at the usual hour and slept. On the third day the mornings felt brighter than any I could remember from the timed years, as if a window had been washed I had stopped seeing as dirty.

The cough loosened and left in stages. First week it rattled harder for a day or two as the chest cleared its backlog, then eased. Second week I woke without needing to sit and clear before standing. Third week I climbed the twelve stairs with the washing basket and noticed at the top that I had not noticed. No hand on the post. No pause to think. Breath simply there, the way breath is there for people who never timed their day.

Taste came back the way light comes back in a room when a curtain is drawn, not in a flash but all at once noticed. Coffee fuller and a little darker at the edges. Food warmer in its own smell. An apple cold from the fridge so loud in the mouth I laughed. The morning air over the fence smelling of cut grass and rain where before it had smelled faintly of last night's coat. My wife said my skin smelled of soap only, and I had not known I missed hearing that.

Money stayed where it had never stayed. The first week I kept reaching for the shop amount on Friday out of habit, then laughed and left it in the jar. Fifty there. Then a hundred. Then the jar bought a new mattress without any talk of saving or effort. I do not name the sum to tempt you with coin. Coin was never the motive and must never be. I name it so you see the counts ran backward into plenty without any other change in how we lived. Same shop. Same house. Same table. Only the daily tax gone.

The old pictures still visited for a while, and I want you to hear this plain because honesty here will save you later worry. Cup, then reach. Door, then yard. Laugh, then light. The picture would lift for a second in its old hour and pass. I did not argue with it or push it down or fill the minute with chewing or pacing or any busy hands. I looked at it, knew the speaker, smiled at how thin it sounded beside the full cup in my hand, and went on. Each visit thinner. Each gap longer. By the second month whole days passed without a single visit. By the sixth month I sometimes went a week and remembered only when something like this book crossed my mind.

No hour was dodged to buy that quiet. I drank where I always drank. I stood in yards when talk moved there. I drove the same roads. I sat after meals at the same table. I changed nothing else in life. The quiet came because the need was gone, not because the world was narrowed.

Ask the questions that close fear of the body for good.

If those first days were torment, how did I work and eat and laugh through them without any record of torment to show. If the hum proved the thing was needful, why did each week after need less, not more, until weeks passed without asking. And if the pictures proved hunger lived on, why did they thin to nothing while life stayed wide open.

They thinned because they were memory, not order. A house does not stay haunted once you see who walks the stairs.

The Same Patio Now

Come back with me to the chair where we began.

Six years have passed since that ordinary one with attention. Then seven. Then more. I have stopped counting in the way you stop counting the years since a fever broke. The chair sits left of the flags, back to the fence, facing the kitchen door. The low table holds a mug and a book and reading glasses. The bare circle where the heavy glass stood has been scrubbed by rain and sun until even I cannot find it.

I sit there through spring and summer and the mild weeks of autumn, and on bright winter mornings with a coat on, and I tell you flat what those hours hold now.

No reach. No picture lifting uninvited. No sentence arriving in my own voice to plead for one. Coffee comes and goes without any pair. Talk comes and goes without any shelter from wind sought for fire. The ten minutes are whole from edge to edge, then the day goes on.

Mornings begin without clearing. Feet on the rug, kettle on, door open to the air, breath easy on the stairs with the basket or the child or the grandchild in arms. Food tastes of itself. Drink tastes of itself. Nights out hold without any split, the whole evening in one room, the whole story heard to its end, the coat smelling of nothing but the night air on the walk home. Drives run mile by mile with the window down for pleasure, not for airing, and the car smells of itself.

The counts from those years tell their own quiet story, and I leave them in so your body can trust mine.

Thousands of mornings woken without the bedside reach. Thousands of meals closed in peace. Thousands of pounds not passed over a counter, gone into ordinary plenty no one had to strive for. Stairs climbed without thought, twelve at a time, hundreds of times a year. Coughs that never came. Hours never spent in cold yards while warm rooms went on without me. I do not list these as prizes earned by effort. I list them as hours returned. They were always mine. They are mine again, undivided.

The senses from those years tell the rest.

Rain on warm flags smelled fresh all the way through. Bread from the oven filled the kitchen to the corners. Coffee dark and clean. Apples loud. The hedge after rain. Soap on skin. My wife's hair when I leaned close. None of these arrived as wonders to marvel at daily. They arrived as the normal background of a man whose mouth and nose were no longer filmed over. After the first bright weeks I stopped marvelling, and that stopping is itself the proof. Wonder fades. Wholeness stays and is forgotten, the way clear sight is forgotten by people who see.

Once or twice a year some stray moment still brushes past. A laugh in a yard under heaters. A hand held out with easy affection. A cold evening after a hard week when the old cry would once have risen that something would take care of it. The brush comes and goes like a moth at the window. It carries no weight, no ache, no sense of missing. What rises in its place, unbidden and cheerful, is gladness that no tending is needed. I look at the held-out hand with real affection for the person and none at all for the paper, take the love, leave the paper, and find the night goes on without a seam.

I want you to feel the force of that nothing. Not a daily battle won. Not a hunger managed. Nothing. The mind does not circle. The hands do not fidget. The evening does not split. Nothing is the whole report from year after year.

Ask the last witness questions and let them answer themselves.

If freedom like this were fragile, how has it stood through deaths and births and money tight and money easy, through winter coughs that were only winter and summers long and idle, without a single hour of secret mourning. If the senses were dulled by leaving, why is every meal and cup and morning fuller in my telling than in any telling from the timed years. And if some hidden want still lived, where does it hide when thousands of mornings in the same chair have found no reach left in the hands.

It hides nowhere because it lives nowhere. What lived was timing and story, and both starved when unfed.

What This Leaves You With

Lay my years beside your shut doors and see how they fit.

You shut the talk of one with the bar you now carry. My years show that bar never needed lifting again. Offers came and went like weather and left no damp.

You shut the talk of drink and company by seeing the night was people and warmth and story. My years show nights stayed wide and glad with nothing dodged, drink where I chose, yards where talk moved, company whole.

You shut the talk of being different by hearing one story speak through many mouths. My years show my mouth was one of them, timed and coughing like yours, and now clear like yours will be.

You shut the talk of tomorrow by seeing the day that holds coffee already proves the time. My years show tomorrow never brought a better day than the day I saw, only more payments for staying timed.

You shut the two fears that are one rope by seeing neither strength nor loss remains. My years show no strength was spent and no loss was mourned, only hours returned undivided.

You shut the kind voices that counsel props and tapers and counts by holding your seeing the way you hold your name. My years show tested seeing outlasted every borrowed counsel without a single drawer of aids to show.

I did not live those years by holding myself away from something precious. I lived them by seeing there was nothing precious to hold away from, and then by living. Work done. Food eaten. Children raised. Grief met straight. Joy met full. Stairs climbed. Money kept. Mornings taken whole. That is the entire method of years, if method is even the word. Seeing held, life inhabited, gladness rising unbidden whenever the old thought brushes past and finds its answer waiting.

You stand now where I stood the evening before my own ordinary one with attention. Mind convinced. Doors shut. Body asking for its proof. You have my proof. It is not a line. It is a chair, a mug, a fence, mornings by the thousand, meals by the thousand, air in the chest on twelve stairs, taste in the mouth, coin left in the jar, nights without splitting, years without mourning. Take it as neighbour talk and weigh it against your own hours. You will find no seam where it fails to meet yours.

Very soon you will smoke one ordinary cigarette with your eyes fully open to what it is, and then you will speak your vow and step over. I will not steal that hour from its own room. I tell you only this so you go toward it light. You do not go toward loss. You go toward the same patio on a different morning, where the chair waits, the coffee waits, the sun comes over the right shoulder, and nothing else is needed.

The life is walked. The walking is easy. Yours waits now at the threshold.

SUMMARY

- A body that lit a pack a day for over twenty years now sits years clear in the same chair with no reach left in the hands.
- Mornings that began with clearing and a bedside light now begin with kettle and open door and breath easy on twelve stairs.
- Taste and smell returned as normal background, coffee fuller, food warmer, rain and bread and apples vivid, then forgotten as wholeness should be.
- Money once passed daily over a counter stayed in the jar into ordinary plenty with no other change in how life was lived.
- The first weeks asked only attention, a thin hum that faded while work and meals and sleep went on undisturbed.
- Old pictures visited as memory and thinned to nothing while every context stayed wide open, nothing dodged.
- Six years and more hold no daily battle and no secret mourning, only thousands of whole mornings and closed meals.
- Stray brushes once or twice a year carry no weight and are met with unbidden gladness at needing no tending.
- The shut doors stayed shut across births and deaths and tight money and easy summers without any door needing guard.
- The witness leaves you ready for your own ordinary one with attention and the step over that follows.
