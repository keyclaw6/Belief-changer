Chapter 4

THE SWITCH THAT FEEDS THE CURRENT

The door sighs shut behind you and the midday air hits your face. The pavement is damp. The clock above the shop across the road says twenty past twelve and your break says thirty minutes and your chest says no time at all.

You hold a sandwich you do not want in one hand and a phone that will not stop lighting in the other. The morning has been a stack of small demands. A manager moved a deadline forward. An email arrived with the wrong tone. A customer spoke to you as if you were slow. Nothing huge. Everything together. Your shoulders sit high near your ears. Your jaw aches from holding itself tight. The hand that holds the phone is already patting the jacket pocket for the shape it knows.

Later there is another room. The kitchen at home. The light too bright. Words said too fast. Someone you love says you never listen, you say they never stop, a door knocks in its frame, and silence fills the flat like smoke. Your face burns. Your hands shake a little. Before thought finishes, the hand moves again toward pack and lighter on the shelf.

IN THIS CHAPTER: the midday pavement and the clock, the kitchen after sharp words, the hand already moving and the want that returns.

*The cigarette never calmed the trouble; it only quieted for minutes the edginess smoking had planted, then handed the trouble back.*

The Fear Is Real

Let me start where you live, because I have lived there too.

You are afraid that without cigarettes the pressure will have nowhere to go. I understand that fear. It is honest. Anyone who has stood on that pavement with the clock running and felt the shoulders drop after the first draw would be afraid to lose the one thing that seemed to turn the noise down.

I am not here to tell you that your days are easy or that your nerves are imagined. Your days are heavy. Bills, bosses, children, traffic, illness in the family, the low hum of money worry that never quite stops. Arguments wound. Deadlines press. Loneliness presses in a different way. If anyone tells you stress is all in your head, they have not lived your week.

We smokers learned early to answer every weight with fire. Bad news, light one. Good news held too much excitement, light one. A row, light one. Waiting, light one. Work piling, light one. The lighter became the full stop at the end of every hard sentence. Of course the thought of facing the same sentences without the full stop feels exposed. Of course part of you says, take anything else, but do not take the off-switch.

I felt that too. We all did. The fear does not mean you are weak. It means you once found something that seemed to help and you are loyal to what helped you.

Keep that loyalty while we look closely at what helped, because loyalty deserves truth. I am not asking you to be brave yet. I am only asking you to watch one ordinary pressured hour with me and see where the calm comes from and where it goes.

The Pavement At Midday

Come back to the pavement.

You have eighteen minutes left. The sandwich sits half unwrapped. The phone shows two messages you have not opened. Your stomach is tight. Your breath is shallow. The fingers of your right hand feel light and empty, as if they are waiting for their shape.

You tell yourself you need to calm down before you go back in. You light.

Watch what happens now in present time, because the present tells the truth that memory smooths over.

The tip glows. Smoke fills the mouth, then the chest. Within seconds something shifts. The shoulders drop a fraction. The jaw loosens. The noise in the head thins. Breath comes a touch slower. For a minute the pavement feels wider. The clock seems less loud. You think, there, I can face it now. If anything stressful happens in my life, a cigarette takes care of it. That sentence arrives whole, without argument, because the body just felt it.

I do not deny that minute. The easing is real as feeling. I felt it on a thousand pavements. The shoulders do drop. The head does thin. The present does soften.

Now stay past the minute, because smokers rarely stay past the minute.

You keep drawing because the stick is lit. By the middle the lift has already thinned. The second half brings no fresh calm. The smoke grows hotter at the back of the throat. The fingers smell. The sandwich is cold. The messages are still unopened. The deadline has not moved. The manager has not softened. The customer has not apologised.

You press the end out under your shoe. You breathe out the last grey. You walk back through the door. What do you carry in that is different?

Follow yourself up the stairs, because the stairs tell what the pavement hid.

You sit. The screen wakes. You try to read the same paragraph twice. The brief softness from outside holds for a few minutes while you answer one easy message, then thins while you stare at the harder one. Your legs cross and uncross. You sip cold tea. You tap the pen. A thin impatience gathers behind the eyes, not about the message itself but about sitting itself, about staying with the hard line without a pause that burns. You tell yourself the morning is unusually trying. Look closer. The morning is ordinary. What is unusual is only the interval growing long since the last light, making ordinary effort feel heavier.

A slower breath for a few minutes, yes. And under it, the same stack, untouched. The work waits exactly where you left it. The messages wait. The tightness in the stomach, which had loosened for a minute, begins to gather again before you reach your desk. By mid-afternoon the shoulders sit high again. The hand pats again. The mind says again, I need to calm down.

Look at that shape whole. Tension, light, brief easing, dull finish, tension again. The cigarette did not meet the trouble. It met the interval between troubles and filled it with a small quiet, then left the trouble standing where it was.

Ask the simple question that cuts through loyalty to the minute. What did the smoke take care of?

Did it answer the messages? Did it move the deadline back? Did it teach the customer manners? Did it put money in the account or patience in the manager? You know it did none of those. A man who never smoked stands on the same pavement with the same messages and the same clock. He breathes the damp air, he eats, he returns. His trouble is no larger for lacking smoke. His shoulders may be tight, but they are tight from work, not from work plus a clock that says when he may ease.

You will say, but I felt calmer, and feeling calmer is something. Yes. Feeling calmer for minutes is something as feeling. The question that matters is where that calmer came from. Did it come from the trouble lifting, or from another pressure lifting that only smokers carry?

After The Sharp Words

Come into the kitchen now, because the kitchen proves it faster than the pavement.

The words are still in the air. Your face is hot. Your heart knocks. The other person has gone into the other room. The silence rings. The pack sits on the shelf by the kettle where it always sits, as if it were part of the kitchen, as if it belonged to the room the way cups belong.

Your hand moves before you decide. Lighter, tip, draw. The first draw after a row feels the kindest of all. Breath comes deeper because breath was held. Shoulders drop because they were braced. For a minute the sting thins. You think, at least I have this. At least something steadies me when people do not.

Stay with me through to the next want, because the next want tells what the kind minute was.

You finish. You rinse the mouth. You sit at the table. The row is not mended. The hot face cools, but the words remain said. The other door stays shut. What has changed between you and the person you love? Nothing, except that for ten minutes you stood with fire rather than knocking on the shut door or letting your own heart slow on its own.

Stay longer at that table, because the table shows the loop closing.

The television talks in the corner. You do not follow it. You replay the tone more than the words, and each replay tightens the same place under the ribs. You get up, you wipe a clean counter, you look out at the dark glass, you sit again. The restlessness is not grief about the row deepening. Grief would sit heavy and still. This paces. It checks the shelf with the eyes. It counts hours since the last light and calls the count nerves. When the hour grows long enough, thought dresses the pacing as wisdom. One will steady me to apologise better. One will stop me saying worse. So you light again, and again breath comes easier for minutes while the shut door stays shut.

An hour later the unease returns, a little sharper for having been paused. The chest feels tight again. The mind replays the sharp sentence and adds a second sharp sentence you might say next. The hand remembers the shelf. The same thought returns in the same voice. I need something to take the edge off.

If the cigarette had taken care of the row, why does the edge return on its own clock whether the row mends or not? If it steadied people, why do smokers shake just as hard in the next row, and reach just as fast?

Think of the evenings when there was no row and no deadline, when the house was quiet and the work was done, and the edge still came. You were not calm then without. You reached then too, on the sofa, with nothing to calm down from except the time since the last one. That reach gives the game away. The same hand moves in trouble and in quiet. The same stirring calls in noise and in peace. What returns on its own clock in every mood cannot be the mood. It is something else that wears the mood as a coat.

I smoked through rows and deadlines for years. We all did. We called the lighter our friend in hard times. A friend who only arrives when you pay him, who leaves within minutes, and who makes sure you need him again within the hour is not a friend. He is a lender who charges for the loan of your own calm and calls the loan kindness.

What Did It Take Care Of

You will tell me I make it sound too simple, and that your pressure is different.

You will say, you do not know my boss, you do not know my mother, you do not know the nights I lie awake adding numbers. You smoke after lunch because the mornings grind you down. You smoke after rows because otherwise you would say something worse. The calm may be brief, but brief is better than nothing when the day is long.

I hear you. I lay awake adding numbers too.

Let me ask you three plain questions, as one smoker to another, and answer them with the pavement and the kitchen fresh in mind.

First, when the cigarette is finished, is the hard thing smaller? Is the inbox emptier, the debt lower, the sick relative better, the loved one less hurt? You know the answer because you have watched it a thousand times. The hard thing stands exactly as tall. Only your notice of it dimmed for minutes while smoke filled your mouth.

Second, if cigarettes calm nerves themselves, why do smokers feel more edgy through the day than people who never smoked? Look around any office or any queue. The people who shift weight, who check pockets, who cannot sit through a long meeting without glancing at the door, who wake with that reach before your feet touch the floor in a single token of how mornings run, they are not the calm non-smokers. They are us. We were the edgy ones. We carried an extra nervousness through every pressure and called the brief pause from it calm.

Third, why does the calm always wear off within the hour and ask for another coin? True settling lasts. A walk that clears the head clears it for the afternoon. A mended quarrel holds through supper. Food that fills holds for hours. Only this calm fades on a short clock and returns with the same voice, whether the day is hard or easy. What fades on a drug clock and returns in every mood is not the day settling. It is a dose settling, then fading.

The fact is plain when you hold those answers together. Smoke reaches the lungs, and within seconds the dose reaches the brain. The speed is why the credit feels so sure. Fast feels true. A pressure that has been building for an hour quiets within draws, and the mind stamps the nearest act as the cause. Then the acute lift thins just as fast as it came. The quiet was replenishment, not repair. It was the ending of a want that had grown since the last dose faded, mistaken for the ending of the day's trouble because trouble and want speak in the same tight voice.

Picture it without stories. You carry two loads on that pavement, not one. One is life. Deadlines, rows, money, noise. The other is the fading of the last dose, which tightens the chest and thins patience and makes every outside load feel heavier than it is. You light. The second load lifts for minutes. The first load never moved. You thank the stick for lifting life, when it only lifted its own weight off life for minutes, then set it back down.

That is why the same stick seems to help opposite states. It seems to wake you and to settle you, to sharpen you and to soften you, to fill pauses and to close efforts. No true helper does opposite jobs with the same act. Only a returning want can follow you into every state and claim every easing as its gift, because every state leaves gaps where the want can be heard.

You were conned about the order, not foolish about the feeling. We all read the order backwards. We felt tension, we smoked, we felt less tension, and we read smoke as the cure for tension. We missed that smoking had taught the tension to return hourly and had dressed its return in the clothes of the day. The brainwashing that says smoke steadies nerves is not a lecture you heard once. It is a hundred pavements and kitchens where fast easing arrived right after lighting and stamped itself as care.

Once you see the order right, the minute itself changes meaning without losing its truth. The easing stays real as feeling and becomes false as proof. It proves the dose ended its own alarm for minutes. It proves nothing about deadlines or rows or courage or love. That was never a real relief or reward. A true settling would leave the hard thing smaller after it passed. This leaves the hard thing standing and the want rebuilding.

This is the nicotine trap. It sells you the ending of its own noise as help with life, and charges you in coin, breath, time and peace for the sale.

The Edge Has A Short Crest

There is a second fear under the first, and it needs plain speech.

You fear the edginess without will be endless. You picture yourself snapping at people you love, unable to sit still, sleepless, crawling out of your skin for weeks, trading a small crutch for a large torment. That picture keeps many of us lighting long after we see the order backwards.

Hear this from one who went through it and watched thousands go through it. The restless empty pressure is not your nature showing without its mask. It is the fading dose asking once more, and its voice has a short crest.

When smoke stops arriving, the body notices within hours. Tightness, itch for something in the fingers, shorter temper, harder focus, lighter sleep. That is true, and I will not pretend it is nothing. Then for most daily smokers the crest comes early, a day or two of sharpest edge, then an easing over days, then weeks where the waves grow shorter and thinner until they are only memory knocking. The exact height shifts with how heavily and how long you fed the need, and with the shape of your days, but the shape holds. Rise, crest soon, ease. Not a flat torment without end. Not a new self. A passing settling while the body returns to the level the non-smoker stands on all along.

Watch that crest the way you watched the pavement, hour by hour, and it loses its threat.

The first evening without feels loud because every pause that once held a light now holds only itself. The morning queue, the bus wait, the call that runs long, each gap asks for its old filler. The second day feels sharpest, temper short, focus thin, hands searching for work. Then a morning comes when you wake and the first thought is of the day, not of the pack, and you notice the air in the hall feels plainer and cleaner in your chest. The waves still visit, but they visit shorter. A smell, a break bell, a sharp word brings a flicker, then the flicker passes without building because no fresh dose replanted it.

Ask yourself why that matters for stress. If the edginess you call my nerves were truly your nerves, stopping would leave you permanently more nervous than a man who never smoked. But men who stopped years ago are not permanently more nervous. They sit through delays and rows without patting pockets. They sleep. They laugh. They face deadlines with clear heads. Their calm is not a dose. It is the baseline you knew before the first puff taught you hourly want.

Think back to before you learned to inhale. You had rows at home. You had exams. You had bosses. You shook before hard words and burned after them, and you steadied without fire. No child needs smoke to survive a scolding. No non-smoker needs it to survive a deadline. The steadiness is in the person, not in the paper tube. Smoking did not add a new power to meet life. It laid a second tremor over the natural tremor of life, then took a bow each time it paused its own tremor for minutes.

I want you to feel the weight of that in present time, because relief kept in present tense stays honest. Right now, as you read, there may be a faint stirring under your ribs, a wish to pause and light before you finish this page. Notice its voice. It does not speak in the voice of your deadline or your row. It speaks in the voice of time since the last dose. An hour or two since, and the fingers grow light. That is not life calling for help. That is the last dose fading and asking to be fed. Feed it and the voice quiets for minutes. Leave it unfed and the voice rises a little, crests, then thins on its own while life goes on around it.

You do not need to test this by torment. Only watch the next stirring when it comes and name its true source before you obey. Is the inbox fuller than ten minutes ago, or is the interval fuller? Did the row grow sharper, or did the hour grow longer since the last light? Each time you catch the interval speaking in life's voice, the coat slips. Tension starts to read as returning want, not as proof you cannot meet the day.

We feared the crest because we thought it was ourselves. I thought my edge was my character. Then I watched it rise and crest and thin within days, and I saw my old steadiness standing underneath, untouched. You will find the same steadiness. It never left. It was only drowned hourly by a small alarm you mistook for your own heart.

The Current And The Switch

Stand back on the pavement with everything we have seen, because the whole move lands here.

You believed cigarettes calm you. The belief felt proven a hundred times a day. Pressure, light, easing. Row, light, easing. The stamp of fast relief looked like care.

Seen whole, the order runs the other way. Smoking teaches the body to grow edgy within the hour. Life supplies its own pressures on top. The two blend into one tightness. You light. Within seconds the dose quiets its own part for minutes. Life's part never moved. Within the hour the dose part returns and borrows life's voice again. That loop is why you need the switch more on hard days and still need it on easy days, why one is never enough to hold the afternoon, why the calm never accumulates into a calm life.

A switch that feeds the current it claims to switch off is not an off-switch. It is a meter that charges you for pausing its own charge.

You do not need a new power to face hard days. You need to stop paying for the extra tremor that makes hard days harder. Deadlines will still demand. Rows will still sting. Money will still need counting. But you will meet them without the hourly tightening and loosening laid over them, without the pocket checking and the clock watching, without the second job of feeding a dose that only ever fed itself.

Keep your loyalty to what truly steadies. A breath of air on the pavement steadies. A few slow breaths before you answer steadies. Eating when hungry steadies. Sleep steadies. A kind word sought after a row steadies. Work faced directly steadies because the stack shrinks as you touch it. Those were always the true settlers. The stick only stood nearby while minutes passed and claimed the credit for time doing its slow work.

When the next pressured hour comes, watch it through to the next want as we did here. Let the hand lift and then ask, before it reaches, what load am I asking fire to lift? The deadline or the interval? The row or the fading dose? You will hear how often the answer is the interval. Each hearing loosens the old stamp. The off-switch loses its magic without losing your respect for how heavy days feel.

You came to this page believing tension proves you need smoke. Leave it seeing tension double. There is life's tension, which smoke never touched, and there is the returning want, which smoke only ever paused for minutes before replanting. Read the second as the second, and the first grows lighter to carry because you carry only one load where you carried two.

SUMMARY

- Pressure and rows are real, and fear of facing them without smoke is understandable.
- On the midday pavement the cigarette quiets feeling for minutes while deadline, messages and clock stand untouched.
- After sharp words the kind minute passes and the row remains, with the same want returning within the hour.
- Three questions show the order backwards: the hard thing is no smaller after, smokers carry more edginess through the day, and the calm fades on a short clock in every mood.
- Fast easing feels like care because the dose arrives within seconds, then thins quickly and asks to be fed again.
- Two loads blend into one tightness: life's pressure, which smoke never moved, and the fading dose, which smoke only paused.
- The restless edge after stopping rises, crests soon and eases over days for most daily smokers, revealing steadiness underneath.
- Tension now reads as returning want wearing life's voice, not as proof smoke calms life.
- A switch that feeds its own current is not help with stress, and meeting days without the extra tremor leaves only one load to carry.
