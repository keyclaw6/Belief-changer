Chapter 5
THE LIFT THAT STEALS

IN THIS CHAPTER
The fluorescent office at 3:07pm, the drawer that opens by itself, tight shoes worn all day for the pleasure of taking them off, the friend at the riverbank, the quiet pantry inside your own body

*You were never lifted above yourself — you were briefly relieved of the weight BAD SUGAR itself had just placed on you.*

### The Fuel You Think You Believe In

You have surrendered the kindness story. That took honesty, because that story felt like love. Now we meet the second story — the practical one, the responsible one, the one that sounds like common sense and therefore is harder to question.

It says: "It gives me energy / I need sugar to function / it helps me concentrate."

That is not my phrase. That is yours, in your own dialect, and I quote it exactly because I want you to hear how reasonable it sounds. It doesn't sound like greed or weakness. It sounds like maintenance. Like fuel.

We all live this story. We are tired. There is work still to do, children still to feed, a screen still glowing. We tell ourselves we are powering through. You pride yourself on powering through, don't you? You have powered through for years with the help of something quick and sweet — a biscuit, a chocolate bar, a sugary drink — because you believed it *helps you function.*

I am warm to you here, because that belief was not foolish. It was engineered to feel true. And I will be harsh to the trap that made you believe it, because that belief is what keeps you tired.

So let me ask you plain, and answer me inside your own head with the honesty you have already shown you own:

When you eat something sweet at 3pm, do you actually rise above how a person who never touched BAD SUGAR feels at 3pm — brighter, clearer, more alive than normal? Or do you, for a few minutes, feel less bad than you did five minutes before you ate it?

That question is the next sentence. Do not hurry past it. Which is it?

The fact is, BAD SUGAR has never given you energy. It has only ever relieved, briefly, the dip it created — and sold you the relief as if it were a gift.

### The 3pm Drawer

Come with me to the exact place this lie lives. Not a theory. The place.

It is 3:07pm. The fluorescent light is humming. The morning coffee is long gone. You got through lunch, but lunch was whatever you could grab, and now your shoulders have that concrete feeling and your head has that thin hum behind the eyes.

You know that empty, edgy, slightly needy feeling that arrives before any thought does? That is its name. Not hunger. Not true need. That empty, edgy, slightly needy feeling that says *I-need-to-eat-something-RIGHT-NOW.* It arrives like a faint alarm under your breath. We have all misread that little alarm as proof we need a lift.

And the drawer knows. The drawer slides — you didn't decide, your hand decided — the packet rustles before the taste does, because the brain has learned the rustle *is* the dose. The first bite is sweet and, for ninety seconds, the chatter gets quiet. Your shoulders drop a centimetre. You think, *There. That's better. I needed that. I can think again.*

Then what?

Watch what really happens now, not what you were taught to expect.

Within twenty minutes the brightness thins. The shoulders come back up. The head gets heavier, not lighter. A faint edginess returns, a little heavier than before. And the hand is back in the packet — not from greed, but because the quick lift was already falling. You would have something quick and sweet but now you know that will only give a short term lift which will inevitably be followed by a fast drop. That is not my sentence. That is the lived sentence of people who have watched this loop enough times to see its shape: lift — drop — need.

Look at the sequence without the story on top.

You were steady. You had a sweet hit. You felt a brief rise. You felt a fast drop. You felt that empty, edgy, slightly needy feeling again, a touch louder, and you read that louder need as proof you need another lift.

When did the need appear? *After* the sweet, not before. The sweet created the dip it then briefly relieved. The relief felt like energy because the dip felt like tiredness. Remove the sweet and you remove not a pleasure but the manufacture of tiredness itself.

You never rose above the steady baseline of someone free of BAD SUGAR. You fell below it, then climbed partway back toward it, then fell again.

That is the whole trick.

If BAD SUGAR really were a genuine pleasure, treat or lift — if it really gave you energy — the energy would add. One biscuit would lift you, two would lift you higher, and you would finish the packet feeling magnificent. But you don't. You finish the packet feeling tired, wired and crashing. That phrase is not an insult. It is the precise name for the permanent state the lift-story keeps you in: tired because the dip keeps returning, wired because the hit jangles you, crashing because the fall always follows. Once you see that triple, you cannot mistake it for fuel again.

Ask yourself now, with the drawer still open in front of you: did the biscuit give you concentration? Or did it briefly quiet the scattered, edgy dip that the last biscuit created an hour earlier? The answer is in your own mouth. You already know.

### Tight Shoes For Sugar

I want to give you the picture that will kill this story everywhere, so you can see it in any kitchen without needing me.

Imagine you decide to wear a pair of shoes two sizes too tight. All morning they pinch. Your toes throb. By lunch you are wincing with every step. At 3pm, someone says, "Here — take them off for five minutes." You slip them off. The relief floods up your legs. You sigh. You think, *Fantastic. That was wonderful. Shoes that tight really help me relax.*

Would you say those shoes gave you relaxation? Would you thank them?

Of course not. You would see that the relief came from ending a pain the shoes themselves caused. You never felt better than a person in comfortable shoes — you only felt briefly as good as you would have felt all day if you hadn't worn the tight shoes at all. And the longer you keep putting the tight shoes back on to get the relief of taking them off, the sorer your feet become.

That is BAD SUGAR.

BAD SUGAR is tight shoes for your blood, your appetite, your mood. The first pinch is tiny — that empty, edgy, slightly needy feeling we name the way the trap wants you to misname it — the little whine from the Sugar Worm, the trivial creature that complains when unfed and starves within days when you stop feeding it. You were taught to read that whine as *I need energy*. It is not. It is *you fitted the tight shoes again, and they are pinching.*

Each dose pinches, then sells you the loosening as a lift. You feel the brief quiet as the lace loosens. You credit the dose for giving you energy. Then the lace tightens again, a notch tighter than before, so the next loosening feels even more necessary. After weeks and years of this, you live tired, wired and crashing and believe that is normal tiredness that needs more lift. It is not normal tiredness. It is the ache of tight shoes you were taught to lace every day.

This picture matters because it shows why willpower never worked and was never needed. You don't need discipline to keep taking off tight shoes. You need to stop putting them on. When you do, the mild whine from the little creature lasts a few days and then is gone — because you are no longer creating the pinch you were thanking the pinch for relieving.

You say, "But I *feel* a crash after sugar — that must be real low blood sugar, so I must need sugar to fix it." Let's be precise, because precision is kindness and it protects you from fear.

There is a real after-dip mechanism: a sweet spike can be followed by a dip as your insulin does its job, and that dip can feel like need. So the low that follows sweetness can be physiologically real. And — this is not low blood sugar as doctors define it. In healthy people who do not have diabetes, a true clinically low reading — rare, at or below 55 — is uncommon and everyday slumps are not that disease, and that label for ordinary afternoons is contested. Both halves are true: the mechanism that makes you feel the drop after the spike is real, and the slump is not a medical emergency that proves you need BAD SUGAR. It proves BAD SUGAR gave you the drop it then offered to relieve.

You were not correcting a deficiency. You were answering a discomfort BAD SUGAR had just created. The lift never lifted you above steady. It returned you toward steady, briefly, then robbed you again and called the robbery energy.

### The Push At The Riverbank

There is a smaller picture inside the big one, and I want you to carry it for the days when memory tries to soften the trap.

Imagine we are walking by a river. A friend walks beside you, smiling, offering you sweets. Without warning he pushes you in. You flap, you swallow water, you panic. He dives straight in after you, pulls you to the bank, wraps you in a blanket, and you are flooded with gratitude: *He saved me.*

Only later you remember: he was the one who pushed you.

That is what the lift does. It pushes you into the dip, then offers its hand as rescue. You thank the rescuer for the relief and forget it was also the perpetrator. Each time you think BAD SUGAR saved your afternoon, you forget it made the afternoon need saving.

I use this token on purpose, briefly, so you see the shape in one breath: the lift that steals first creates the need, then sells the relief of its own creation as energy. Once seen, you cannot feel grateful to that hand in the same way.

You were not ungrateful to be saved. You were misled about who pushed you.

### The Quiet Pantry Inside You

Now I want to remove the last fear that keeps this story standing: the fear that without BAD SUGAR you will be flat, that your brain will starve, that you need an import to function.

We have all been taught that sugar *is* energy, that without it we will run empty. It sounds like biology. It is not biology. It is advertising speaking in a lab coat.

The fact is, your body makes the glucose it needs itself. It manufactures it from protein and from glycerol, steadily, quietly, without any need for BAD SUGAR to be imported. This does not mean you must eat some severe, joyless prescription or live on meat and fat — I am not prescribing any diet, and you are not reading this book to quit plain good food. It means, simply, that eliminating BAD SUGAR is not deprivation. Your brain does not go dark without sweets. Your body had a pantry of its own long before any manufacturer put a packet in your drawer.

Think what that means for the 3pm story.

If your body can keep the lights on without BAD SUGAR — and it can — then what was that urgent *I must have something sweet right now* feeling? It wasn't fuel-need. It was that empty, edgy, slightly needy feeling — the little whine from the Sugar Worm that has learned to whisper in your own voice, *I need sugar to concentrate,* right before the drawer opens. The Worm is trivial and already dying when unfed. The belief that the whine means *need energy* — that is the big lie that feeds it.

We were all conned in the same way. We learned to hear that whine and to call it hunger, to call it tiredness, to call it proof we are not coping, and to answer it the only way the trap profits from: another hit, another pinch of the tight shoes, another push into the river so we can be saved again.

You are not a person who needs a lift. You are a person who was living with a manufactured dip.

### You Never Beat Steady

Stand back now and see the whole inversion the way you will live it, not the way you argue it.

We believed BAD SUGAR lifts us. We believed the crash means we need more. We believed our tiredness proves we are people who need a sweet to function.

Turn it the other way around and feel how much truer it feels in your own body.

You have a natural steady state. Real hunger arrives cleanly, you eat real food, you are satisfied and clear and you carry that steadiness for hours. BAD SUGAR does not raise you above that steadiness. It never did. It drops you below it, lets you climb partway back for a few minutes so you credit it with giving you what was already yours, then drops you again a little lower. The only lift it ever gave you was the brief relief from its own pinch. The cost was living tired, wired and crashing in between lifts.

So there was never a boost. There was only a debt, repaid in part, then increased.

If that is true — and your own drawer proves it — then stopping does not remove energy. Stopping removes the thing that was stealing it. You do not need to find a substitute that feeds the same lie. You do not need to brace for flatness. You need to stop putting on the tight shoes and let the mild whine die of neglect.

I know the brave, tired part of you will say, "But I have powered through for years — without that lift I will get nothing done." You have powered through *despite* the lift, not because of it. The power came from you, from your will to keep going, from the real food that actually steadied you underneath the hits. BAD SUGAR snuck a ride on your own stamina and took the credit.

Let the credit return to its owner.

The afternoon you remember as BAD SUGAR getting you through was an afternoon you got yourself through while BAD SUGAR charged you for the favour. The focus you credit to the chocolate was focus that returned when the whine went quiet for a minute — the whine BAD SUGAR had made. The steadiness you think you will lose is the very steadiness that returns when you stop robbing it.

You do not need energy from a packet. You make your own, quietly, all day, the way your body was built to.

- The 3pm drawer shows the loop in real time: a short lift inevitably followed by a fast drop and a louder need that looks like proof you need more.
- The tight shoes tell the truth in one image: BAD SUGAR first pinches, then sells you the relief of loosening as if it gave you something.
- The push at the riverbank names the inversion flat: the rescuer you thanked was also the perpetrator who pushed you in.
- Your body manufactures the glucose it needs without BAD SUGAR, so quitting it is not deprivation and does not starve the brain.
