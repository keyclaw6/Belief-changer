Chapter 4
THE TREAT THAT TAKES

IN THIS CHAPTER
The after-school kitchen with the tin on top of the fridge, the 8pm sofa when the day is still humming in your shoulders, the house that is real food and the gatecrasher who eats the host, the same evening replayed without BAD SUGAR and the warmth that stays anyway

*The love was in the moment — BAD SUGAR just gatecrashed it, ate the host, and took the credit.*

### The Kindest Lie

We need to talk about the kindest lie in the trap.

Not the loudest. The kindest. The one you defend because it feels like kindness itself.

You know the voice. It doesn't sound like craving. It sounds like care.

"It's a treat / I deserve it / it's how I reward myself."

That is not my summary. That is your own line, in your own dialect, and I print it exactly as you say it to yourself because I want you to hear how tender it is. "Celebrating, rewarding myself, going to the cinema, etc with chocolate." That is how the brainwashing sounds when it wants to be loved. It doesn't shout. It offers a gift.

We were all taught this early, weren't we? Sweets as love. Sweets as "well done." The tin that came out when visitors came. The chocolate pressed into your palm after a hard day at school when you were trying not to cry. The adult who meant nothing but kindness and taught you, without meaning to, that sugar *is* kindness.

So of course you feel warm toward it. Of course you hear "but it *is* a treat" and something in you bristles if I question it. I am not questioning your need for kindness. I am showing you where the kindness really came from — so we stop letting the con sell your own warmth back to you.

The fact is, BAD SUGAR has never given you love. It has borrowed the love that was already there and signed its own name on the card.

Think of your best treat-memory and be honest about its ingredients. Was the warmth the sugar, or was it being chosen, being seen, being allowed to stop and be held for a minute? The taste was brief. The feeling filled the whole room. That is the clue.

If BAD SUGAR were a genuine pleasure, treat or lift, the pleasure would track the dose. The second square would feel as good as the first. The quiet evening with one biscuit would feel finished. But it doesn't. The hand goes back. The packet lightens. The warmth you wanted curdles into negotiation and a faint, familiar shame. That shame is not proof you are greedy. It is feedback from the trap — the bill arriving for a gift that never was.

We keep this tender on purpose. I am warm to you, and I will be harsh to the trap. You were not weak for wanting comfort. You were conned into believing comfort comes wrapped in weaponized sweetness, sold by a manufacturer who profits while you pay.

And that belief is precisely what keeps the loop alive. The reward-frame keeps the loop alive — not because you are weak, but because you were taught to read relief as proof of love.

### The 8pm Sofa

Come and sit with me at 8pm. The real one, not an idea.

The day has taken everything it was going to take. Work ran long, or the children needed everything at once, or the empty flat felt too quiet. Your shoulders are up by your ears. You have done the decent, adult thing all day — you have coped, you have been good this week. The sofa is there. The kitchen is three steps away. And the old script slides in, soft as a blanket:

"I deserve this."

We all know this sofa. We have all lived on it. The packet opens with that neat rustle that the brain has learned to hear as relief before the taste even arrives. The first bite is sweet, then sweeter, then sickly. For two minutes the chatter in your head gets quieter. Then it gets louder. Then you are negotiating the second one, and the third, and the quiet calculation of "how bad have I been?" begins.

Now — keep the sofa. Keep the day that was hard. Keep the relief of stopping, the company or the quiet, the blanket, the laugh from the television, the fact that you are finally horizontal and no one needs anything from you for twenty minutes.

Replay that exact same evening, beat for beat, with no BAD SUGAR.

What happens?

You still exhale. You still feel the day drain out of your shoulders. You still get the warmth of having survived it and given yourself permission to rest. If there is someone next to you, the hand in yours is still warm. If you are alone, the aloneness is still — for the first time that day — a rest instead of a demand. The taste that lasted ninety seconds is missing. The love is not.

That is the reassign. The situation did the work. Your body did the work. The moment did the work. BAD SUGAR was only ever sneaking a ride.

You say, "But it *feels* like it helps. It *feels* like comfort." Of course it feels that way. The con is built to feel that way. It gives you a hit that briefly mutes the low *it created*, then lets the low return a little deeper, so the next hit feels more necessary. You credit the hit for the quiet. You forget it brought the noise.

We are not arguing that you don't deserve comfort. You do. You deserve real comfort — clean rest, real food that finishes, company that stays, pride that doesn't flip to shame five minutes later. What you do not need is a dose that sells itself as comfort and delivers craving.

Let me put it flat. The evening was a genuine moment of rest and kindness. BAD SUGAR pretended it was a genuine pleasure, treat or lift and charged admission to a house you already owned.

### The House-Party Gatecrasher

I want to give you the picture that kills this story for good, so you can see it in a kitchen without needing me to explain it again.

Think of real food as a house.

Real food is the house you live in. It is not exciting packaging. It is walls, heat, a table, a stove, a fruit bowl that is actually sweet because fruit is sweet, a pan of roast vegetables that smell like dinner, a piece of good bread that chews and finishes, plain yoghurt that is tart and clean. The house is not loud. It feeds you. It lets hunger arrive cleanly and lets satisfaction arrive and *stop.* Hunger comes, you eat, you are done. That is the house working.

Now imagine the house is holding a small, decent party. Nothing grand. Just life — you are hungry, you cook, you eat, you are satisfied. The guests are there: taste, appetite, steadiness, ease.

Carbs that are real food know how to behave at this party. They come, they talk to hunger, they leave when dinner is over. Even plain carbs are not the problem — they are a bit lonely, they invite company, but they still know when to go home.

BAD SUGAR is not a guest. BAD SUGAR gatecrashes.

It does not knock. It kicks the door, turns the music up so loud no one can talk, eats everything in the fridge, flatters you for two minutes — "I'm a treat, I'm love, you deserve me" — then starts an argument in the kitchen, breaks a glass, and leaves you to clean up while whispering that the party was boring without it.

And the worst trick: on the way out it tells you *you* invited it. It tells you the party *was* the gatecrasher. That the warmth was the sugar. That the fun was the fix.

It wasn't. The house was the pleasure. The gatecrasher just ate the host.

Look at that image and test it against your own mouth. When you eat real food when you are truly hungry — a peach, a proper meal — the taste is bright and it finishes. When you eat BAD SUGAR as a "treat," the taste is loud then cloying, and it does not finish — it invites the next one over. "Carbs are inherently lonely — they invite company over" is true of plain carbs in a gentle way. BAD SUGAR is different: it invites *itself* over, and then invites ten more.

That is why "just a little" so often becomes the box. It is not a failure of character. It is a gatecrasher who, once inside, keeps opening the door for more gatecrashers and calling that "more love."

You do not need to moralize sweetness to see this. GOOD SUGAR — the sweetness already inside whole fruit, vegetables, plain dairy as part of real food — stays in the house. It belongs there. It behaves. We are not quitting sweetness. We are showing the gatecrasher out so the house can be sweet again without being robbed.

Tell yourself the truth in one line and feel the inversion land: you were never being given a treat. You were being taken from while being told you were being gifted.

That is how the Sugar Trap sells taking as giving.

### Why The Packet Gets Bigger

"But why do I need more than I used to? Why is it the family size now? Why am I not enjoying it like before?"

This is where the loop shows its shape. You are not imagining the escalation. The same sweet gives you less, and the thought of it gives you more.

There are two moves happening at once, and they look like one.

First, the reward gets crowded. The more often you feed BAD SUGAR as a treat, the more blunted the reward response becomes. The first squares felt bright. Now they feel dull unless you have more. So you do. You buy bigger. You eat faster. You chase the brightness that used to come from less. This is why "I need the family size now" is such a common, quiet confession. It is not greed. It is tolerance — the trap quietly raising the price of the same illusion.

Second, the cue gets louder *before* the bite. The wrapper rustle, the smell from the bakery on the cold street, the sight of the tin on the fridge — these fire before you have eaten anything. Your mouth waters before your hand moves. The brain has learned the script and runs it early, so you feel need before you feel taste.

Both of those are interpretations consistent with brain imaging — the blunted response after, the hyper-reactivity to cues before — not proof that you are compelled, not proof that you are damaged, and not a sentence about who you are. Imaging doesn't prove irresistible compulsion, and it doesn't mean permanent harm. It shows a pattern that fits what you already live: the treat that promised more now needs more to feel like anything, and the packet calls to you before you are even hungry.

Lived experience says it simpler: you get less from it and want it more. That is not love. Love does not work like that. A trap does.

And here is the tell that this was never a genuine pleasure, treat or lift in the true sense: a genuine pleasure does not need to escalate to survive. Real food does not need curs to get louder. A peach in August does not need a family-size peach. A good meal does not need you to hide the wrappers. The escalation is the fingerprint of the con — the gatecrasher needing to invite more gatecrashers because one was never enough.

Once you see escalation as evidence of blunting and cue-learning — not of deeper enjoyment — the "I deserve a bigger treat" story collapses without a fight. You don't deserve a bigger theft. You deserve the house back.

### The Love That Was Already There

Let's do the hardest, kindest reassign of all — the celebration.

You are at the cinema, or it is a birthday, and the table is bright and there are people you love. Someone says, "Go on, it's a treat," and BAD SUGAR is put in the middle of the table like a member of the family. For years you have let it sit there and take the credit for the warmth in the room.

I want you to be precise, because precision is how we steal the credit back.

What made that afternoon beautiful?

Was it the sugar, or was it the dark room and the laughter you felt in your chest, the hand that found yours, the story on the screen, the rare permission to sit together and do nothing useful? Was it the cake, or was it that everyone sang at once and a child blew hard and the candles went out and everyone clapped too loud?

Take BAD SUGAR out of that scene and run it again in your head, exactly as it was, without changing anything else.

The film is still funny. The hand is still warm. The candles still go out. The love is still there — in fact it is clearer, because no one is quietly negotiating with a plate, or counting, or feeling the faint edge of "should I?" that BAD SUGAR smuggles into every celebration it attends.

BAD SUGAR did not make the moment. The moment made BAD SUGAR look good. It sneaked a ride on your life and sold your own joy back to you with a confectioner's logo on it.

You do not need to forbid sweetness to see this. You do not need to become a person who lectures their family about ingredients. GOOD SUGAR stays. Fruit is still sweet. A real dessert made from real food, eaten as food, still belongs at a table. What does not belong is the engineered dose that calls itself love while delivering the very craving and shame that make love feel conditional.

We fell for this together. We were all taught that sweetness equals lovability, that to deny the sweet is to deny the love. We all reached for the packet when we wanted to say "I matter" to ourselves. That is why we say we. We were conned in the same kitchen, by the same promise, from the same bright packets at child-eye height.

But you are the one who gets to leave. You.

I told you I would be surgical here, and I have been. Not cruel. Surgical. The treat that takes is a phrase that tells the truth in four words: every time it said "I'm a treat," it took. It took steady appetite, it took clean finish, it took pride that could have sat with you after the plate was empty. It took and it called the taking "love."

See that once, and you cannot unsee it. The next time the voice says "you deserve this," you will hear what it really means: you deserve rest, you deserve kindness, you deserve sweetness that does not steal from you — and BAD SUGAR is not any of those things.

The fact is, the warmth was the sofa, the company, the rest, the pride of a hard day done. The fact is, real food is the house, and the house was already sweet enough without the gatecrasher.

### What We Keep

Do not leave this chapter thinking I am asking you to live without sweetness. I am not.

I am asking you to keep everything that was ever sweet about the "treat" — the pause, the permission, the love, the laugh — and to stop paying the gatecrasher to stand in the middle of it.

You keep the after-school kindness — you just stop letting a tine of engineered sweetness impersonate a parent's love.

You keep the 8pm permission to stop — you just stop feeding the thing that turns stopping into sneaking.

You keep the celebration — you just let the people, not the product, be the reason it sings.

That is the belief that changes now. Not "I am forbidden." Not "sugar is evil and I must be pure." Simply: that reward was never a genuine pleasure, treat or lift. The treat took. The moment gave.

Hold that line as lived fact, not as idea. You do not have to fight the next packet. You have to see what it is. A gatecrasher knocking on a house that is already warm — and you, this time, not opening the door because you finally recognize who is knocking.

SUMMARY
- The treat story feels like kindness because it was planted as kindness in childhood, but BAD SUGAR borrowed warmth that already belonged to people, rest, and celebration.
- An 8pm evening with real rest and company feels as warm without BAD SUGAR, which shows the moment — not the dose — was doing the comforting.
- Real food is the house; BAD SUGAR is a gatecrasher that eats the host and sells the theft as a gift — that picture explains why "just a little" becomes the box.
- Needing more to feel the same and hearing wrappers call before you are hungry fits a familiar pattern of blunted reward and louder cues, which does not mean you are compelled or permanently damaged.
- Every celebration was beautiful because of people and permission, not because BAD SUGAR was on the table; it was only ever sneaking a ride.
