Chapter 8
WHO BUILT THIS CAGE?

**IN THIS CHAPTER**
The supermarket canyon at child-eye height, bright packets and "treat yourself" slogans, the quiet laboratories where sweetness is tuned, the body's warning lights you were taught to call weakness, the yo-yo that tightens with every rule

*You did not build this cage with your appetite — it was engineered around you and sold to you as pleasure.*

### The Canyon You Didn't Design

Walk with me down the aisle you have walked a thousand times.

It is not an aisle. It is a canyon. On both sides the walls rise above your head, colour on colour, cartoon tigers and gold foil and words that sound like love: *treat yourself, you deserve it, little reward.* At your knees, at your child's knees, the smallest packets sit where the smallest hands can find them without asking. At the till, the last bright row waits for the moment your will is thinnest — tired, after work, with a queue behind you.

We told ourselves this was choice. We told ourselves those packets were there because we wanted them.

The fact is, they are there because someone decided you would want them more if they were there.

You did not ask for the canyon to be built. You walked into it after it was finished. For years you blamed the feeling in your trolley on a sweet tooth. It was not a tooth. It was architecture. The packets were placed, the colours were tested, the slogans were bought to make the sight of BAD SUGAR feel like an idea of your own.

Do you feel the shift? The hunger you thought was yours, private and personal, was put out in front of you on purpose, at the exact height your eyes fall when you are tired. The "I can't stop once I start" voice you heard in the evening was not evidence of a flaw in you. It was evidence that the design was working.

We were not weak. We were outgunned.

And that is why this chapter cannot be about your appetite. Your appetite is honest. It asks for hunger, then it asks for enough, then it stops. What you have been living with is not appetite. It is the Sugar Trap and the Big Sweet Lie made solid — walls, lights, sounds, smells — built by an industry that profits precisely when you feel you need what harms you.

When you see the canyon as a canyon, you stop arguing with yourself inside it. You get angry at the builders. That anger is clean. It restores your dignity in one move, because it puts the blame where it belongs: on the trap, not on you.

### The Rooms Where Sweetness Is Tuned

Let me take you behind the wall for a minute, not into conspiracy, into kitchens you will recognise.

There are rooms — real, bright, ordinary rooms — where people in white coats do beautiful, clever work with one purpose: to find the exact point where sweetness makes you reach again. Not too little — you would leave it. Not too much — you would put it down. The precise middle where the tongue lights up, the brain says *more*, and the hand goes back into the packet before the first piece is finished. Food scientists call it the bliss point. I call it weaponized abundance.

They do not hate you. They do not need to. They are paid to make a packet harder to stop eating than real food ever could be. Real food is the house: an apple, a bowl of soup, roast vegetables, plain bread that still tastes like wheat. It fills you, then lets you go. BAD SUGAR gatecrashed that house. It learned to be louder than the house. It loiters on the shelf in armies — biscuits, sweets, chocolate, cakes, pastries, ice cream, sugary drinks, sweetened yoghurts that were plain yoghurt until sugar was poured in, white bread and white rice and chips eaten as snacks and lifts. When this book says sugar, we mean BAD SUGAR — and GOOD SUGAR, the quiet sweetness already inside whole fruit and plain food, remains free. It always was.

Armies of scientists did not have to invent your craving. They only had to tune the hit so the craving would keep returning, and then let the world sell it back to you as love.

You have been told your "sweet tooth" is who you are. The fact is, the tooth was trained. The palate that now finds a peach bland was taught to find it bland by packets engineered to shout over it. The body that now dips at three o'clock was taught to dip by a hit that guarantees a dip to follow.

There is a reason you felt fine while the heat rose. We have named that once before — the frog that sits in water while it warms degree by degree and calls it comfortable until the water is cooking him. You felt fine while the canyon was being built around you, while the packets got brighter and the portions got bigger, while the guidance says keep added sugar below a tenth of energy, ideally below a twentieth, and ordinary baskets sailed far beyond it without anyone feeling they had done anything unusual. The norm itself was moved. That is not your failure. That is the measure of how completely the engineered norm surrounds you.

See it, and you stop negotiating with yourself about discipline. You start seeing the con.

### Your Body Was Not Failing — It Was Warning

Now the kindest inversion of all, because you have been shamed by the very signals that were trying to save you.

That crash after the biscuit. That restless, empty feeling before the drawer opens. The way the wrapper seems to glow on the counter, the smell from the bakery that hits you from half a street away before you have decided to want anything. You were taught to read those as proof you are broken — too sensitive, too greedy, too emotional.

They are not character flaws. They are warning lights.

The crash is your body telling you the lift was borrowed. The lift you called energy was a spike, and your system working to bring you back down is not low blood sugar as doctors define it — it is the after-dip the spike created. The Glycaemia falls, the steadiness wobbles, and the body says *that was not fuel.* That message is not a command to eat more BAD SUGAR. It is a report that BAD SUGAR took.

The craving is your body reporting an old debt. That empty, edgy, slightly needy feeling you met in the last chapter is the little whine. It is not love. It is debt-collecting. Thanking BAD SUGAR for quieting it is thanking the hand that keeps the debt on the books.

And that glow before the bite — the wrapper that catches your eye a second faster than anything else on the shelf, the saliva that arrives before the hand moves — that is not proof you cannot help yourself. It is the learned cue. Two things happen together when the trap has lived in you a while: you need more to get the same brief quiet, and the signals that predict the quiet get louder. Wrappers shine. Smells pull. Aisles call. Enjoyment itself gets a little thinner while wanting gets louder. That pairing explains why the family size that once seemed absurd now seems normal, and why you can feel grabbed before you have tasted anything — yet being grabbed is not being compelled. A signal can fire without an order being obeyed. A light can flash without you driving toward it.

We fell for the translation that said every light meant *go.* The Big Sweet Lie taught us to read *warning* as *wanting.*

And there is one more piece the canyon hides in plain sight. It is not mere exposure that tightens the trap. It is the yo-yo. The rule that says *I won't keep it in the house, just a little, once a week is fine* — followed by the evening when a single biscuit becomes the box — that on-off schedule drives bigger binges than steady anything ever would. The restriction makes the next hit more precious, the hit makes the next dip deeper, the shame makes the next restriction stricter. The trap feeds on forbidding and permitting in turns. It does not need you to be grazing all day. It needs you to swing.

Do you see now why moderation never worked? Not because you lacked character, but because the schedule itself was the engine. You were not failing at control. Control was the engine.

Once you see the lights as lights, the whole self-story reverses. You are not someone who cannot control sweet things. You are someone whose body has been accurately reporting, for years, that BAD SUGAR does plenty TO you and nothing FOR you. The tiredness, the wired edge, the crash — the body kept the score while the mind kept the slogan.

And when the slogan is seen — "treat yourself" on a packet that takes — you cannot hear it the same again. The villains come into focus. Not you. The manufacturer that tuned the bliss point, the lobby that profits while you pay, the pretty paper around processed poison. Be warm to yourself. Be harsh toward them. That is exactly the right place for the harshness to go.

You do not need to fear this seeing. It does not ask you to fight harder. It shows you there is nothing in you to fix, only a cage to step out of that was built around you before you knew what was being built.

We have all lived in the Sugar Trap and believed the Big Sweet Lie because it was built to be believed. You are not defective for believing it. You are free the moment you stop.

**SUMMARY**
- The trap does not live in your character; it was engineered and placed where you would meet it — at eye height, at the till, in slogans that sell BAD SUGAR as love.
- Cravings, crashes, and cue-driven urges are not flaws; they are your body's warning lights reporting that BAD SUGAR created the low it briefly quiets.
- The wanting can get louder while the liking gets thinner — which explains needing the family size and feeling grabbed by a wrapper — without meaning you are compelled to obey the signal.
- The on-off yo-yo of restricting and then bingeing tightens the trap more than the substance alone, which is why "just a little" kept becoming the box.
- Guidance suggests keeping added sugar below about a tenth of energy, ideally below a twentieth, yet ordinary life now carries most people far beyond that — not because most people are weak, but because the normal has been engineered to be high.
