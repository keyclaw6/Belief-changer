Chapter 4
THE TREAT THAT TAKES

**IN THIS CHAPTER** — The kitchen after school, the tin on the worktop, the sofa at eight o'clock, the house that was already warm, the gatecrasher at the door.

*BAD SUGAR never gave you love — the love was already there, and BAD SUGAR stole the credit.*

### THE KINDNESS WE WERE HANDED

We were handed sweets as love before we had language for either.

We all remember it. The kitchen after school. The door banging, the bag on the floor, the tin lid lifted and the rattle inside. A hand — mother's, grandmother's, teacher's — offering gloss and sweetness and saying, without words: you are seen, you are good, you are home. The sweet was not food then. It was translation. It was adult tenderness made small enough to fit in a child's palm.

I know what that memory does to you when I talk about BAD SUGAR. It fishes instantly. It says: how can you call that a con? That was kindness. That was safety. That was love.

It was kindness. It was love. That is precisely why the con worked.

We were not foolish children. We were given phoney information by people who had been given the same phoney information, and who passed it on warmly, innocently, completely. A whole world taught us that sweetness that comes in paper equals sweetness that lives in the heart. That BAD SUGAR was a genuine pleasure, treat or lift. That is not your fault. It is brainwashing, and brainwashing, by definition, does not feel like brainwashing while it is happening. It feels like normal life.

Do you see the trap in that? When love arrives with a wrapper, the child does not learn to separate them. The wrapper gets the love. We grow up and the child inside still answers correctly: love is good, therefore the thing that arrived with love must be good. We go on saying it in the adult's voice, without hearing how tender and how mistaken it is:

"celebrating, rewarding myself, going to the cinema, etc with chocolate."

Have you ever said that, or thought it without saying it? "I deserve this." "It's how I reward myself." "I've been good this week." That is not a greed voice. It is a tenderness voice. You have had a hard day. You have been good. You want to be kind to yourself. And the kindest thing you can think to do — because it is what you were shown kindness looks like — is to hand yourself BAD SUGAR.

I am warm to that person. That person is you, and is me too. We all did it. We were all conned in the same kitchen.

But love is not in the wrapper. It never was. And once you see where the love actually lived, you cannot return it to the wrapper.

### THE SOFA AT EIGHT O'CLOCK

Come to the adult version. The same play, new set.

It is eight o'clock. The day has taken what it takes. Work, commute, messages, dishes, the small frictions that add up to a tired person on a sofa. The light is low. The blanket is there. Someone you love is beside you, or you are alone and finally, finally off duty. And there, in the hand or on the table, is the fix — chocolate, biscuits, ice cream, sweet drink, the packet that rustles.

Feel it honestly. What is good there?

Is it the chocolate? Or is it that for the first time today no one wants anything from you? Is it sugar? Or is it warmth, undoing, a breath out, the permission to stop performing and be held — by a cushion, by a room, by company?

The fact is… the sofa was already kind before the packet opened.

We credit BAD SUGAR with the kindness that belongs to rest. We credit the hit with the relief that belongs to stopping. We tell ourselves the sweet made the moment sweeter, when the moment was the sweetness.

Do the simplest test, in imagination first, and soon in your ordinary evenings, and watch how ruthlessly clean the answer is.

Replay the same evening, beat for beat, without BAD SUGAR. Same sofa. Same low light. Same tired body finally allowed to be tired. Same company, same conversation, same film, same laugh you would have had anyway. The warmth is still there. The undoing is still there. The relief of the day being over is still there — more clearly, because no packet is rustling to hurry you through it. Your mouth tastes clean. Your hands are still. Your mood does not climb and then snag.

What did BAD SUGAR add to that evening that the evening did not already contain?

Be strict. Did it add flavour? Real flavour fades after the third bite. Did it add calm? The calm came from stopping, not from sugar. Did it add love? Love was the person beside you, the room that holds you, your own body finally allowed to rest.

BAD SUGAR is not love. It is an engineered dose that learned to arrive at the exact moment love was already happening, so you would mistake one for the other. It sneaks a ride.

And when you see it sneak a ride once, you see it everywhere. At the cinema, the film is the beauty, the hand held is the warmth, the laugh is the joy — chocolate in the lap adds nothing to any of them and takes the edge off the taste of the film. At the birthday, the singing is the love, the faces are the love, the child blowing out candles is the love — cake is the thing that makes the child wobble after and the adult negotiate with shame. In every case the moment was doing the work. BAD SUGAR was taking the applause.

That is the surgical credit reassignment that will free you from the treat excuse forever. Not: treats are bad so you must deny yourself. But: the treat was never the sweet. The treat was the rest, the company, the celebration, the permission to be kind to yourself — and BAD SUGAR has been stealing that credit for years, then sending you the bill as craving and shame.

You say, "but it *is* a treat." I hear you. It feels so true. That feeling is the whole engine of the Sugar Trap. The trap needs you to believe the sweet is the treat, because if the sweet is the treat, you will pay for it endlessly. If the rest is the treat, you are free already — you can have the real treat without paying.

Ask yourself, gently, with the new question from the last chapter — not what BAD SUGAR does TO you, but what it does FOR you — what did it actually do FOR you on that sofa?

The fact is… it took more than it gave. It gave you three minutes of gloss and then left you with the need for gloss again, a little emptier, a little more wired, a little less proud of how you looked after yourself. The sofa gave you rest. BAD SUGAR gave you a reason to need rest again tomorrow.

### THE HOUSE PARTY

This is where the gatecrasher picture makes it physical, so you feel it in your body, not just in argument. Stay with the picture because it undoes a second lie tucked inside the first: "carbs are lonely — they invite company over, and I need something sweet."

Imagine real food as a house. Not a lecture about nutrition. A house you live in.

The house is plain, warm, solid. Vegetables roasted until sweet, fruit that snaps and floods, bread that is bread, dairy plain, beans, fish, meat, rice that came from a field and not a factory — the things your body recognises without translation. When you eat those things when you are truly hungry, you feel it: the quiet arrival of satisfaction, the steady return of energy, the clean finish that says enough. No drama. No negotiation. The house does what a house should — it shelters you.

Plain carbs are not the house. Plain carbs are guests who loiter. White bread, white rice, chips, the starchy snacks that behave like sugar in the trap — they come in, they are pleasant, they take up the sofa, they leave crumbs. They say they will keep you company. They invite company over.

And BAD SUGAR is the gatecrasher.

You left the door ajar because the guests seemed friendly. The gatecrasher does not knock. He walks in loud, in bright paper, with weaponized sweetness far beyond anything a field would make. He eats the host. He turns the music up too loud. He spills. He tells you the party is him. By the time he leaves — briefly — the house feels emptier than before he arrived, so you think you need him to come back to feel full again.

Do you see it? Real food never left. It was always the house. BAD SUGAR gatecrashed the house and then convinced you the emptiness he left was proof you needed him, not proof he had taken something.

This is why the sentence "I need something sweet" is so perfectly back to front. You do not need something sweet. You need the house. Your body already knows how to be satisfied, sweetly, clearly, from real food — the natural sweetness inside whole fruit and plain dairy as part of a real meal is not BAD SUGAR and was never quit here. That sweetness is the house's sweetness. It satisfies and it finishes. BAD SUGAR is the gatecrasher's sweetness — it arrives, it spikes, it leaves a mess, it makes you think the house is bland.

The house is not bland. The gatecrasher dulled your taste for the house.

When you stop feeding the gatecrasher, you do not deprive the house. You give it back to itself. Taste comes back. Real food tastes sweet again, Dessert stops being the point of the meal because the meal was the point all along. You are not switching to something inferior. You are evicting the thing that was eating your food and calling it yours.

Hold that picture for your next ordinary meal. Do not use it to fight yourself. Just see it while you eat: the house is feeding you; the gatecrasher is not invited. Feel how natural it is to enjoy the house when the party is not being crashed.

### WHY ONE SQUARE BECOMES THE BOX

You will say, "that is tidy, but it does not explain why I cannot stop once I start. If BAD SUGAR is not a genuine pleasure, treat or lift, why does one biscuit become the box? Why do I say 'just one' and then watch my hand keep going? That feels like pleasure pulling me, not emptiness."

It feels like pleasure Because we have misread the pull.

Here is what people describe when they are free to speak plainly: "I just crave sweets and when i eat them, i feel like i cant stop." Not I enjoy them so I continue. I cannot stop once I start. That is not a treat signature. That is a trap signature.

There is a mechanism people point to that helps make sense of this without turning you into a case or a diagnosis. It goes like this: the more often BAD SUGAR hits the reward circuit, the more the circuit is crowded and the less bright the hit feels — so you need the family size to chase what a single square used to do. At the same time, the cues fire hotter — the wrapper, the smell, the drawer, the sight of the aisle — so wanting glows before the first bite. Blunting on one side, hyper-reactivity on the other. Together they explain the miserable pair we all know: "why am I not enjoying it like before" and "why do I want it more than ever before."

I put it to you that way, carefully, because the honesty matters. That crowding-and-cue picture is an interpretation consistent with what brain imaging shows, not proof from imaging. Much of that imaging comes from obesity and binge-eating samples, and interpretation is still contested. It does not prove you are compelled, it does not prove damage is permanent, it does not make you helpless. It simply names why escalation and triggered wanting happen together — so you stop reading them as love or need, and read them as the trap's workings.

The wanting that glows when you hear the packet rustle is not pleasure arriving. It is the learned circuit firing before pleasure, selling you a memory of relief that the next bite will not deliver more brightly than the last. You eat. The hit is briefer, duller. The mind says more. You have more. The finish never arrives. The box empties and the only thing fuller is disappointment.

That is not you loving food. We will meet that story — "I love food / it's who I am" — later and undo it cleanly. For now see this: loving food looks like satisfaction. Needing the family size to feel anything looks like tolerance. Time to retire the nice word for an uncomfortable thing. You were not enjoying more. You were chasing a duller hit with a louder cue.

And there is the inversion hidden inside that chase: the rescue is the perpetrator. Each hit that promises to satisfy the need creates the need for the next hit by crowding pleasure and sharpening the cue. You are not being looked after. You are being farmed.

You do not need to fix that with effort. You need to see it. When you see that "I can't stop once I start" is how the Sugar Trap behaves, not how love behaves, the shame lifts. You are not weak. You are in a loop that was engineered to look like a treat.

A brief word so we keep sweetness honest, because I am warm to sweetness itself. This book does not moralize sweetness as forbidden. Whole fruit, vegetables, plain dairy as part of real food — that is GOOD SUGAR, natural sweetness already inside real food, and it is not BAD SUGAR and is not quit. You are not being asked to live without sweetness. You are being shown which sweetness is yours. The house's sweetness stays. The gatecrasher's dose goes.

### THE MOMENT WAS LOVE — SUGAR SNEAKED A RIDE

Let us land this cleanly, because landing it now saves you years of bargaining with the same thought.

From the kitchen after school to the sofa at eight, we were taught one lesson: that to make a hard day kind, to mark love, to celebrate, you hand over BAD SUGAR. That lesson stuck because love and sugar arrived together. But together is not same. Thunder arrives with lightning. Thunder does not make the light.

When you separate them, the whole architecture of "I deserve a treat" collapses without a fight.

What you deserve after a hard day is the real treat: rest, warmth, food that satisfies, company that does not bargain, time that is yours, pride in how you looked after yourself. BAD SUGAR cannot give you any of those things. It can only interrupt them with gloss, then leave you a little more in need of interruption again tomorrow. The fact is… the day was hard; the evening deserved tenderness; BAD SUGAR is not tender. It is the manufacturer in pretty paper selling you back your own need for kindness at interest.

We have lived long enough in the Sugar Trap to hear how strange that sounds at first and how obvious it feels a week later, when the house is yours again. People tell it plainly when they are free: how well you feel when you are able to avoid sweet cravings. Not how hard it feels, not how deprived. How well. How steady. How kind the ordinary evening felt once the gatecrasher stopped eating the host.

I want you to let that in without turning it into a rule to resist with. Do not make this a new ban to white-knuckle. Make it a correction of credit. The next time the thought says "I deserve a treat," answer it with the truth, kindly, as settled fact: I do deserve a treat — real rest, real food, real company — and BAD SUGAR was never that treat. It was the thing that took from the treat and made me think I needed it.

Do you feel how that already removes the sacrifice? There is nothing to miss in a treat that took. There is nothing to give up in a love that sneaked a ride. There is only the moment itself, returned to you, whole, without its loud guest.

You will meet the other old story soon — that BAD SUGAR gives you energy, that it lifts and steadies you — and we will meet that story in the body, at three in the afternoon, where it lives, and undo it there. For now let the treat story go for good. Not holistically, not morally, but factually. The celebration was beautiful despite BAD SUGAR, not because of it. The sofa was kind despite BAD SUGAR, not because of it. The childhood love was love despite sweets, not because of them. Every drop of warmth you credited to the sweet belonged to the room, the person, the hour, your own body being allowed to rest.

Once that credit is returned, the sweet has no voice left. It said: I am love. You have answered: no. Love was the house. You were the gatecrasher.

Call it what it was, and be relieved it no longer gets to sign love's name.

### SUMMARY

- The treat belief was installed when BAD SUGAR arrived at the same time as love, so we mistook the wrapper for the kindness inside.
- The sofa at eight was already a treat — rest, warmth, company — and BAD SUGAR only sneaked a ride on the relief the moment provided.
- Real food is the house and BAD SUGAR is the gatecrasher that eats the host; evicting it returns taste and satisfaction, it does not remove sweetness.
- Needing more to enjoy less and wanting more before the first bite are not proof of pleasure but the trap's signature of tolerance and cue, read as interpretation not proof.
- The kindness you deserve after a hard day is real rest and real food, and that treat remains fully available without BAD SUGAR.
