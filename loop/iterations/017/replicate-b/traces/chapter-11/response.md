Chapter 11
BUT I LOVE FOOD

**IN THIS CHAPTER** — The family table and the sweet-tooth story, the traits laid out on the table and turned the right way up, three whispered myths answered at the door, a kitchen six months later with numbers and taste

*You were not born to need BAD SUGAR; BAD SUGAR taught you to feel as if you were.*

### THE STORY AT THE TABLE

We all know this table.

It is your family around food. Someone laughs and says it for you before you can say it yourself: "We have always had a sweet tooth in this family." "I love food — it is just who I am." "I have got a sweet tooth, I have always had it, it is in my genes." It is said warmly, almost proudly, with a plate pushed toward you.

And you feel a little thud of relief. *Ah. That explains it. That excuses it.* If it is identity, then it is not a trap. If it is genetics, then it is not brainwashing. If it is personality — the food-lover, the sensuous one, the one who really appreciates taste — then you are not conned. You are just being yourself.

I understand why that thought feels kind. It lets you off a hook that was never yours.

The fact is we have all been handed that story. We grew up watching the adults we loved treat BAD SUGAR as love. Sweets after school when we were sad. Cake to celebrate. Chocolate to say we deserve it. White bread and chips as the quick, easy fill when we were tired. Year after year the act repeats until the feeling attaches: *people like us love sweet things.* It feels like a description of who we are.

It is the other way around.

We did not love BAD SUGAR because we are that kind of person. We came to feel like that kind of person because we were put, very young and very often, into the Sugar Trap. We learned to live inside that little loop — the hit, the brief lift, the fast drop, the empty, edgy need to fix it again — and then we gave that loop a name that sounded like identity so we would stop questioning it.

Think of it plainly with me. A child does not decide, at five, to be a "sugar person." A child is given sugar. The child feels the spike, then the sag, then the nudge for more. The adults around the child call it a treat, a reward, a sign of love. The child hears that and does the only sensible thing a child can do: she believes it. He believes it. We believed it.

Tell me — at what moment did you decide that your personality needs BAD SUGAR? Was there a day you examined the evidence and chose it? Or did you find the habit already in your hands and then explain it backwards?

We never decided. We inherited a habit and then stitched an identity around it so it would feel chosen.

And once you see that, a lot of weight falls off. You are not exposing yourself as weak. You are seeing that you were outgunned by an engineered repetition that would make almost anyone feel the same way. As you saw, many report this pattern — not as a diagnosis, not as a label for life, but as self-report from ordinary adults who grazed, binged, or negotiated just as you do. You are not uniquely flawed. You are not exemptly flawed either. You were simply in a trap that teaches anyone inside it to talk about it as if it were character.

That thought alone frees a little air.

### THE TRAITS TURNED THE RIGHT WAY UP

Let us put the traits on the table and turn the table the right way up.

You know the list by heart. The sugar person is said to be impulsive around food. To have no off-switch. To need the family size now where a square once did. To think about wrappers and smells long before the bite. To be a grazer, a secret snacker, a night eater. To be tired, wired and crashing by afternoon. To feel they love food more than other people do.

We were told those traits explain why we are in the trap.

Look again.

Those are not the traits that *led* you into the trap. They are the traits the trap *leaves* on anyone who stays in it.

A person who eats BAD SUGAR in the craving-snacking loop learns to crave. Of course they think about wrappers. Of course the smell fires before the food arrives. Of course one bite carries them to the box. That is not personality. That is a learned cue reacting exactly as it was trained to react. Image after image, packet after packet, the brain learns: wrapper means hit, smell means hit, 3 p.m. means hit. Then it fires ahead of the bite. That does not prove you are compelled. It proves you were conditioned. Conditioning can be unlearned the moment the lie that powers it dies.

A person who rides lift-then-drop every day learns to need more to feel the same flicker. Of course you now need the family size where a square once did. There is a way to picture why — the reward circuit gets crowded and dulled by repeated hits, so the same dose does less, while the cues grow louder — but that picture is only an interpretation consistent with what has been seen, mostly in other groups, not proof that you are broken or permanently blunted. It is not a sentence on this reader. It is just a way to understand escalation: tolerance whispers, *more*, and cue-reactivity shouts, *now*, and you obey because you still believe the hit helps.

Put it flat: you are not tired, wired and crashing because you are that kind of person. You are tired, wired and crashing because you keep feeding a loop that makes you tired, wired and crashing and then sells you the next hit as relief. We were all taught to read that state as personality. It is not. It is the cost of staying.

Here is the inversion in one sentence: we were told the traits cause the trap. The truth is the trap causes the traits.

Once you see it, the whole identity excuse collapses without shame. You do not need to decide whether you are impulsive or sensuous or born with a sweet tooth. You only need to see that a trap that repeats a hit and a dip, day after day, would make anyone look impulsive, sensuous, and sweet-toothed. The traits are its footprints, not its entry ticket.

### EVEN IF IT WERE TRUE, YOU STILL WALK FREE

Now I will be even more generous than I need to be, because I want to leave you no crack to slip back through.

Suppose the other side were true. Suppose some part of appetite, temperament, or family habit did make you a little more drawn to sweet taste than the next person. Suppose — just suppose — you really do love food more intensely, or you really did inherit a bias toward sweetness. Let us grant it entirely for the sake of argument.

So what?

Even then, this method still frees you, and it frees you for the same reason. The physical part of the bargain is trivial. The little creature that complains with that empty, edgy, slightly needy feeling starves within days when unfed. Whether you were ever more drawn to sweet than your neighbour, that mild loop still dies quickly. What keeps people going back is not the whine. It is the translation of the whine by a big belief: *this means I need it, I love it, it helps me, it is me.*

Kill that belief and the whine is just a whine — already fading, already dying, even rejoice-able.

That is why I spend no time arguing about whether sugar is "addictive" in some final, settled scientific sense. Honest, serious scientists genuinely disagree about that very question, and both sides grant the behaviour you already know — bingeing under a restrict-binge schedule — happens. Whether they call it addiction or not, you know what it feels like to binge once the bite is permitted. I do not need the label to free you. You do not need to be diagnosed to be free. You only need to see that the benefit you ascribe to BAD SUGAR is illusory, and that the identity you ascribe to yourself is the after-image of the trap, not its cause.

And there is another honesty you deserve. I will not re-present a single population number as a new revelation in this chapter. You have already seen the self-report picture once: many ordinary adults say they experience addiction-like patterns around highly palatable food. That is not a disease label for you. It is just permission to stop calling yourself uniquely weak. The figure does not matter now. The fact that many know this pattern matters — because the story "it is just me, just my genes, just my personality" cannot survive it.

So hold both thoughts gently. First: the traits are the result, not the cause. Second: even if a sliver of cause were true, it would not protect BAD SUGAR, because this escape does not require you to be a different person. It only requires you to see that BAD SUGAR does nothing for you. People of every temperament walk free the moment that lie dies. The method does not ask you to change who you are. It shows you who you were before the trap taught you to misname yourself.

We fell into the Sugar Trap without seeing the walls. That sentence is not an accusation. It is an absolution. And we leave it the same way we entered it — not by rebuilding personality, but by seeing clearly.

And while you are seeing clearly, notice how hard you have tried. You have tried to manage this by counting, by cutting down, by keeping rules, by keeping promises to yourself on Sunday night. That was the Willpower Method talking, and it turns every natural, strong-willed person into a person who feels weak. You were never weak. You were using a method that makes any trait look like a flaw.

### THREE MYTHS THAT SOUND LIKE SCIENCE

This is the room where the last clever objections line up at the door. They sound like science, but they are just the Big Sweet Lie in a lab coat. I will meet each one plainly and leave nothing standing.

> "Fruit will spike me too?"

No. BAD SUGAR is not the sweetness inside whole fruit as part of real food. That sweetness arrives inside fibre, water, and a meal that your body knows how to handle, and it satisfies. BAD SUGAR is the added and refined sweetness engineered to hit fast and leave you edgy. An apple can be food. A packet engineered to a bliss point is a fix. Do not let a confusion about fruit become a reason to keep feeding the trap that is robbing you of steady taste.

> "I need sugar for my brain."

Your brain needs glucose, and your body makes the glucose it needs from real food — there is no need to import BAD SUGAR to feed it. Think of your own steady days: you have handled difficult work, cared for people, and solved problems for hours without a sweet hit, and your brain did not stop. The shaky, can't-concentrate feeling you call need is the after-dip the last hit created, not proof your brain will stall without the next one.

> "Dark chocolate is healthy, so…?"

So nothing about freedom. A bar can contain things you can get from real food and a dose that keeps the loop alive. Calling one BAD SUGAR "healthy" is like calling a prettier packet less of a trap. If you keep one deliberate exception because it sounds virtuous, you keep the belief alive, and the belief keeps the Worm alive. Keep the good food. Let the trap go entirely.

Notice what those three questions did. Each one tried to save one special case. One inside the line is never one. It is permission to keep the whole story.

### HOW WELL YOU FEEL WHEN YOU ARE ABLE TO AVOID SWEET CRAVINGS

I want to give you a life that proves it, not as an appendix, not as a footnote, but here, in the main flow, because you need to feel a steady escape in your bones before the strongest lie comes calling.

This is not my story. It is a composite of many, but every detail is true to what ordinary people describe.

*I was forty-one, two children, a job that lives at a desk. I would have told you I was a sugar person since childhood. My mother said it kindly: we just love our food in this family. I believed her.*

*My day was a timetable of sweet somethings to get me through. Biscuits at ten, something from the drawer at three, chocolate on the sofa to reward myself for getting through. I did not keep it in the house — then I did. I ate a little — then I ate the box. I told myself I do not keep it in the house on Monday and I am standing in the garage eating it straight from the shopping bag on Wednesday. I told myself it helps me concentrate. The fact was I was tired, wired and crashing and calling that needing energy.*

*The first week I read this book I argued with every page. I did not quit. I carried on exactly as I was told — eating as normal while I read. When I reached the part about traits being the result, I felt a little shock I had not expected. I listed mine: impulsive, no off-switch, family size now, wrappers glowing. I saw for the first time they were footprints. I did not feel blamed. I felt seen.*

*The evening I made the vow I was not heroic. I sat at the kitchen table after dinner, the kids in bed, a last ordinary sweet in front of me that I ate with attention. It tasted sickly and loud and then, minutes later, edgy and needy. I said it out loud — that I would never feed that loop again — and I felt, not deprivation, but a small, clean click, like a lock opening.*

*The wobble came, honestly. Days two to five were edgy. That empty, edgy, slightly needy feeling flickered — not pain, not drama, just a thin, nagging thinness. I remembered I was supposed to rejoice at it. I laughed once, which surprised me, and said out loud, good, you are starving. I drank water. I ate real meals — eggs, vegetables roasted until sweet, fruit I had not tasted properly in years — and I noticed hunger actually arrive and satisfaction actually finish. I walked past the 3 p.m. drawer and felt, for the first time, relief rather than battle.*

*Numbers, because you want them: in the first three weeks I stopped buying the four or five packets a week that had been automatic. I stopped eating the 8 p.m. bar, then the second bar I never counted. I did not weigh or measure anything else. I ate when hungry, stopped when satisfied. My afternoons, which had been a fog of tired, wired and crashing, levelled to steady and clear — not buzzing, not high, just ordinary and workable. I did not become a different person. I tasted again.*

*The taste is what I did not expect. Fruit — I know this sounds small — fruit became sweet. Really sweet. Not the shout of BAD SUGAR, but clean, round sweetness that finishes. My tea without sugar tasted, after a week, like tea, not like missing sugar. My children noticed I was less snappy at five. I noticed I did not need a treat to love them or to love food. I love food more now, because food is food again and not a fix.*

*There were pings. Week four, cinema, chocolate in laps around me. I watched the film. I laughed. The warmth was the film and the hand in mine. No pang became a tragedy. A thought crossed my mind — just one can't hurt — and I answered it without strain: fantastic, I am free, and I watched it pass like a bus I do not need.*

*Six months now. If a stray sweet was in a sauce at a restaurant, I did not turn it into a story about failure. My body coped with the blip; my mind did not reopen the decision. That is the whole secret. Guard the belief, not the behaviour. I did not swap one BAD SUGAR for another clever version. I did not keep a healthy exception as a pet. I kept fruit, vegetables, plain dairy — good sweetness as part of real food — and let the rest go.*

*How well you feel when you are able to avoid sweet cravings — that line from the book is exactly it. It is not a high. It is the absence of a low you had stopped noticing. You feel well because you are well. Steady. Clear. Free to get on with enjoying your life.*

You hear what that life is telling you. It had the same excuse — genes, personality, love of food — and it fell away when the trap was seen. It wobbled for days, not forever, and then it steadied. It did not become perfect. It became free. And it kept GOOD SUGAR — the simple sweetness inside real food — because this was never about depriving you of sweetness. It was about stopping the robbery of it.

So let me land the verdict of this chapter plainly.

You are not a sugar person. There is no sugar person. There is a person who was taught that BAD SUGAR is love and energy, who lived inside a loop that creates the need it pretends to relieve, and who then read the loop as identity. See the loop, kill the lie that translates its whine into want, and the identity story collapses like paper. And even if you cling to the story — even if you whisper, but I really do love food — I answer, good. Then you will love real food properly again now that the loud, engineered hit is no longer shouting over it.

There is nothing wrong with you that needs fixing. There is a trap around you that needs seeing. And you have just seen it.

The next room will be the most beautiful lie of all — the celebration that you fear will be grey without BAD SUGAR — and I will meet it head-on and show you, with your own eyes and hands, that every drop of sweetness you thought it gave was the moment, the company, and your own taste sneaking a ride.

**SUMMARY**

- The "sweet tooth" and "I love food, it is who I am" are not the cause of the trap; they are the result the trap leaves on anyone who lives in it.
- The traits you think prove identity — needing more, hunting wrappers, thinking about food, the afternoon crash — are footprints of lift-then-drop and cue learning, not proof you are a certain kind of person.
- Even if a personal or family bias toward sweet were true, it would not protect BAD SUGAR — the mild physical whine fades in days and freedom comes from killing the belief that mistranslates it.
- Honest scientists genuinely disagree about the addiction label, and many ordinary adults report the same pattern you know; you do not need a label to be free.
- Myths that sound like science — fruit, brain fuel, healthy chocolate — each try to save one special case; one deliberate exception keeps the whole belief alive.
- Freedom is described by those who walk it as honest, not perfect — a wobble for a few days, then stability, steady taste, and simple relief in ordinary days.
