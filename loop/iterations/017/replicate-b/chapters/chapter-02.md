Chapter 2
THE TRAP YOU DIDN'T SEE

**IN THIS CHAPTER** — The kitchen drawer at ten, the kitchen drawer at three, a child with a peach, the cage with the open door, the two quiet ropes that pull in opposite directions.

*You did not choose freely to eat this way — you were conned into a trap that took choice away without asking.*

### THE DRAWER THAT OPENS ITSELF

You know the drawer.

It is ten in the morning and you are not hungry. Not really. You have had breakfast. You tell yourself you will be good today. You answer two emails. And then — without a decision you can point to — the drawer slides. The packet rustles. Your hand is inside it. The first bite is quick and sweet. You tell yourself it is just a treat. You put the packet back. You feel a little sharper for a minute.

It is three in the afternoon and the same drawer opens. Or another one. The office drawer. The kitchen worktop. The bag in the cupboard you told yourself you would not touch. The same hand, the same rustle, the same quick fix. Only now you are tired as well as sweet-mouthed, and the packet stays open a little longer. You count what you have had today and still call it just a treat. By supper you have counted three just-a-treats and you are already bargaining for a fourth.

If that is your day, welcome. We have lived that day together — you and I and almost everyone else who picked up this book. We call it choice. We say, I could stop if I wanted to. I just like a little sweetness. Everyone does it.

Watch what actually happened in those two small moments.

At ten you were not deciding. You were responding. At three you were not choosing. You were obeying. In both, the feeling came first — that empty, edgy, slightly needy feeling, a little hollow in the stomach, a little restless at the edges — and the thought came second to explain it: I deserve this. I need a lift. Just one won't hurt. The feeling pulled, the thought justified, the hand moved. Afterwards you supplied the reason, not before. That is not how free choice feels. Free choice weighs options calmly and picks. This is a nudge, a whisper, a reflex.

You did not decide to want BAD SUGAR. You found yourself wanting it.

We call the whole device by its true name from now on: the Sugar Trap.

Say it plainly and you feel the frame shift. A trap is not a preference. A trap is not a character flaw. A trap is something you walk into on false information and then cannot walk out of by wanting to, because the walls move while you are inside. Once you see it, the question is no longer why can't I control myself. The question becomes who built this and how does it keep me inside.

That change is warm, not frightening. I am not here to tell you that you are broken. I am here to tell you that you were conned, as we all were, and that being conned is not the same as being defective. The con removes choice without your consent and then sells you the story that you chose it. Seeing that is the first click of the safe. It costs you nothing and it gives you back your dignity in one breath.

### IF YOU WANTED TO STOP, WOULD YOU NEED THIS BOOK?

Let me ask you a simple, friendly, trap-closing question.

If BAD SUGAR were a free, happy choice — like choosing a favourite jumper or a favourite walk — would you need a book about how to stop?

You would not. People do not seek help to stop things they freely enjoy. They do not need a method, a plan, or a promise to stop doing something they truly control. The very fact you are holding this book proves something you have been afraid to say out loud: somewhere inside, you feel you are not fully in charge of this.

That feeling is accurate. It is not shameful. We sense accurately when something has taken hold of us.

Think of any genuinely free choice in your life. You love apples or you do not. You enjoy a long walk or you prefer the bus. No one needs to persuade you. No one needs to give you rules about not keeping apples in the house. No one hides apple cores in shame. You do not count days without an apple. You do not promise yourself just one apple on Fridays. The whole apparatus of control — rules, bargains, hiding, counting, promising, failing, blaming yourself — appears around BAD SUGAR and not around real food. That apparatus is the footprint of a trap, not of a taste.

We fell into it the same way. Not because we were weak but because the information was phoney from the start.

You were told sweetness is a treat. You were told it gives energy. You were told you deserve it, you have earned it, everyone does it, and a little is harmless. You were told this by the people who love you, who handed you sweets for comfort; by the bright packets at your eye level and your child's eye level; by the slogans that speak directly to your tired end-of-day self; by the manufacturers who engineered weaponized sweetness to be craved and re-craved while the lobby that profits tells you to enjoy responsibly. You made your first choices on that information, as a child does, before you had any other frame. By the time you noticed the cost — the grazing, the negotiating, the 3pm slump — the belief was already installed and driving the behaviour.

That is the confidence trick. Not a moral failure. A con. You were sold a picture of BAD SUGAR as a genuine pleasure, treat or lift, and you bought it in good faith, as we all did. The trap then made sure the feeling that followed each dose seemed to prove the picture true.

We will pull that picture apart, piece by piece, in the chapters ahead. Not now. For now it is enough to feel the ground move: you were not freely choosing. You were choosing inside a frame that had been built for you. Freedom will be seeing the frame and stepping out of it, not proving you have stronger will.

Let me give you two names for the machinery so we can talk about it cleanly for the rest of the book. I will name them in passing here — vocabulary, not a lesson. They will do their full work later, when the ground is ready.

The first is the Sugar Worm. Picture a tiny, trivial creature that lives on BAD SUGAR and complains when it is not fed. It does not roar. It whines. It asks for its next dose with that empty, edgy, slightly needy feeling — a mild hollow, a little edginess, a slight neediness that feels like hunger but is not hunger, like tiredness but is not tiredness. It is small. It is already dying from the moment you stop feeding it. In a few days it starves. It is not a mighty enemy you must battle with willpower. It is a small parasite whose whine you have been misreading as a need.

The second is the Big Sweet Lie. That is the lifelong brainwashing that taught you what the whine means. The Worm whines. The Lie translates: you need sugar, you deserve sugar, sugar will lift you, calm you, reward you, make the moment sweeter. The Lie is the voice of every packet, every advert, every well-meaning adult who taught you that love tastes like sweets. Kill the Lie and the Worm has nothing to say that you will obey. Keep the Lie alive and the Worm will seem to speak with your own voice forever.

We will meet both again. For now, hold them as names: the Sugar Worm and the Big Sweet Lie. One in the body, small and starving. One in the mind, big and false. The trap is the two together — a physical whisper made powerful by a false translation.

And there is a third small picture I want to leave at the edge of your mind for later, when you feel a flicker and wonder what it means. A lion in a cage whose door stands open still paces the old perimeter out of habit, not need. That pacing is not proof it belongs inside. It is proof it learned the bars by heart. You have paced the same old perimeter — the drawer, the biscuit with tea, the sweet on the way home — long after the door was open. The unease you sometimes feel when you do not feed the trap is not evidence you need it. It is cage-habit, and it passes when you keep walking free.

I said we would not demolish today's justifications yet. We will not. We will put them on the table and look at them honestly in the chapters where each is due. But we can already feel the two ropes that keep people arguing with themselves.

One rope pulls you one way: I must stop — I feel tired, I feel ashamed, I worry about what this is doing to me for the rest of your life if nothing changes. The other rope pulls you the other way: I cannot stop — life would be flat without it, what would I enjoy then, also for the rest of your life? Both ropes feel like yours. Both feel like genuine fears. What you will see, soon and clearly, is that both ropes are held by the same hands — the hands of the trap. Fear of failure and fear of success are not two opposing truths. They are one trap speaking in two voices. When we cut the trap, both ropes fall.

For the moment, keep just this: you have spent years thinking your problem was weakness. The fact is, your persistence proves the opposite. It takes a strong will to keep bargaining, restarting every Monday, hiding evidence, managing cravings around meetings and family meals, and still showing up to try again. The weak move would have been to shrug and not care. You cared and you kept trying with the wrong instructions. That is not personal failure. That is the trap using your strength against you.

### YOU ARE NOT UNIQUELY WEAK

You are not alone in this. Not close.

When ordinary adults are asked on a validated pattern for eating that feels addictive — loss of control, craving, keeping going despite wanting to stop — about one in seven describe it in their own words. That is not a medical label put on you, and I am not putting one on you. It is people, like you and me, saying plainly, I can't stop once I start, or the craving takes over. At that scale the pattern looks exactly like what population surveys find for alcohol and tobacco on their own scales — common, human, and not a private defect. The point of that number is not to rank harms. The point is to lift the blinding idea that you are the anomaly. You are not weak. You are not uniquely greedy. You are caught in something that catches many, because it was built to catch many.

Now look at how normal has been set while we were not watching.

The world guidance for free sugars — the added and refined sugars that are BAD SUGAR in this book — says keep them below ten percent of your energy, about fifty grams for many adults, with extra benefit below five percent. Look at an ordinary day you call normal: sweetened cereal or juice at breakfast, biscuits at ten, a sweetened yoghurt or sandwich on white bread at lunch, a cold sugary drink in the afternoon, a handful of sweets on the way home, a pudding after supper. Add it without judgment, just honestly, and see where it sits against that ceiling. For a great many of us that "normal" sails past the ceiling by afternoon without ever feeling extreme. That is not because you have no discipline. That is because normal was engineered to be high. The packet at every till, the taster sachets by the door, the just a treat that is sold at every hour — the whole scenery has been moved while we paced our cage.

None of this says any single spoonful is poison, and I do not want you to hear it that way. Inside the BAD SUGAR line there is no healthy level that is worth protecting as a treat — the belief that one fix helps you is what keeps the wanting alive — but your body can cope with an accidental mouthful that turns out to have sugar in it without ruining anything. What it will not cope with is a deliberate, romanticised exception you choose because the Lie is still whispering. Guard the belief, not the behaviour, and the behaviour follows.

You do not need to fix this by counting grams or weighing guilt. You need to see that just a treat was never just, and that the drawer that opens itself was taught to open itself. That seeing is not heavy. It is light. It is relief. We were all taught the same wrong lesson at the same time, and we can unlearn it together at the same time.

### THE BODY THAT ALREADY KNEW

There is another authority in this room besides the trap, and it has been quiet while the trap shouted.

Watch a child eat real food when no one is selling them anything.

I mean a small child at a table with a peach, or a bowl of berries, or warm vegetables and bread that is food, not confection. Watch the sequence. Hunger arrives cleanly — a clear, calm signal, not edgy, not desperate. The first bite is vivid. Chewing is pleasure. The body says more, or enough. And then — this is the marvel we forgot — the child stops. Not by discipline. Not by counting. By satisfaction. The body says done, and the child gets down and runs off. No negotiation. No drawer. No needing something sweet to get through.

You were that child. Your body still knows that sequence. It has not lost the wiring for hunger and satisfaction and steady energy. The trap has only layered noise on top of it. When we remove the noise, the signal returns on its own, without a new system, without replacing the void with effort, without learning to eat like a saint. The body returns to its own baseline because that baseline is where it lives unless we override it with processed poison in pretty paper.

You have felt that baseline in glimpses, even recently. A morning when you ate something plain and real — eggs, or plain yoghurt with fruit, or soup and good bread — and felt clear and steady for hours, not wired and then sagging. An afternoon when you were absorbed in work or a walk and forgot to graze and felt better, not worse, for forgetting. A meal that satisfied you so fully you did not want anything after it. Those were not flukes. They were your ordinary, natural eating peeking through the noise.

The trap teaches you to read the Worm's whine as hunger. The body teaches something different when you let it: real hunger is calm and specific for food — any real food will do — and it is satisfied by real food. That empty, edgy, slightly needy feeling is restless and vague, fixated on the sweet fix, and it is not satisfied but briefly quieted by BAD SUGAR, only to return stronger later. Learning to hear the difference is not a technique you must master. It is simply noticing what you already know when you listen without the Lie's translation.

This is why we can talk about freedom as immediate gain rather than future reward. You are not waiting for a new body to be built. You are escaping a con that sits on top of the body you already have. The steady, clear energy you think BAD SUGAR gives you is your own, returned. The taste you think it lends to life is your own taste, restored. The calm you think it brings is your own calm, no longer being pinched away. Nothing is being taken that you need to mourn. What was taken is being given back.

That is a marvellous piece of news, and I want you to let it be marvellous without a tail of doom. There is a lot of commercial sweetness engineered to be craved — that is true. You can see it in the supermarket canyon with its bright packets at child-eye height and its slogans that say you deserve a fix. Seeing that can make you indignant, as it should. But the indignation is on your side. It restores dignity; it does not sentence you to fear.

For the rest of your life can be a threat or a promise. Trapped for the rest of your life is a threat the trap whispers whenever you imagine quitting: imagine never again. Free for the rest of your life is the promise that arrives the moment the belief dies: imagine always. Same horizon, opposite feeling. We choose the second, not by straining, but by finishing the seeing that makes it true.

### WHY ELATION, NOT DREAD, IS THE CORRECT BEGINNING

You may still feel a tug of dread. That is normal. You have tried to stop and it felt hard. You have tried to cut down and it made BAD SUGAR more precious, not less. You have been told this requires discipline for life, counting, resisting, staying strong, recovering one day at a time. It does not.

The hard part you remember was never the Worm's tiny whine. The hard part was the Big Sweet Lie telling you that the whine meant deprivation — that without BAD SUGAR life would be flat, joyless, less loving, less yours. While that Lie lives, any attempt feels like sacrifice, and sacrifice feels hard because it is framed as loss. When the Lie dies, there is nothing to sacrifice. There is only a con you have stopped feeding, and that feels like relief, not loss. You do not need to be strong to feel relieved. You only need to see.

So we begin as we mean to go on — with elation, not dread.

We begin warm to you and harsh to the trap. We begin with the clear, settled fact that you were conned — not weak, not greedy, not broken — and that a con loses all power the moment it is seen through. We begin by naming the two creatures so you can recognise them instantly when they stir, and by telling the truth, plainly and compassionately, that the unease you feel when you do not feed the trap is cage-habit, not proof of need, and that the pull you feel in both directions belongs to one trap, not to two competing truths about you.

The drawer that opened itself is not your destiny. The packet that sells you back what it stole is not your friend. The body that knew how to eat before the packets arrived is still here, ready and steady, waiting for the noise to lift so you can get on with enjoying your life.

You came to this chapter thinking I freely choose this and could stop if I wanted. You leave it knowing you were conned into the Sugar Trap, that the choice was taken without your consent by phoney information, and that the names for what did it — the Sugar Worm and the Big Sweet Lie, speaking as that empty, edgy, slightly needy feeling — give you back choice by giving you back sight.

Hold that sight lightly. Do not test it. Do not fight. Keep reading. The next step is to ask a kinder, more honest question than does this harm me more than it helps me — a question that finishes the demolition by asking what good it does for you at all.

**BEGIN WITH ELATION, NOT DREAD**
Cast aside doom and gloom; you are escaping.
