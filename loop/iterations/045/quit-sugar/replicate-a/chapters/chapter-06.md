Chapter 6
EAT LIKE YOU WERE BUILT TO EAT

IN THIS CHAPTER: the ordinary lunch, the filter over the tongue, the ride-home apple, and the table with no sugary capstone.

*Real food was always enough — BAD SUGAR only taught your tongue to miss it.*

It is half past twelve and you are hungry.

Not the tired-edgy hollowness behind the eyes that comes an hour after a dose. Clean hunger. The stomach calls plainly. The mouth waters at the smell from the kitchen. Bread and cheese will do. Soup will do. The plate in front of you looks good because you are hungry, and hunger makes good food taste good.

We have all sat at that table and spoiled it before it began.

You have seen the afternoon drawer for what it is. That quick sweet was never a genuine treat or lift as fuel. It borrowed brightness and charged you the slump. You have seen the nine o'clock light for what it is. The sweet never comforted you. It only muted you for minutes, then left the pain with interest. The love was in the people and the pause. The wrapper only rode along.

There remains the last fear, and it speaks quietly.

It says life without BAD SUGAR will be bland. It says real food cannot hold you. It says without sugar there is nothing enjoyable left.

I know that fear because I carried it for years. I believed my appetite was broken. I believed hunger could not be trusted. I believed satisfaction belonged to other people, the ones with easy nerves and easy tables, while mine needed a sweet capstone to close every meal or the meal felt unfinished.

The fact is your appetite was never broken. It was filtered.

THE FEAR OF BLAND

Listen to the sentence as it comes in your own voice, because it comes dressed as common sense.

It says, "I love food too much to live without sweetness." It says, "special occasions need something sweet or the day feels thin." It says, "just a treat keeps life kind."

Hear whose voice that is.

That sentence is the Sweet Talker.

The little empty whimper cannot speak English. It can only tug, a faint, empty, slightly edgy feeling that comes when the last dose wears off. The speaking is done by the louder partner, close to your own voice, who learned the old language and now lays it over every vacancy. Empty plus midday equals need-something-sweet. That equation was taught, not felt. The mite whimpers. The Talker translates the whimper as treat, and here the translation wears the word bland.

We learned that translation the way we learned everything in the Sugar Trap. We ate sweet when hungry and sweet when full. We ate sweet to start the day and sweet to close it. We dosed between meals until hunger itself became strange to us, a signal we no longer trusted, a call we answered with a fix before we could hear what it asked for. No wonder real food sounded dull. We never met it hungry. We met it full of dose, with the tongue still coated, and blamed the food for having no taste.

Ask yourself as an investigator whether bland was ever in the food.

When did you last sit down truly hungry to a real meal and eat it with attention to satisfied? Not standing. Not scrolling. Not full from the biscuits at four and now picking at supper to be polite. Hungry, seated, with a plate of real food, eaten to the point where the body says done.

Most of us cannot name that meal. That is not proof appetite is gone. That is proof the loop kept us from the table where appetite speaks.

Would true hunger find plain food bland? Would the body that asks for food refuse food? Would satisfaction that truly satisfies leave you hunting within the hour?

You know the honest answers because you have lived both sides without naming them. Hunger takes real food gladly and ends in done. The dose takes and takes and ends in hunting. Bland was never in the plate. Bland was in the tongue that arrived coated.

Picture the midday table cleared by hunger, with the plate doing all the talking.

THE LUNCH EATEN HUNGRY

Come with me to that lunch, because this is where the fear gets tested where you live.

It is one o'clock. You did not top up at eleven. The stomach calls. Not a roar. A clean pull. The mouth is fresh. The hands are ready to hold a plate and stay with it.

Set the plate down. Real food, enough, what you like of the food that holds you. Hot soup and bread. Eggs and potatoes. Fish and rice. Meat and greens. Cheese and fruit. Nothing measured. Nothing counted. Nothing eaten to earn or to pay.

Eat it hungry and eat it with attention.

Notice what hunger knows that the Talker never mentions. Hunger has a beginning and it has an end. It rises clean. It takes the first bites bright. The flavours stand forward because the tongue is clear. Salt tastes like salt. Butter tastes like butter. The chew satisfies. Halfway through the pull softens. Near the end the body sends the plain signal, enough, done, the plate can wait. You stop because stopping feels good, not because a rule says stop. The stomach is full and the head is clear. No rush. No fog. No call on top of fullness.

I ate that lunch for the first time after years of grazing and I remember staring at the empty plate in surprise. I had not enjoyed a meal that much since childhood. Not because the food was clever. Because I was hungry and I stayed with it. The same soup I had called dull the week before, eaten full of dose between meetings, now tasted deep. The food had not changed. The tongue had.

Notice how little hunger asks and how much it returns. It asks only for real food and a minute seated. It returns brightness to plain flavours, steadiness to the hands, clarity to the head for hours after the plate is cleared. We believed satisfaction lived in the wrapper because the wrapper shouted loudest in the shop. Hunger shows the opposite where you live. The wrapper shouts for minutes and leaves hunting for hours. The plate speaks quietly and holds you till supper. Once you have eaten one such lunch with attention, the old story that real food cannot hold you loses its kindness and stands revealed as translation.

That is the difference between hunger and the whimper, and once you feel it you cannot unfeel it.

Hunger comes hours after real food, open and clean, glad of bread and cheese. The whimper comes quick after a dose, faint and edgy, wanting something, not anything, something sweet and quick that needs no plate. Hunger ends in done. The whimper ends in hunting. Hunger trusts the plate. The whimper translates every vacancy into proof you are owed a hit.

The fact is your body makes the glucose it needs from the food you already eat. Sugar is not our energy supplier. Our liver is. Eat real food hungry to satisfied and the supply line runs on its own, without a spoon in your tea and without a bar in your drawer. You do not need to import sweetness one dose at a time to stay lit. You need to eat and let the body do the work it was built to do.

Test it where you live while we talk. Note the next clean hunger. Note what it asks for. Note how bread and soup answer it. Note the signal at the end, the quiet done that needs no capstone. Then note the next whimper. Note how fast it comes after a dose. Note how it refuses bread. Note how it wants no plate and no waiting. Your own belly will teach faster than any sentence which voice is hunger and which is the mite.

Would hunger refuse bread? Would satisfaction need a sweet signature to count? Would the body that was fed hungry to satisfied stand up still empty?

You know it would not. What stands up still empty was never hungry. What sits down hungry and eats to done stands up steady.

Eat as much real food as you want, whenever you are hungry, and never crave sugar again.

It does plenty TO your hunger it claims to answer. It does nothing FOR it. Once you eat hungry to satisfied, you stop calling the coating flavour.

Picture the plate pushed back with pleasure, with the afternoon still yours.

THE FILTER OVER THE TONGUE

There is a reason the same apple tastes different on different days, and it has nothing to do with the apple.

Take the evening you eat a slab of chocolate gateau after supper, rich and dark, then reach for a strawberry from the bowl while the chocolate still coats your mouth. What does the strawberry taste like? Dull. Watery. Sour at the edges. You tell yourself fruit cannot compete. You tell yourself real sweetness is weak stuff beside the real thing.

Isolate the variable and you will see it plain.

It was not the strawberry that failed. It was the tongue that arrived filtered. Tasting everything through chocolate-or-cheesecake just eaten, fruit tastes dull until the filter lifts, then the same fruit tastes sweeter. The dose did not prove its superiority. It borrowed the mouth and then judged the contest.

We all lived inside that filter for years and called it taste.

Think of the morning after a night of doses. Tea tastes flat. Bread tastes like paper. An orange tastes sharp. You tell yourself you need something strong to wake the mouth, something sweet and loud, and the Talker agrees at once. Of course it agrees. The filter needs its own coating to feel normal. The tongue that was blasted last night needs blasting again to register anything, the way eyes that stared at a bright lamp need brightness to see.

Think of the child who never lived filtered. Give a hungry child a ripe peach in August and watch the juice run to the elbows. No wrapper. No chase. The eyes close. That is not a weak pleasure beside gateau. That is taste working as built, bright because the tongue is clear.

I lived both tongues and I tell you flat which one tastes more.

When I fed the loop, dessert was loud for three bites and then mechanical, the hand continuing after the tongue stopped caring, the box finishing itself whether I enjoyed it or not. Fruit was background noise. After a while off the doses, without a fight and without a plan, the same market fruit turned loud. The first grapes after a clear week stopped me at the sink. I stood there eating them slowly, tasting skin and burst, sweet and sharp together, and I thought, where was this all the years I called food bland. It was here. I was filtered.

Would a genuine treat need to dull every other taste to win? Would true flavour arrive only coated and leave the mouth flatter after? Would love of food make an orange taste like paper?

You know it would not. What dulls the tongue to prove its strength proves only the dulling. What leaves the mouth cleaner after the meal than before was the flavour all along. The Talker calls this weak because it cannot sell the quiet. Hunger calls it enough because the body knows the difference between loud and deep.

Watch the filter lift where you live, because you do not need to take my word for the lift.

Eat a real meal hungry to satisfied and leave the capstone off. Drink water. Wait twenty minutes. Then take a piece of fruit, an apple, a handful of berries, a slice of melon, and eat it with attention. Note the first bite. Note the second. Note how the sweetness arrives clean, without rush and without fog, and ends in done. Then remember the strawberry after gateau. Same mouth, different coating. Same fruit, different tongue. Your own tongue will show you which sweetness fed you and which only filtered you.

The Talker will try to change the subject here to strength. It will say fruit sugar is still sugar, so nothing has changed, so you might as well have the gateau. Hear the trick and keep it simple. Whole fruit, vegetables, plain staples and the glucose your own liver makes are not BAD SUGAR. They belong to the body. BAD SUGAR means the added and free sugars with the junk carbs that behave like them in the loop, the sweets and pastries and biscuits and ice cream and chocolate confectionery and sugary drinks with juices and sweetened mixes, the sweetened yoghurts and cereals, the white-flour snacks that vanish without satiety. When this book says sugar, take it to mean BAD SUGAR. The apple was never the trap. The dose was.

Do you see the inversion? The dose did not beat fruit. It filtered it, then claimed the win.

Picture the same apple a week clear, tasting sweeter because nothing stands between you and it.

THE RIDE-HOME APPLE

Now take the test onto the road, because the loop loves the road home.

It is half past five. The bag is heavy. The train drags or the traffic hums. You ate lunch hungry to satisfied and the afternoon ran level, without the timed top-up and without the drawer. Now the old hour comes, the hour when the hand used to go to the pocket or the machine or the shop on the corner, the hour when the chest says, "I-need-to-eat-something-RIGHT-NOW."

Hear it plainly this time.

That urgent sentence is the Sweet Talker.

The mite whimpers faintly as the last dose wears thin. The Talker lays the old wording over it at once, in your own voice, kind and urgent. Tired plus road equals candy. Long day equals owed. Everyone on the train holds something, so holding something belongs. Different days, one translator. The vacancy rises, small and empty, and the Talker dresses it in the reason the road offers, so the reason feels like the cause. The reason was never the cause. The vacancy was looking for its old dose.

A man I know met that hour without a fight after he stopped feeding the loop. He told me after a while he no longer kept craving sweet treats on the way home, reaching for olives, nuts, fruit when hungry, eating supper hungry to satisfied and finding the evening level. He did not force it. He did not count days to earn it. The calls came less and the tongue cleared and the road stopped meaning candy. I give you his story not as a promise of your week but as what a clear tongue does once the filter lifts. Your road will teach its own timing.

Try the apple where you live, not as a rule and not as a prop, but as a way to hear which voice is speaking.

Put an apple in your bag in the morning. Real fruit, crisp, what you like. Ride home hungry or half-hungry, not stuffed with a four o'clock fix. When the candy call comes, loud and reasonable, hold the apple and hold the call side by side for a minute without answering either. Note the call. Note its urgency. Note how it wants something, not anything, something quick with a wrapper that needs no bite and no pause. Note the apple. Note its weight. Note how it wants teeth and chew and a minute on the bench.

Then eat the apple with attention if you are hungry. Bite. Chew. Taste the skin and the burst. Note how hunger takes it gladly. Note how the chew slows the road. Note how the stomach answers, steady, not rushed. Note the station arriving with the apple finished and the body done, without stickiness and without hunting.

Ask yourself on the platform whose call that was.

Would hunger demand a wrapper? Would hunger refuse an apple and hold out for candy? Would true need speak in urgency and vanish after chew?

You know it would not. Hunger takes the apple gladly and ends in done. The whimper refuses the apple because the apple ends the whimper without feeding it. That refusal is the whole confession. The road never needed candy. The mite needed translating.

We were conned here in a small daily way that adds up to a life. We called the commute unbearable without a dose, then dosed through the commute and arrived tired, foggy and heavy, blaming the commute. Take the dose out and ride the same road with clear blood and the road shortens. The train is still slow. The traffic still hums. But the body arrives level because nothing borrowed the ride.

Picture the platform stepped onto steady, with the bag lighter for what you did not buy.

THE TABLE WITH NO CAPSTONE

Now bring the test home, because home is where the unfinished meal haunts us.

It is supper. The table is set. Real food, enough, what the house likes. Steam rises. Hands pass plates. Talk runs. You eat hungry, with attention, to satisfied. The body sends the plain signal. Done. The plate can wait.

And then the old minute comes.

The plates are cleared. The belly is full and the head is clear. Yet the evening feels unclosed. The hand looks for the sweet capstone, the pudding, the bar, the bowl eaten on the sofa, without which supper does not count as finished. The Talker speaks softly now, almost tender. Just a treat to close. Special occasions close sweet, and is not every supper a small occasion. The day deserves a full stop.

Watch what that full stop does, because your own table keeps better records than your story.

You bring the capstone. For three bites it tastes loud. Then the tongue coats and the hand continues past pleasure to mechanical finishing. The full belly grows heavy-full. The clear head fogs a finger's width. The quiet done you held a minute ago thins into fullness with hunting on top of it, the strange state of being stuffed and still called. You sit on the sofa with the bowl and the evening blurs at the edges, then the blur lifts and the night is still there, now joined by flatness and the hunt for tomorrow's close.

Isolate the variable and you will see where the close really lived.

Think of the supper you closed without the capstone. Same table. Same people. Same talk. Only this time you let done be done. You drank water. You sat a minute. You let the meal settle. The fullness stayed fullness, not heaviness. The head stayed clear. The evening closed because eating ended, not because a dose signed it. No chase to the cupboard. No count of what is left for tomorrow. The kitchen went dark and stayed dark, with nothing hunting behind the door.

What is different between those two nights? Not the food. Not your strength. The signature. One night the meal closed itself and stayed closed. The other night the dose muted the close for minutes and left you heavier for hours, and the muting was then called finishing.

Would true finishing need to be unwrapped? Would a meal that truly satisfied reopen hunger within the hour? Would love of the table raid the table after the table ended?

You know it would not. A closed meal ends in gladness and stays ended. The capstone ends in wanting and schedules more wanting. That scheduling is not your weakness. It is the loop's signature. The craving, once triggered, carries the finishing.

Keep the people. Keep the talk. Keep the second helping when hungry and the no when done. Let the pause keep the love while the wrapper goes. The warmth was in the company and the real food underneath. The sweet only sneaked a ride.

Picture the kitchen dark at nine and staying dark, with supper closed and the night staying quiet on its own.

4. EAT REAL FOOD WHEN YOU ARE HUNGRY AND TRUST YOUR BODY
Your body knows hunger from the mite.

You entered this chapter fearing life without BAD SUGAR would be bland and fearing hunger could not be trusted. You leave it holding the plate flat. Hunger takes real food gladly and ends in done. The whimper refuses real food and ends in hunting. The filter dulled every flavour and then claimed the win. The lunch eaten hungry, the apple on the road, the table closed without a capstone, each showed the same inversion where you live. Real food satisfies. The dose scheduled.

Carry that plate into every ordinary day while we talk. When the hand moves before hunger, ask in daylight what that dose itself ever added that hunger and real food and the body's own steadiness could not. Watch how quickly the voice changes the subject to deserving, to occasion, to having earned it. Bring it back. Did the last close satisfy you, or did it schedule this vacancy.

You will find the answer arrives without strain. It scheduled it. It always scheduled it.

Picture the morning clear, with hunger trusted and the day fed as built.

SUMMARY

- I now trust clean hunger because it takes real food gladly and ends in done, while the faint edgy whimper refuses the plate and ends in hunting.
- I see the same fruit tastes dull through a coated tongue and sweeter once the filter lifts, so bland was in the coating, not in the food.
- I know the ride home and the supper table close level without a sweet capstone, because the meal itself satisfies when eaten hungry to satisfied.
- I feel no loss leaving BAD SUGAR off the plate, since the body makes what it needs from real food and the apple answers hunger where candy only scheduled more.
