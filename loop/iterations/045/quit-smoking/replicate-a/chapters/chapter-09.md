Chapter 9
WHO BUILT THIS WANT

IN THIS CHAPTER: the pack in the palm, the filter and the mild promise, the two fears pulling opposite ways.

*You did not grow this want from your own nature; it was built for you, and both fears that hold you were built with it.*

You have stood at the kettle without lighting and found the morning complete. You have sat at the table after the plate emptied and found the taste linger longer without smoke. You have stepped out mid-morning and found the pause hold without burning. I know those three rooms steadied you, because they steadied me when I first lived them without a dose.

Pick up the pack now and weigh it in the palm, because the next belief sits in that small box.

Not the smoke. The box. The cardboard, the foil, the row of white tubes standing shoulder to shoulder. Twenty doses in a paper case, counted for the day. You believed you bought flavour and company and calm. You bought a day's supply of a drug, packed to look like pleasure.

I want you to look at it with clear eyes for ten minutes, and then I will ask you to remember who packed it and why.

Turn it over slowly. Note the foil glinting under the lid, the neat row, the smell that rises, sharp and sweet at once. Note the weight, small in the hand yet heavy in the day, gone by night and bought again by morning. We called that weight company. I call it now what it is, a measured day of hunger packed to look like choice, and the hand that packed it never shook.

## THE BOX IN YOUR PALM

Hold the pack as what it is. A supply box.

We all learned to see it as a personal thing, a friend in the pocket, a little badge of who we are. The colour. The lettering. The click of the lid. We tapped it twice before opening, the way other men tap a watch. We offered it round to say we belong. We kept one brand because the taste felt like ours.

The fact is, none of that was made for your pleasure. It was made to keep the dose regular.

Among themselves, in rooms you were never invited into, the makers wrote it down plainly. The cigarette is not the product. The nicotine is the product. The cigarette is only the package that carries it to the lungs. I state that flat because it is true, and because you have a right to know how your want was weighed and wrapped before you ever felt it.

Look at the tube with that knowledge and it changes shape.

The paper is a wrapper. The filter is theatre curtains. The tobacco is filler soaked to deliver the same drug, puff after puff. The pack is a day's carton of doses, twenty squirts in a row, counted so you never run dry between waking and sleep. The factory does not count pleasures. It counts daily intakes, and it prints the carton to hold exactly that number.

Do you see why the pack never varies. Why one is never enough and twenty never satisfy for long. Why the hand reaches at the same hours each day, morning and meal-end and break and night, as if a bell rang. A pleasure would vary with mood and hunger and company. A dosed drug rings its bell by the clock, because the last squirt fades within minutes and calls for the next.

Ask the plain questions that strip this box as they stripped the kettle and the plate.

If that box held pleasure, why did you need the same number yesterday and the day before and the year before, with no day of true satisfaction to close the account. If the brand was yours by taste, why did every brand do the same office once the nicotine arrived, and why did no smoker ever settle on one and smoke less. If you chose this of your own accord, why does the choosing look identical in every pocket in every town, same tap, same offer, same panic when the box runs low at midnight.

You know the answer because your own pocket gave it each night when you counted what was left for morning. The box did not serve your nature. It trained it. That box is the nicotine trap doing its daily work.

And that voice that says the box is part of you is the Smokescreen talking, not you.

We were conned, not foolish. We took the first ones on phoney information, to look grown, to join the circle, to try the mild promise, and the drug closed the lid behind us. From then on the belief fed the hunger and the hunger fed the belief. The little physical tug called, and the big brainwashing answered that the call proved pleasure and support. Call and answer, answer and call, twenty times a day for years, until the carton looked like character.

Blame the maker for that circle, never yourself. You stood against chemists and designers and advertisers with one pair of lungs. No wonder the circle held. This time is different, because this time you see the circle from outside.

Recall how early the planting began. Posters on the shop wall showing laughing faces with smoke curling. Films where the calm man lights before he speaks and the tired woman lights after tears. Older hands at work offering round on the first day, saying take one, join us. The shelf itself glowing at every corner shop, packs standing like small flags. We did not weigh evidence and choose a carton. We breathed in pictures for years before the first puff, until the carton looked grown and normal and kind.

I stood at that shelf at sixteen and chose red because red looked bold, as if colour were character. I did not know chemists had tuned the dose behind the colour, or that the holes in the filter had been placed to keep the number low on paper while the lungs took the same. I believed I shopped for taste. I shopped for a package built to hold me.

Ask who gained while we learned that shopping. Not the lungs. Not the pocket. Not the partner waiting in clear rooms. The gain sat far from our street, in offices counting daily intakes, while we counted coins for the next box. Warm anger belongs there, not shame here. You were a young person met by a large machine, and the machine knew exactly what it packed.

"I like smoking. That is just who I am. Take it away and I will not be myself."

I hear that sentence because I said it at the same table with ash already in the tray. It sounds like self-knowledge. It is the con speaking in your voice.

Were you born with that taste in the mouth. Did you love the first ones, with the cough and the swimming head and the burning throat. Did the child you were ask for stale lungs and midnight counting. You know he did not. The want arrived after the dosing began, and it grows only while the dosing continues. Take the dosing out and the want thins, while you remain entirely yourself, with the same humour and the same work and the same friends, minus the carton.

It never gave you character. It borrowed your character to sell its carton.

## THE MILD PROMISE

Turn one tube in the fingers and look at the filter, because the second lie sits there in brown and white.

We all believed some version of it. The mild ones are lighter. The filter catches the harm. The holes round the rim let air in and soften the pull. I smoked milds for two years and told myself I had chosen the careful path, while my chest grew heavier all the same.

The fact is, in human hands the mild promise does not hold.

Those tiny vent holes are placed where lips and fingers naturally sit. In ordinary use the mouth covers them. The fingers cover them. The smoker, seeking the accustomed dose, draws harder and longer without knowing it, and takes in what the machine never measured. The machine sips air through open vents and prints a low number. The man blocks the vents and takes the full dose, then trusts the printed number.

I do not say every hand blocks every hole on every dose in exactly the same way. I say the design invites the covering, and the covering returns the dose, and that is why milds never made any smoker mild.

Check it against your own throat, because your throat keeps honest accounts.

If milds gave less, why did you smoke the same number once you switched, or more. If the filter protected you, why did the morning cough stay, and the stairs stay hard, and the grey waking stay grey. If the taste was cleaner, why did the tongue still wake coated, and the coat still carry last night into the day, and the kiss still taste of tray.

You know the answer because you lived the switch and felt nothing change except the colour of the pack. The mild did not soften the drug. It softened the fear of the drug, so you would keep buying the box.

"At least I smoke lights. At least I have cut the harm."

I hear the bargain because I made it, standing at the same shop counter, choosing the silver pack as if colour were care. It sounds prudent. It is the same con in a paler coat.

No tube is a careful tube. I do not tell you to seek a stronger tube to get honesty, and I do not tell you any tube is safe. I tell you the whole shelf is one shelf, different curtains over the same dose, and the only careful move is to see the curtains for what they are.

I remember my own switch in detail because the hope was so plain. I asked for the silver pack at the same counter, tapped it the same twice, lit the first on the same step. The draw felt airy and thin, and I told myself thin meant careful. By noon I had taken two where I once took one, pulling longer to find the familiar knock at the back of the throat. By night the tray held the same count, the mouth held the same stale coat, the stairs asked the same price. The only thing lighter was the print on the pack.

We all ran that small trial on ourselves. Cover the vents with lip and finger without knowing, draw harder to meet the old level, trust the low number while the body takes the old dose. That is not weakness. That is a hand meeting a design exactly as the design expected. Once you see the vents, you cannot unsee them, and the paler coat stops looking careful.

They did not meet your want. They manufactured it.

Keep that seeing in the pocket beside the box. When the lid clicks, remember the hands that filled it. When the vent touches the lip, remember the paper number that never measured a human draw. When the old voice calls the carton yours, remember the shelf of small flags that taught you to call it so. Each remembering loosens one more thread the maker tied.

Read those two sentences again and let them do their office, because they carry the whole room. The want did not rise from your nerves or your blood or your type. It was packed, vented, flavoured, priced and advertised into you, dose by dose, until you called the packing your own voice. Once seen, that cannot be unseen.

## THE RENT YOU PAY

Stay with the box a moment longer, because I must show you what the rent has been, and I will not leave you standing in fear of it.

We paid all day to stand where the non-smoker stood at waking. That is the whole account, and it is a hard one to face.

Morning grey. Throat clearing before speech. Nose blowing before work. Eyes watering on the stairs. Breath short on the small hill. Food half-tasted. Drink followed by coughing. Money counted out daily for the privilege of staying level. Hours counted out yearly in doorways and garages and rain, feeding a paper tube while life went on inside without us. Hiding from partner and child with spray and mint, then hating the hiding more than the hunger. Lighting with friends and calling it belonging, while attention narrowed to when the next lighting could be arranged.

I lived that account for years and called it normal. I told myself the cough was weather. I told myself the stairs were age. I told myself the hiding was politeness. We all tell those stories, because the alternative is to admit a carton has taxed the body daily since youth.

The body kept the true books while we told the stories. Lungs that were built to breathe without thought learned to breathe through smoke. A tongue that was built to taste supper learned to taste ash after supper. A nose that was built to carry cooking and rain learned to carry stale air from pocket and curtain. A heart that was built to climb without comment learned to knock harder on the small hill. None of that arrived in one frightening day. It arrived coin by coin, dose by dose, until the man who paid it called the paying ordinary.

Look at a week of it added up and the picture hardens further. Twenty doses a day is one hundred and forty doses a week. Seven thousand doses a year. Each one working hard on the mouth and throat and chest while doing nothing for the hunger it claimed to quiet except to still for minutes the want the last dose left. Year on year the damage grows while the help stays at zero. That is not a pleasure with a cost beside it. That is a cost with a pleasure painted over it.

Morning after morning it leaves you stale, breathless and chained.

Read that sentence at the table where you once sealed the meal with smoke, because it holds there as it holds at the kettle and the step. Stale in the mouth where supper should linger. Breathless on the stairs where breath should come without thought. Chained to the hour where attention should be on work and talk and company. Three words for the permanent state the carton rents out as normal.

Count the hours the carton rents besides the body. The stepped-out conferences missed mid-sentence. The films paused at the door. The meals left to cool while you stood in rain with a tube. The nights cut into twenty small hungers, each dressed as a pause, each ending whether shoulders had eased or not. We called those breaks. The non-smoker beside us took the same step, breathed the same cold air, watched the same roofs, and returned rested without signing anything. The step did not fail us. The signing taxed it.

I lay no timetable over your body. I state only what your own tray and coat and stairs have told you for years. Small doses, plainly packed, taken all day, leave a large bill for a small office that was never needed.

I land that picture at full weight and I do not soften it, because you have paid it and you know it true. And I tell you now, in the same breath, do not make your move from fright.

Do not quit because the picture scares you. Fear quits last a week and then the fear fades and the old voice returns. Make your move because the picture is false to your nature and you see who painted it. The carton painted the grey and sold you the half-inch lift as dawn. Once you see the painting, you do not need to be scared of it. You only need to stop paying the painter.

Your body does not ask to be frightened into health. It asks to be left alone to do its own office, as you watched it do at the kettle and the plate and the step. Breath returns. Taste returns. The stairs grow kinder. The morning grows clearer. Not by effort, not by handling from outside, but because you stop taxing what already knows its work.

## THE TWO FEARS WITH ONE OWNER

Now we come to the pull that holds most smokers longer than any hunger, and I ask for your open mind here most of all.

You stand between two fears and both shout at once.

On one side, the fear of failing. "I have tried twenty times with patches and promises and morning vows. I will try again and fail again and prove I am weak." The drawer remembers each attempt. The calendar remembers each broken date. The thought of another failure burns worse than the cough, so the hand stays with the known carton rather than risk the shame of another fall.

On the other side, the fear of succeeding into a stranger. "If I stop, who will I be on the break, at the drink, after supper. Will the talk still include me. Will the day feel flat without the punctuation. Will I become dull and restless and hard to live with." The break without smoke looked thin until you lived it. The night without smoke still looks thin from here, so the hand stays with the known self rather than risk becoming no one.

I know both fears because both held me at the same table. I feared the shame of failing and I feared the flatness of succeeding, and I called that proof I was caught for life.

The fact is, both ropes belong to the same hands that packed your want.

Who taught you that stopping is hard and humiliating. The same shelf that sold you milds as careful, and filters as protection, and twenty attempts as proof of your weakness. The failures were not proof you are weak. They were proof the method was wrong, demanding strained effort against a belief still intact, while the carton stayed precious in the mind. With the belief unchanged, any attempt must fail, and each failure then sells the next carton as mercy.

Who taught you that life without will be flat and strange. The same voices that told you smoke seals the meal and gathers the circle and punctuates the break. You have already lived three rooms without it and found them fuller, not emptier. The flatness you fear is not the world without smoke. It is a painted picture from the Smokescreen, made to keep you buying.

"I will fail again." "I will succeed into someone I do not know." Two sentences, opposite directions, one owner.

Ask the questions whose only honest answer cuts both ropes at once.

If failure proved you weak, why did strong-willed men and women fail beside you in exactly the same way, at the same hours, with the same midnight counting. If success meant becoming a stranger, why did the kettle morning and the lingering plate and the breathed break leave you more yourself, not less, with hands busier and taste brighter and talk unbroken. If both fears told the truth, why do they contradict each other, one warning you cannot stop and the other warning you cannot live once you have.

You know the answer because your own week gave it once the rooms were lived through. The fears do not describe you. They describe the carton defending itself from inside your head.

That pull both ways at once is the tug-of-war of fear.

Feel its geometry and the war ends. A tug-of-war needs two sides pulling. Here both ends are held by the trap. One rope says you will fail and be shamed. The other says you will succeed and be flat. Drop the belief that the carton ever gave, and both ropes fall slack at once, because neither fear has any ground left to stand on. There is no shame in seeing a con, and there is no flatness in a morning that wakes by itself.

Picture a man kept long in a small cell who fears both the locked door and the open corridor. Behind him he fears another night on the narrow bed, counted and coughed through. Before him he fears the wide corridor where he must walk as himself without walls to lean on. Both fears point at the cell as home. Both keep him pacing between bed and door. The fact is, the cell taught both fears, and the corridor needs no learning. He already knows how to walk. He walked before the cell, and his legs remember.

We lived that pacing for years. Morning fear said you will fail at the door. Evening fear said you will be lost past it. Both spoke in your voice, both kept the hand on the known carton. Once you see one jailer holds both keys, the pacing stops. You do not need to be taught the corridor. You only need to see the cell was never your nature.

I predict tonight for you, as I lived it myself. You will turn the pack over after supper and read the small print you never read, and the old thought will arrive on schedule, "just keep this one comfort while the fears are loud." You will hear it arrive and you will know its maker now, and the thought will pass like talk from another table. Tomorrow at the kettle you will breathe before you think and the tug will call thinly at its hour and thin again by noon. That thinning is not a warning. It is the sound of ropes going slack.

So see the seller and the fears together, and let the seeing do the work. We fell in together, you and I and millions beside us, through phoney promise and measured dose. You walk out by seeing, not by straining, and what you walk toward is the same day with the paint washed off.

9. SEE THE SELLER BEHIND THE SMOKE
Remember who built the trap and why.

Carry that remembering as warmth into tonight and tomorrow. When the hand finds the box, remember the room where the box was packed. When the filter touches the lips, remember the vents beneath the fingers. When either fear speaks, failure or flatness, remember the hands holding both ropes. Each remembering takes a coin from the maker and gives the hour back to you, steady and plain and entirely yours.

SUMMARY
- I now see that my want was built by a seller who packed a drug as pleasure, and I believe the box never served my nature.
- I now know that milds and filters never softened the dose in human hands, and I believe no shelf holds a careful tube.
- I now see that both fears pulling opposite ways belong to the same trap, and I believe dropping the false credit drops both ropes at once.
