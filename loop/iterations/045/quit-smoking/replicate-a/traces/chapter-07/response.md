Chapter 7
THE ITCH IT PRETENDS TO SCRATCH

IN THIS CHAPTER: the meter on the corner, a day of fading doses, the small tug under the ribs.

*That lift never raised you above normal; it only put back a little of what the last dose took.*

The corner outside the bakery holds a parking meter with a scratched glass eye. I passed it each morning with a pack in the pocket and a lighter that knew my thumb better than any pen. Coins sat beside the keys. Tobacco sat beside the coins. The day sat ahead with its small bells for lighting, and I obeyed each bell as if it rang from outside me.

Watch one such day with me now, hour by hour, because the drawer stands closed and the glasses stand emptied and the numbers stand back in their rooms, and what remains is the bare mechanism ticking under the day. No wrappers now. No counts. Only the hand, the mouth, the fading quiet between lights, and the talk that covered it.

The first dose goes down before the kettle boils. Feet have not found slippers before the hand finds the pack. The shoulders drop half an inch. The head clears a fraction. The kitchen looks kinder for two breaths. Then the bowl is washed and the coat is lifted and the kinder look thins before the door shuts.

The second dose waits at the bus stop. The third rides the mid-morning pause between tasks. The fourth seals the meal. The fifth steadies the hands after hard words on the phone. The sixth keeps company on the walk back. Each lighting brings the same brief softening, and each softening thins within minutes while the ashtray fills. By night the mouth tastes of ash and the chest feels heavier and the mind still claims the day would have collapsed without those pauses.

I lived that day for years and called each softening a gift. I told myself the dose lifted me, calmed me, carried me over small walls. I did not watch what it lifted me from, nor how fast the lift leaked away, nor who built the low it lifted me out of. I credited the match with the kindness of the morning. I blamed the hours for a heaviness the match itself renewed.

## THE METER ON THE CORNER

Stand at that meter a while, because it shows the day more honestly than memory does.

Drop a coin and the needle jumps. The red flag drops. You have bought time. The eye says paid. For a short while you walk away without glancing back. Then the needle sinks. The flag creeps up. The eye says due. You dig for another coin, not because the street fined you, but because the bought time ran out by design.

We lived by such a post from first light to last. Each puff put a coin in. Each coin bought a brief quiet under the ribs. The quiet never held past the hour. The eye never stayed paid. The hand learned the sinking rhythm better than any clock, reaching at the same bends of the day, after food, after work finished, after words turned sharp, during coffee, during waiting, during walking. The reaching felt like hunger for the thing. It was hunger for the needle to rise again before it hit due.

That post on the corner taught me how the nicotine trap keeps its hours.

See the order plainly, as you checked the order of work and pellet and the order of food and lid. The dose did not meet a low that the day brought. The last dose lit the low that the next dose then quieted. Smoke pulls the drug into the blood through the lungs, and the head feels it within breaths, and the tug softens for a little while, and then the softening thins and the tug returns and calls it proof that another dose is needed. The circle looks like help from inside. From outside it reads as billing.

You say the softening felt real. You say no picture of meters and needles can talk away those two breaths when the match burned down and the shoulders dropped and the buzz softened the edges. You say the classroom and the table proved nothing against the body, and the body asked and was answered.

I grant those two breaths without argument, as one who waited for them each hour. They lived at the lighting and nowhere else. They never taught one student. They never cooked one dish. They never settled one quarrel. They never stayed past the cup cooling. A kindness that lives only at the lighting and dies before the work resumes is not a kindness to the day. It is a coin that buys back a minute of the hour the last coin sold.

Ask with the plain honesty you brought to the drawer.

If that softening raised the day, why did the same hour yesterday need the same softening again. If the morning dose fixed the morning, why did the bus stop demand another before the bell rang. If the meal dose completed the meal, why did the taste turn to ash within minutes and ask for water to wash it away.

You know the answers already, because your own day gave them while you watched. The softening never stacked. It never carried forward. It never left the day higher than it found it. It only lifted the needle off due for a short while, then let it sink again toward the same red. A meter that must be fed all day does not enrich the driver. It taxes him.

Follow the needle past noon, because morning kindness hides how steep the afternoon tax grows.

Lunch ends and the mouth still holds taste when the hand already moves. The pause between tasks shortens. Where morning allowed two hours between coins, afternoon allows one, then less. The mind blames workload and weather and other mouths. The meter knows better. Each coin buys a shorter quiet because the patch underneath widened while we praised the scratch. By four the shoulders sit higher. By six the throat feels coated. Night does not arrive as rest. It arrives as arrears, coin upon coin laid all day to stand where the non-smoker stood at waking without paying once. Check those arrears against your purse and your breath and your evening temper, and see who grew rich from your day of kindnesses.

## THE DAY THAT NEVER RISES

Hold that taxed day beside a day that never smoked, because the comparison breaks the last kindly picture we kept.

A man who never smoked wakes with his own morning mouth and his own morning chest. He coughs less. He tastes the bread whole. He meets the sharp phone call with nerves that jump and then settle on their own while the problem sits on the desk waiting for hands. He finishes the paragraph and feels the finish ring without signing it in smoke. He does not rise higher than you at your best minute after lighting. He simply stays where you only touch briefly before sinking again.

We believed our best minutes after lighting proved the dose gave something extra. We mistook a return for a rise. The fact is simple once seen from beside that other day. The dose never carried us above the level where the non-smoker lives all day without thinking. It only carried us back toward that level for minutes, from a low the last dose dug, then dropped us again to dig again. The lift was a loan against itself, repaid within the hour with interest taken from breath and taste and calm attention.

Think of tight shoes worn all morning for the joy of loosening them at noon. The loosening feels sweet. The feet sing. A watcher might swear the loosening healed the feet. But the watcher knows the shoes caused the ache the loosening then quieted. We wore the shoes and praised the loosening and never blamed the laces we tied ourselves each hour with our own hands.

You say the shoes picture insults a real lift. You say the dose sharpened the mind before driving and settled the hands before speaking and sweetened the pause between tasks, and no non-smoker knows that sharpening because no non-smoker lost it.

I hear you, because I praised that sharpening through long drives and late stories. But check the sharpening against the whole drive, not only its first two minutes. The mind felt sharp while the drug arrived, then dulled again before the junction, then asked for another sharpening before the town sign. A sharpener that dulls the blade it sharpens is not a sharpener. It is a renter of edges, taking back each edge it lends before the road straightens. The non-smoker drove the same road without renting. His attention never sang at the lighting, and never sank at the hour. It stayed. Ours sang and sank and sang, and we called the singing a gift while paying for the sinking with each pack.

Take the same lens to the pavement walk you once signed in smoke.

We stepped out saying the legs needed a dose to complete the pause. Watch the walk whole. The first pulls warm the throat while the street opens. Then the throat dries before the corner. The legs keep moving while attention narrows to when the next pause can be lit. The non-smoker walks the same street with eyes on roofs and dogs and shop glass, his pause complete without signing. Our pause looked complete only while the drug arrived, then thinned before the turn. The street did not change between our walks and his. The signing changed. We credited the dose with the kindness of moving limbs and open sky, while the dose only lent back for minutes the calm attention the last dose had taken.

The brainwashing did the pricing. Pictures laid smoke across every finish and every trouble until both looked as if they belonged to the dose. The con read from those pictures, not from the body. The body asked for a small quieting it had been taught to ask for. The pictures told you the quieting was calm and company and taste and courage all at once. The body asked for a coin. The pictures told you the coin bought the house. Which of the two kept you lighting, the coin or the house.

You know already. The coin never bought the house. It only paid the meter past the next corner.

## THE ITCH AND THE SCRATCH

There is a plainer picture for this circle, and I want you to wear it a day because it follows the hands better than needles and posts.

Late summer leaves a bite on the ankle. It itches. The nails find it in sleep. Morning shows a red point. You scratch and the scratch feels kind. Warmth spreads. The itch quiets. For a minute the ankle feels better than before the bite. You praise the nails.

By noon the point has widened. The skin burns at the edges. The itch returns deeper. You scratch again and praise again. By night the ankle carries a raw patch that weeps and stings at the sheet. Each scratch quieted the minute and enlarged the wound that made the minute unbearable. The nails never healed. They farmed.

We farmed such a patch under the ribs with our own hands and praised the farming as care.

The first smoke of youth dug the first point. Few of us recall pleasure in those early lights. Most recall cough and dizziness and a bitter mouth endured to look grown among friends. The patch was planted then, not the pleasure. Each later dose scratched the point the last dose kept open. Each scratch felt kind while the drug arrived. Each kindness thinned within minutes while the patch widened underneath, asking for deeper pulls, harder draws, more frequent pauses, exactly as the mouth once laboured over small vent holes without knowing the lips covered them. The flavour changed across years. The farming rhythm did not.

Follow those early lights forward, because each year tightened the farming without changing its name.

A pack lasted two days, then one, then less than one, while the hands swore the days grew harder and the nerves grew thinner. The days did not grow harder. The patch grew wider and asked for more scratching to buy the same two breaths of quiet. The throat learned deeper pulls. The fingers learned shorter intervals. The mind learned to read every bend, coffee poured, keys lifted, phone down, as a bell for nails. None of those bells rang from coffee or keys or phones. They rang from the widening point that needed scratching on schedule. We called that schedule habit and blamed long years for its weight. Years only kept the accounts. The nails kept the wound.

Watch the farming across one evening without flinching, because this is where the kindest excuse dies.

Supper ends. The itch stirs. You light and the itch quiets while the plate is cleared. The talk at the table feels warmer for two breaths. Then the warmth thins before the cups are washed. The itch stirs again, edgier. You blame the washing. You light again on the step and the step feels kinder for two breaths. Then the kindness thins before the door shuts. You blame the night chill. You light again before bed and the pillow feels nearer for two breaths. Then sleep comes slower with ash on the tongue. Three kindnesses. Three thinnings. One patch wider at midnight than at supper. Who helped whom across those hours, the nails or the ankle.

Ask the three plain questions that close this room, and answer as you answer when no one listens.

If the scratch healed, why did the itch return before the ash cooled. If the scratch gave, why did the evening need three gifts to stand where one gift once stood. If the scratch belonged to pleasure, why did midnight feel heavier than supper despite three pleasures laid over it.

No honest throat answers that the scratch healed. It quieted and widened. It soothed and farmed. It borrowed from midnight to gild supper, then sent the bill to midnight with interest. Once seen, the kindness cannot be praised again, because the praise belonged to the minute while the cost belonged to the day.

It never fixed the itch. It caused it.

Let that sentence stand where the smoke stood, because it carries the whole day in eight words. The dose never met a need the day brought. It met the need the last dose left. The rescuer wore the same coat as the robber. The hand that soothed at lighting was the hand that stung at the hour. We thanked the nails for moments while the ankle paid for days.

## THE SMALL TUG AND THE LOUD TALK

If the farming picture holds, one fear still stands, and I will meet it fully because it kept me lighting long after the pleasures thinned.

You fear the hunger itself. You fear a gnawing under the ribs that will not be reasoned with. You fear nights pacing the kitchen while the mind counts hours. You fear temper shortening with children and hands shaking at the wheel and sleep breaking at three. You say the talk of meters and bites insults the sweat of past nights when you held out and suffered, and the suffering proves the need is mighty and will return mightier once the last dose dies.

I know that fear from the inside, because it owned my evenings longer than any taste did. I stood at windows and told myself the body had spoken its final word. I counted hours and read each hour as proof I was chained to something larger than me. The counting itself fed what I counted.

I remember midnight in the kitchen with the tap dripping and the clock loud. The hands shook around a cup. The mind said the shaking proved the dose was food. I watched the shaking with the old reading and it grew. I watched it later with clear seeing and it showed its size, a thin tremor from taught bells, not a storm from nature. The room did not change between those watchings. The reading changed. What I once read as a mighty tide showed as ripples where coins had splashed for years and then stopped splashing.

Separate now the small thing from the loud talk over it, because the two were glued together and the glue was the whole con.

What remains when the talk stops is only an empty, slightly restless, slightly edgy little tug that loses strength when unfed.

Feel its true size without pictures laid over it. It sits low under the ribs, light as hunger before supper, thin as tiredness before bed. It does not tear. It does not burn. It asks, emptily, edgily, for the small quieting it was taught to ask for. Then it passes if unfed, then returns thinner, then passes again. In most bodies it sharpens for a short spell after the last dose and then loosens day by day while attention returns to work and food and walking. No two bodies keep identical hours, and I promise no calendar over your skin. I state only its nature as I found it and as countless quiet mornings confirmed once the talk thinned. Small. Brief beside a life. Already dying once unfed.

That tug is the voice of the Nipper, a small creature already losing strength.

Hear how small I mean. A gnat against a window, not a hound at the door. A bell heard from another street, not a bell in your room. It lives only while fed at its hours. Miss its hour and it rings thinner next time. Miss again and the street sounds louder than the bell. No mighty enemy demands battle here. Only a tiny feeder asks for its coin through the old slot while the slot rusts shut underneath.

The loud talk over it belongs to the Smokescreen, which turns a small hunger into six large losses.

That talk says the tug means you will lose calm and company and taste and courage and pause and self all at once. It borrows the voice of past evenings and dresses the gnat as a hound. It counts hours aloud to keep attention fixed on the next hour that must not bring a dose. It reads each thinning as danger rather than dying. It did the same office during counted attempts, when one part of the mind ordered the hands to leave the pack alone while the other part kept a fond count of what the hands were missing, until the small tug drank the attention and swelled in memory far past its body. We believed we were starving the hunger by watching it. We were schooling it by watchfulness.

Once the talk is seen through, you walk out of it easily, immediately and permanently.

Read that sentence slowly, because it holds the hinge of this whole chapter. The walking is not done against the tug. The tug asks little and fades fast. The walking is done against the talk that dressed the tug as fate. Refuse the dress and the body follows without battle. The tug thins underneath while the days fill over it with work noticed fully and food tasted fully and streets walked with attention undivided. Neither voice can keep you once the stamp is refused. The coin stops. The bell thins. The pictures lose their weather.

You say the body must still be obeyed. You say a hunger, however small, still proves the dose gave something the body lacks, and something lacking must be paid.

Check whose lack you mean. The non-smoker lacks nothing at your worst hour, yet lives your best minute all day without thinking. His body asks for food when hungry and sleep when tired and water when dry, and never asks for smoke through any hour. Our bodies asked because we taught them to ask by feeding at fixed bells for years, then read the taught asking as native need. A taught bell is not a native hunger. It rings from training, not from nature. Let it ring unfed and training loosens. That is not theory. It is what happens when payment stops and attention stays on the life the hands produce.

## OTHER HANDS, OTHER LIGHTS

One influence still reaches for your hands from outside, and I place it last because it speaks loudest once the inner talk thins.

Step outside with me at break time and watch with new eyes, because yesterday you watched as a member and today you watch as a witness.

Hands cup flames in wind. Shoulders hunch. A man draws deep and coughs and draws again. A woman taps ash and checks the time and taps again. Talk pauses for lighting and resumes thinner. Laughter pauses for coughing and resumes lower. Someone says the dose steadies him. His thumb trembles on the wheel of the lighter. Someone says the dose gathers her. Her eyes keep leaving the circle for the door. Someone offers round the pack with a wide smile. The smile does not reach past the lighting. Two breaths of softening show on each face, then the old line returns between brows before the butt is stubbed.

Yesterday you read those faces as proof of pleasure. You borrowed their softening for your own next hour. You feared standing apart while the circle glowed. You heard one will not hurt as kindness.

Stay a minute longer at the offer itself, because it carries the whole firewall in one hand.

A pack tilts toward you. Light glints on foil. The voice says go on, one with us, and the circle watches. Yesterday that tilt felt like belonging. Today read what the tilt asks. It asks you to scratch with them so their own scratching keeps its praise. It asks you to lend your throat as witness that their farming is pleasure. You can meet the eyes, thank the hand, leave the dose where it sits, and keep the pause. Belonging never lived in the shared scratch. It lived in the shared pause, the talk, the night, the work laid down together. Those remain when the dose stays in its box.

Read them now with the meter and the bite beside you. Each face shows the same circle you tracked, coin in, brief quiet, needle sinking, hand already planning the next coin while praising the last. The glow at lighting is the scratch warming the bite. The line between brows before stubbing is the patch widening underneath. No face in that circle rises above the level where the non-smoker stands all day. Each only touches it briefly, then pays to touch again. Once seen, the circle cannot lend judgment, because its judgment comes from inside the same farming it praises.

You say refusal sounds cold. You say these are friends, generous with packs and lights and pauses, and turning clear eyes upon them betrays warmth.

Warmth stays. Blindness goes. I honour each smoker in that circle as I honour you, a decent person farmed by pictures he did not draw. You can stand among them, take the pause without the puff, hear the offer without borrowing its reading, keep your own counsel while keeping your kindness. Their hands do not decide your ribs. Their lights do not measure your day. The talk that once passed hand to hand as wisdom now reads as weather, and weather needs no obedience.

So at the point where the meter stands exposed and the bite stands confessed and the tug stands small and the talk stands named, here, then, is our seventh rule. Take it in the same warm, firm spirit as the six before it. You have weighed the so-called lift and found it a loan. You have watched the scratch farm the itch it praised. You have felt the tug shrink once the talk over it thinned. The only hand that can still reach your pocket is the hand of another farmer praising his own nails at the break-time door. Wish him well and keep your own seeing. His kindness is real. His reading is the farming talking.

7. REFUSE TO BE INFLUENCED BY OTHER SMOKERS
Watch them with clear eyes and keep your own judgment.

Carry that watching as warmth, not coldness. The man with the trembling thumb labours under pictures you have now stepped outside of. The woman watching the door counts coins you no longer count. When they tell you the dose steadies, hear the old talk speaking through kind mouths, not the body reporting. When they press one as friendship, hear the meter asking for another coin, not friendship asking for company. You owe them courtesy and you owe them no obedience. Your ribs are not their ribs. Your day is not their circle.

Tonight the old bells will ring at their hours, after food and after tasks and during waiting. Greet each bell with the new seeing, and the seeing shortens with use. First the meter picture comes whole, coin and needle and sinking flag. Then only the bite picture comes, nails and widening point. Then only the tug comes, small and unfed and thinning. Then a breath at the window is enough, then a smile before the pocket is touched. Each bell met with seeing wins back a stretch of the hour. Each stretch won back leaves less static and more of yourself.

SUMMARY
- I now see that each dose only quiets a hunger the last dose lit, and never lifts the day above where a non-smoker lives.
- I know the tug under my ribs is small and brief beside a life, and the loud talk over it is what kept my hands reaching.
- I understand that other smokers cannot lend me judgment while I watch their circle with clear eyes and keep my own.
