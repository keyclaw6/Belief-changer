Chapter 1
READ THIS FIRST AND BE FREE

IN THIS CHAPTER: the stairwell at work, the landing window, the chair where you sit to read.

*Stopping can be easy and lasting without force, and you can prove it to yourself by reading on with an open mind.*

This book will show you how to stop smoking in a way that is easy and permanent and takes no willpower at all — you find that hard to believe — read on.

I know how that sentence lands. You have heard big claims before. You have stood at the chemist counter and paid for boxes that promised help, and you have woken on Monday mornings full of resolve and gone to bed the same night angry at yourself. You believe stopping must hurt. You believe it must take effort and gritted teeth and white knuckles, because every attempt you ever made hurt, and everyone around you talks as if pain were the price. I understand that belief completely. I held it myself for years. And I tell you now, in plain words at the start, that the pain you remember was never proof of what stopping has to be. It was proof of the way you were told to do it.

Take the stairs with me for a moment. Not as a lesson. Just as a picture you already know.

## The Landing Window

Picture a stairwell you climb most days. Mine was at work, between the ground floor and the second, two flights of painted concrete with a rubber edge on each step and a small window on the landing halfway up. I climbed those stairs a dozen times a day for years. I knew every mark on the walls. I knew the hum of the strip light and the smell of dust and warm pipes.

One wet Tuesday I climbed behind a colleague of mine, a man about my age who never smoked. He carried the same sort of bag I carried. He wore the same sort of shoes. He was no athlete. He talked over his shoulder as he climbed, about a delivery that had gone missing, and he did not pause for breath at all. I answered him, and I heard my own voice come out thin. By the second flight my chest was working hard. My legs were fine. My legs could have walked all day. It was my breathing that had changed. I reached the landing window and stopped as if I wanted to look at the rain, and he stopped with me out of politeness, and I stood there getting my breath back while he stood there waiting, and I knew with complete certainty what had made the difference between us. You know that moment too. You have lived some version of it. We all have our own stairwell.

I want you to hold that picture without flinching. I am not telling it to frighten you. I am telling it because your body already told you the truth on those stairs, long before any book did. Your body keeps the score honestly. It does not argue or excuse. It simply shows you, step by step, what each dose costs. And here is the part I need you to hear: the fact that your breathing labours on the stairs does not mean you are broken. It means you are observant. Something in you noticed. Something in you brought you to this page.

Do you want to stop smoking? Of course you do. That is why you are holding this book.

If you truly chose each cigarette with a glad heart, why are you reading a book with this title? If each dose gave you what you hoped for, why did you need another one an hour later, and another after that? And if stopping were purely a matter of character, why have so many strong and stubborn people you admire failed at it and blamed themselves? Let those questions sit. They need no clever answer. They point one way.

I smoked the way you smoke. Let me tell you plainly who I was, so you know who is talking to you.

## I Smoked the Way You Smoke

I started young, the way most of us start, with borrowed doses and a cough I laughed off to look grown among older boys. I did not start because I weighed anything up. I started because the others did it and I wanted my share of whatever they seemed to have. That is how we all begin. Nobody sits down at fourteen with a pencil and lists the advantages of inhaling smoke. We copy. We pose. We cough. Then one day the coughing stops and the pattern holds, and from that day on we tell ourselves a story about why we carry on.

I carried on for years. I was a daily smoker, morning to night, in all weathers, in all moods. I smoked when work pressed and when work went quiet. I smoked while I waited for buses and while I talked on the phone and while I stood outside pubs in the rain pretending I preferred the cold air. I told myself the same things you tell yourself. I told myself it steadied me. I told myself it marked the pauses. I told myself the day would be grey without it. I believed every word at the time, the way you believe it now, and I say that with warmth toward both of us, because we were not fools. We were learners in a school that taught one lesson badly.

I failed the way you have failed. I set dates and broke them. I cut down and crept back up. I made bargains with myself about which doses counted and which did not, and I always lost the argument with my own hand. I know what it is to stand in a doorway at midnight finishing a dose you do not even want, cold at the ears, thinking that tomorrow must be different. I know what it is to wake with a sour mouth and swear the oath, and to be lighting up again before lunch with a hot face. I know the arithmetic of shame, the counting and hiding of it, the crushed packs at the bottom of bins. If you think no one understands the hold this pattern has on you, understand that I do. I lived inside it.

And then I got out. Not by becoming a different person. Not by growing a will of iron. I got out by seeing something I had looked at for years without noticing, and once I saw it I could not unsee it, and stopping followed as simply as putting down a bag you suddenly notice is full of stones. You do not need to be stronger than me. You do not need to be stronger than anyone. You need to see what I saw. That is the whole of my qualification for writing to you, and it is enough. I was deep in it, I failed at it, and now I am clear of it, and I have spent the years since showing others the same sight until their hands stopped reaching on their own.

You may think your case is special. Most of us think so. You may think you smoke more than I did, or that you have smoked longer, or that your nerves are worse, or that your life is harder. I hear that thought with respect, because I thought it too. Every smoker keeps a private list of reasons why this will work for others but not for them. "You never smoked the way I smoke." "You never loved it the way I love it." "You never leaned on it the way I lean on it." I smoked heavily and I loved the ritual and I leaned on it through every kind of day, and the pattern still let go of me the moment I understood it. The pattern does not check your list. It works the same way in all of us, and it comes apart the same way in all of us. The fact is, there is no special smoker. There are only smokers, all caught by the same con, all able to walk out by the same door.

So let me state my promise again, without trimming it to sound modest. This book will show you how to stop smoking in a way that holds for good, without a daily fight inside your head. You will not need to measure your days or avoid your life or hold your breath when others light up. You will read, you will see, and you will stop. People tell me every week that this sounds too neat to be true. I welcome that doubt. Doubt is honest. It means you are listening with your eyes open, and eyes open is exactly how I need you.

## Why You Can Trust an Easy Promise

Your doubt has a history, and I want to honour it before I ask anything of you.

You have been disappointed by easy promises before. The chemist shelf, the magazine article, the friend with the new answer — each one sounded confident and each one left you where you started, or lower. So when I say this can be easy, a part of you braces. You think, "Here comes another one." That bracing is not proof that I am wrong. It is proof that you have been let down. A person who has never been let down has no need to brace. You brace because you hoped before and the hope cost you. I respect that wound, and I do not ask you to pretend it is not there.

Think about what easy actually means here. I do not mean you will never think of a cigarette again after page ten. I mean the work is understanding, not enduring. A puzzle is hard before you see the answer and easy after. Nobody calls you weak for struggling with a lock before you held the key. That is the kind of easy I am talking about. The difficulty you remember came from pushing a door that understanding opens.

"But I have no willpower," you say. "That is my trouble. Others can do it and I cannot."

Let me answer that directly, because it matters more than anything else you believe about yourself. The smokers I have known who failed again and again were not the soft ones. They were the stubborn ones, the ones who held down jobs and raised families and kept going through hardship. It takes a dogged kind of persistence to keep paying for something that makes your chest heave on the stairs and still show up every day with your head high. If you lacked backbone you would have folded long ago under far smaller loads. The trouble was never the size of your backbone. The trouble was the direction you were told to push. You were told to overpower a want that your own view of cigarettes kept feeding. That is like being told to hold a beach ball under water while someone keeps pumping air into it, and then being called weak when your arms ache. The ache proves nothing about your arms. It proves the task was wrong.

Ask yourself this, honestly, and answer in your own head. If stopping were truly a test of strength, why do people who pass every other test of strength fail this one? Why do nurses and soldiers and teachers and parents who lift and carry and endure all day find themselves beaten by a rolled tube of paper and leaf? Does that really look like weakness to you? Or does it look like a riddle they were never shown how to read? You already know which answer fits. You have watched strong people hide to smoke and hate the hiding. Strength was never the missing piece. Sight was.

I mean what I say in plain words, and I want you to hear the shape of it because you will hear it again when the time comes to act: you can stop smoking easily, immediately and permanently.

Read that line twice. Note what it does not contain. It does not contain months of misery. It does not contain a lifetime of dodging. It promises three things together that the world told you cannot go together — that it can be easy, that it can happen at once rather than by inches, and that it can hold. I state all three together on purpose, because trimming any one of them would leave you a smaller hope than you deserve. An easy stop that fades by spring is no good to you. A lasting stop bought with years of misery is no good either. You need all three, and all three are on offer here, in this order, through understanding first and action second.

You do not need more effort. You need clearer sight.

That is my whole claim in this first sitting. Everything after this page is the showing, not the straining. Keep your strength for your life. Lend me your attention for the book.

## What I Ask Before We Start

I ask very little, and I ask it openly, the way one person asks another before a walk neither has taken together.

First, I ask for your attention as an investigator, not as a pupil. Do not take my word for anything you can check against your own days. When I describe the stairs, check it against your stairs. When I describe the midnight doorway, check it against your doorway. When I describe the hand moving almost before the thought, watch your own hand tomorrow and see if I told the truth. You are not here to memorise my phrases. You are here to test what I say against the life you actually live. If I am wrong about your life, you will catch me quickly, and you should. If I am right, each small recognition will do more work than any lecture could.

Second, I ask for fairness toward yourself while you read. You may catch yourself thinking, "How could I have missed this?" when something lands. Meet that thought kindly. We all missed it. We grew up among smokers, among advertisements and film scenes and older friends who made it look adult and settled. We heard the same talk from every side until the talk sounded like our own voice. A person raised on a wrong map is not stupid for taking wrong turns. They are simply holding the wrong map. This book hands you a truer one. There is no blame in having walked the old roads. There is only sense in folding them away.

Third, and this is the one I want you to carry like a pocket stone through every page, I ask for the kind of hearing you would give a friend who told you they had found a way out and wanted to show you before you judged. You would not interrupt them on the second sentence. You would not decide on the third. You would hear them to the end and then weigh it whole. That is all I ask here. Hear the argument to the end. Let each part arrive before you rule on it. A half-heard case is easy to dismiss and hard to learn from. A fully heard case answers its own doubts as it goes.

People sometimes tell me they fear being talked into something. "What if I agree while I read and it all falls apart on Monday?" That fear does you credit. It shows you want something solid, not a mood. So test me on solid ground. I am not asking for your enthusiasm in advance. I am asking for a fair run at the evidence of your own days. Moods pass. Sight stays.

In plain terms, and I put it plainly because plain is what lasts: you have nothing to lose and everything to gain.

Weigh that sentence the way you would weigh any honest offer. What does reading on cost you? A few evenings in a chair. What does it offer? An end to the payments and the coughing on the stairs and the midnight doorways and the sour mornings, and an end to the private scolding that follows you between doses. No one can promise you a new life in every particular. I do not promise riches or luck or the absence of trouble. I promise you this book costs you nothing but attention, and it opens a door you have knocked on for years. A person who refuses such an offer out of habit is not being careful. They are paying good money to keep a doubt they could settle by reading.

And now, at the point where asking matters most, I lay down the single rule for our walk together. Take it in the spirit it is given, which is warm and firm at once:

1. KEEP AN OPEN MIND
Give this book a fair hearing and it will free you.

That is the whole of it. You do not need to agree with me yet. You do not need to feel confident yet. You need only to keep the door open while we look. A closed mind cannot see a new map, however plainly it is spread. An open one can, and yours can, because you have already shown the two signs of openness that matter: you picked up this book, and you have read this far.

## How We Walk Through This Book

Let me tell you how we will go, so you can settle into the chair and read without bracing for shocks.

We will start where you are. We will look at ordinary days — stairs and corridors, buses and desks, waiting rooms and doorways — and notice what actually happens in them. I will show you my own years inside the pattern, because a guide who never stood where you stand has no right to point. We will keep the language plain throughout. Short words where short words serve. Pictures where pictures serve.

You will notice something about my manner as we go, and I name it now so it does not puzzle you. I speak warmly to you and bluntly about the pattern. That is deliberate. You and I are on the same side of the table. We both smoked. We both paid. We both told ourselves the same stories. The pattern is on the other side of the table, and I will talk about it the way you talk about any con once you see through it — without ceremony and without mercy. Never mistake my bluntness about the pattern for bluntness toward you. I have never met a smoker I did not respect, because every smoker I ever met was coping with more than outsiders knew, and doing it while carrying this extra load. My quarrel is with the load, not with the carrier.

You will also notice that I ask questions and answer them straight away. I do that because I have heard every objection you are about to raise, many hundreds of times, in rooms full of smokers with crossed arms. "This cannot work for me." "You do not know my life." "I actually enjoy it." I do not dodge those voices. I print them as you speak them in your head, and then I answer them the way I would answer a friend across the table — directly, with an example you can check. When you hear your own thought on the page, smile. It means the book knows its reader. It means you are in the right place.

Here is what I want you to picture for tomorrow. You will climb some stairs or a slope — at work, at the station, at the shops — and halfway up you will hear your own breathing. In the past that sound brought a flicker of shame and you pushed it away. This time let the sound stay. Notice it the way you noticed it on the landing window day. Say to yourself, "There it is. My body is reporting." That is all. No scolding. No vow. Just attention. Readers write to me about that small moment more than any other in this first part. They tell me it was the first time they listened to their own chest without arguing with it. Attention of that kind is the beginning of everything we do here. It asks nothing of your strength. It asks only that you notice what is true.

Some books tell you to brace for a hard road. I tell you the opposite. Read in comfort, in your usual chair, at your usual hour. Let the book do the work and let your days run as they run. Understanding builds quietly. You will feel it gathering before you can name it.

If your mind throws up the old warning — "Do not get your hopes up, you have hoped before" — answer it the way I answered mine. Hoping before did not harm you. Letting hope go would. The people who stop for good are not the people who never hoped. They are the people who kept enough hope to look once more, carefully, with both eyes. You are looking once more now. That single fact puts you ahead of everyone who told themselves this year would be like last year and left it at that.

I will make you one more plain statement before we step on, and then the showing begins. The pattern you live inside feels personal. It feels woven into your mornings and your breaks and your nights out and your very character. It is not personal. It runs the same way in the nurse and the driver and the student and the grandmother, and it loosens the same way in all of them once seen. That sameness is good news disguised as dull news. It means no one needs a private miracle. We all need the same clear view, and a clear view can be shared, page by page, in ordinary words.

So bring your doubt and bring your hope and bring your stairs. Bring the memory of the landing window and the rain on the glass. Bring the chair you are sitting in and the lamp above it. We begin with what you already know in your bones and move outward from there, one plain sight at a time, until the whole shape stands clear. I am glad you are here. I am glad you chose this evening to start. Keep the one rule, hear me to the end, and watch what happens to your own hand and your own chest as the pages turn.

SUMMARY
- I believed stopping had to mean suffering, and I now know the suffering belonged to the wrong method rather than to stopping itself.
- I believed my doubt proved the promise false, and I now see doubt as the mark of past letdowns rather than as evidence about this book.
- I believed my case was uniquely hard, and I now understand the pattern runs the same in all of us and comes apart the same way.
