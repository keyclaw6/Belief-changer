Chapter 13
MORNINGS, SHOPS AND ORDINARY DAYS

IN THIS CHAPTER: the kettle, the shop queue, the break in fresh air.

*You do not get through ordinary days — you live them whole and free.*

The ashtray from last night sits washed and clean on the side. The room smells of nothing at all. Morning light comes thin through the curtain and there is no small calculation running under it.

You wake as a non-smoker. Not as someone becoming one. As one.

Let me walk beside you through these first ordinary days, because this is where freedom stops being a sentence spoken at a table and starts being a life lived in slippers and shoes and work coats. Nothing new needs learning. Everything old needs enjoying.

## THE MORNING AFTER THE TABLE

Feet find the floor first on that first free morning while the kettle begins to sing.

## THE KETTLE SINGS ON

You stand in the kitchen in a dressing gown with bare feet on cool lino and you put the kettle on the way you always put it on. You hear the low talk start under the lid and rise to a full chatter. You splash water on your face and you feel the cold wake the skin. You breathe to the bottom of the lungs and the air comes in clean and wide. The mouth tastes of sleep. Water and toothpaste clear it. The fingers smell of soap after washing and the smell stays through the next hour.

You pour the hot water and you watch the steam rise straight in the morning light. You wrap both hands round the cup and you feel the warmth move into the palms. You stand by the window with the cup in your hands and you look out at the street waking. A bus passes. A neighbour lifts a blind. A cat walks the wall with its tail high. The tea warms the throat sip by sip. The morning sits quiet round you and you sit quiet inside it.

Toast goes down and comes up browned and hot. Butter melts through in bright pools. You eat standing up by the counter the way you like to eat on workdays, crumbs on the board, second slice already in. Coffee beside it tastes dark and full and warm to its end. Bread tastes of bread. Butter tastes of butter. Coffee tastes of coffee. The tongue meets each flavour clean and straight. You rinse the plate and you set the cup down and the kitchen stays tidy behind you without a second tidy for smell and ash.

You step into the shower and you let the water run its full course over shoulders and back. The heat loosens the night from the muscles. Shampoo lathers and rinses. Water drums on the tiles and steam clouds the glass. You take your time because no part of the morning pulls you toward the doorstep. You dry slowly. You dress slowly. Shirt from the cupboard smells of cupboard and soap and stays that way to noon. Trousers sit comfortable at the waist. Socks warm from the drawer. Shoes laced without hurry. Hair combed in the mirror with breath staying clean after brushing and staying clean on the walk to the bus.

You keep the chair. You keep the window. You keep the slow minutes before work. You sit five minutes with the second half of the tea and you watch light move across the table. You check the bag by the door. Keys. Wallet. Phone. Lunch box with its lid shut tight. Apple and sandwich and a small square of chocolate for later. The bag feels light in the hand. The coat comes off the hook and smells of wool and night air. You shrug into it and you feel the weight settle easy on the shoulders.

The walk to the bus smells of rain on pavement and cold air off the roofs. Your hands stay in the pockets, steady and warm. The bus comes with its hiss and its smell of coats and wet rubber. You climb on and you find a seat by the window. Houses slide past. Shops lift their shutters. A child swings a lunch bag by its strap. The driver calls a greeting to an old man at the front. You watch it all with clear eyes and you feel the morning widen round you rather than narrow toward a stopping place.

Drivers who take the car find the same wideness on the same road. Both hands stay on the wheel. The heater warms clean air. Music plays to its end without a pause at the lights. The car smells of itself, of seats and dust and the pine square hung from the mirror. You park and you step out and you lock the door with nothing to hide under the seat and nothing to carry for later except the lunch box. The street is bright with ordinary business. Your breath clouds and vanishes, clean as the morning itself.

At the desk before work starts you set the bag down and you hang the coat and you wipe the chair and you sit. The screen wakes. The list for the day waits in its neat rows. The kettle in the corner begins its own small song as someone fills it for the first round. You breathe in the room smell of paper and warm machines and floor polish. The day stands ahead uncut, hour after hour, and you stand at its start with empty hands and a full chest.

YIPPEE! I'M FREE!

You say it inside the chest while the tea warms your hands, a plain report of what happened, and then you lift the cup and drink.

The mid-morning passes in its usual business of keys and voices and small errands. Phones lift and settle. Printers hum. A colleague leans over with a paper to sign. Another asks about the match last night. You answer and you laugh and you sign and you hand it back. The clock moves round without cutting the morning into portions. Attention stays where you put it. When a task needs finishing you finish it. When a message needs a short reply you write it. The thread of thought runs unbroken from start to middle to end.

Lunch comes with its own clean pleasure. The sandwich tastes of cheese and pickle and fresh bread to the last bite. The apple crunches sharp and juicy. The small square of chocolate melts slow on the tongue. Water from the tap tastes cold and bright. You eat at the table with the others or on the bench outside when the light is kind, and talk runs its full length without a pause at the door. A story does not lose its teller halfway. A joke lands whole. You stay to the end of it and you laugh with the rest and you carry the plate back inside with the mouth clean and the breath clear.

## THE SHOP QUEUE ON AN ORDINARY AFTERNOON

On the second or third day you need milk and bread and you join the evening queue with a basket on your arm.

The shop smells of warm bread and fruit and floor cleaner. Baskets rattle. The till beeps its steady song. A child asks for sweets and gets told to wait till Saturday. The woman ahead counts coins from a purse with cold fingers. The man behind holds a paper under his arm and checks his watch and smiles at no one in particular. You stand with light hands and empty pockets and you feel the basket handle cool against the palm.

You lift milk and bread onto the belt. The cashier slides them through. You pay once. Coins come back bright into the palm and go back into the pocket for next time. The receipt curls thin and white. You pack the bag and you feel both handles take the weight even in both hands. The door sighs open and the street air meets you whole. Traffic and rain and cold meet you whole.

You walk home the long way when the light is kind. Past the park where the swings move empty in the wind. Past the gardens where smoke rises thin from one chimney and vanishes into blue. Past the school gate where drawings hang bright in the windows. The bag swings easy at your side. The shop smell stays behind you instead of following in hair and coat. The coins in the pocket stay quiet for bread tomorrow.

At home you put the milk in the fridge and the bread in the bin and the paper on the table. The kitchen fills with the small sounds of putting away. Cupboard shut. Fridge hum. Bag folded and hung on its hook. Kettle filled fresh for the evening round. The rooms hold their ordinary warmth. Nothing needs airing before life can continue. Nothing was paused.

Another afternoon brings a longer list. Potatoes. Onions. Soap. Stamps from the post counter at the back. You take a ticket and you wait your turn on the scuffed floor. The queue moves one step at a time. You watch the notices pinned by the till, lost cats and piano lessons and a flat to let above a bakery. You watch the rain speckle the window and run in bright lines down the glass. When your number is called you step up and you ask plainly and you pay and you pack. The errand takes half the time it used to take because attention never leaves the street it walks. One list, one walk, one return.

On Saturday the market fills the square with canvas and voices. Apples in high boxes. Cheese in waxed rounds. Flowers wrapped in paper cones. You walk the rows slowly with a cloth bag over the shoulder. You lift an apple and you feel its cool weight. You smell melon and cut herbs and fresh coffee from the stall at the corner. You talk with the woman about potatoes for roasting. You talk with the man about honey from his own hives. You pay and you pack and you walk on with the bag growing heavier and the morning growing brighter. The market bell rings for closing and pigeons lift off the hall roof in a single grey sheet and settle again.

You do not preach in any queue. You do not tell the cashier or the stranger what you have seen. Visible ease does the talking. You let them see a face that does not tighten at the shutter, hands that do not twitch toward it, a body that does not hurry. When someone asks how you look so well you say plainly that stopping was wonderful and that you enjoy life more. Then you talk about the weather or the football and you let the seeing do its own work.

## THE BREAK IN FRESH AIR

Work breaks come round with their bells and kettles and feet moving toward the door, and you take the same hour and keep the whole of it.

When the kettle boils at work you make your drink the way you always made it. Tea strong with a splash of milk. Coffee dark with one sugar. Water with lemon when the afternoon runs hot. You carry it to the window or the step or the bench where the air moves. You feel the cup warm the palms. Both palms, because neither holds anything else. You breathe to the bottom of the lungs and you feel how much air they want.

Talk stays whole in those minutes. A colleague tells the end of a story about a flooded bathroom and a plumber who sang through the whole repair. Another shows pictures of a new baby asleep in a blue blanket with fists curled tight. Another argues about the penalty on Saturday and replays it with salt shakers on the table. You stay from first word to last. You laugh at the landing because you heard the climb. Staying looks small on paper and feels enormous in the chest. An uncut hour is worth ten cut ones.

The afternoon coffee tells the same story in a smaller cup. It stands alone on the desk and tastes fully of itself, dark and warm and lingering. You drink it slowly by the window and you watch the street below. Buses pull in and out. A delivery man wheels boxes on a trolley. Two schoolgirls share one umbrella and laugh at getting wet anyway. Hands that once tapped and flicked now simply hold the cup. That simple holding steadies the minute because nothing burns it short.

After food the same wholeness waits at the table. Hot food followed by clean mouth followed by clear breath makes a complete sequence. Bread and soup and cheese on a cold day. Rice and greens and bright sauce on a warm one. Roast on Sunday with the windows steamed and the plates hot from the oven. You sit back with the plate empty and the mouth clean and you notice how the meal ends itself. It settles. It warms outward. It calls for nothing after except a glass of water and good talk.

You do not time these breaks to show anything. You do not count how long you can stay inside or how little crosses the mind. You get on with enjoying your life inside the hour you have. One afternoon you look up and you realise the whole morning passed without a single crossing, and you smile at the noticing the way you smile hearing birds after rain.

## THE BREAK THAT STAYS WHOLE

The work break stays whole now, cup warm in both hands and talk running to its end.

## WHAT HAPPENS NEXT AT THE KETTLE

The next morning the kettle sings again and you know its song by heart now.

You wake before the alarm on some days and you lie a minute listening to the house. Pipes tick. A lorry passes far off. The duvet holds its warmth round the shoulders. Then feet to the floor and curtain pulled and light let in. The window shows the same street washed fresh by night. You fill the kettle with yesterday's measure and you thumb the switch and you hear the click that starts the day.

While it heats you move through the small rooms with easy feet. Bathroom tap runs cold then warm. Face washed. Teeth brushed with the brush standing in its usual glass. The mirror shows a face with clear eyes and clean skin. Bedroom bed pulled straight with the corners tucked the way your mother taught you. Clothes laid out the night before waiting on the chair. You dress in the warmth from the kitchen doorway while toast browns and the tea steeps to its right colour.

Breakfast grows richer as days pass because mornings grow calmer. Porridge with honey and a pinch of salt on rainy days, steam rising thick off the bowl. Toast with marmalade sharp and bright on hurried days. Eggs on Sunday with the yolks soft and the soldiers cut thick. Fruit cut into a bowl with yoghurt poured over when the market was kind. Each plate tastes of itself from first bite to last. Each cup empties clean.

You wash the bowl and you wipe the board and you shake crumbs for the birds on the sill. Sparrows come quick and quarrelsome. A blackbird waits its turn on the fence. You watch them a minute with the tea warm in your hand. Then coat on and bag up and door locked behind you. The key turns smooth in the lock. The step smells of wet stone after rain. The day opens ahead like a clean page and you walk into it with steady feet.

Evenings at the kettle run the same gentle course in reverse. Bag unpacked. Shoes off. Slippers on. Water on for soup or pasta or whatever the evening asks. The radio plays low while onions soften in oil. Steam fogs the small window over the sink. You stir and you taste and you add salt and you stir again. Supper served hot at the table with both elbows welcome and no clock running against the plate. Afterwards one wash and one dry and the kitchen shines again for morning.

## WHAT HAPPENS NEXT IN THE QUEUE

The queue keeps teaching its small lessons without words as the week runs on.

Friday brings the big shop with the trolley and the long belt. You push the trolley with one hand and you steer round the displays of oranges stacked high. You choose greens with bright leaves. You choose fish with clear eyes. You lift a heavy bag of rice and you settle it in the bottom where it rides steady. Children ride past standing on trolley ends while parents pretend not to see. Announcements murmur overhead about offers in aisle nine. The belt carries your goods forward in their patient line. Pack, scan, beep, pack. You pay once and you wheel out with both hands on the bar and the wheels rattling over the car park stones.

The corner shop learns your face again in a new way. The man behind the counter nods when you come in for milk at ten. The woman counts your change without looking down. The paper waits in its rack by the door with the headlines shouting their daily excitements. You take yours and you fold it under your arm. The bell over the door rings coming and going. Each visit lasts minutes and leaves nothing behind except a warm nod.

Rainy Tuesday brings the longest queue of the week and you stand in it dry and patient. Coats drip. Umbrellas leave puddles by the door. The till roll runs out and the assistant changes it with quick fingers while the line waits. No one sighs too loud. A toddler sings to herself in her pushchair. An old man tells the woman behind about his roses surviving the frost. You listen and you smile and your turn comes round in its good time. Milk and stamps and soap go into the bag. The door sighs. The wet street shines under the lamps. Home smells of dry towels and supper warming.

## WHAT HAPPENS NEXT ON THE BREAK

The break keeps widening as days add to days.

Monday brings sunshine to the bench outside and you carry your cup out with three others. The air smells of cut grass from the council mowers. A robin works the border by the wall. Someone unwraps biscuits and passes the packet round. You take one and it tastes buttery and sweet with the tea behind it. Talk turns to holidays planned and gardens dug and a car that failed its test on a bald tyre. Laughter runs easy without anyone watching the clock for service.

Wednesday brings rain against the windows and the break moves inside to the small room with the kettle and the mismatched mugs. Coats steam on the radiator. The noticeboard flutters with rotas and thank-you cards and a photo from the summer outing. You sit shoulder to shoulder with the rest and you drink and you listen. A new starter asks how the job really runs and three voices answer at once with three different maps of the same place. You add your own small direction and she writes it down. Belonging sits in that room thick as steam.

Friday brings cake because someone passed an exam. Icing white and thick with a cherry on top. Candles borrowed from the drawer and lit with much laughter. Everyone sings a little off key. Slices cut uneven and handed round on paper plates. You eat yours to the last crumb with coffee beside it tasting richer for company. Pictures taken with arms round shoulders and crumbs on chins. The plate empties. The day ends early with the light gold on the yard wall.

## WHEN THE HARD HOUR KNOCKS

The hard hour gets met whole, with water poured and the one next thing done.

## SLEEP AND THE NIGHT BETWEEN

Sleep comes quicker in these free days and runs deeper through the night.

In the old years the last minutes of the day sat hot in the chest at midnight with the mouth dry and the heart tapping a little faster. Now the body lies down clean. The room smells of bedding washed and dried in wind. Breathing slows on its own to the bottom and back. You fall into sleep the way a stone settles in clear water, without a last errand on the doorstep. Dreams run bright and fade by morning. Waking finds the same clean sheets and the same steady chest.

You keep the lamp low and you read a few pages with the duvet up to the chin. The book smells of paper and glue. The landing light makes a thin line under the door. The house ticks as it cools. When the eyes grow heavy you mark the page and you switch off and you turn into the pillow. The dark feels kind rather than busy. Morning will come with its kettle song soon enough. There is no dread in waiting for it.

Children ill in the night get a parent fully present. Bottle warmed. Forehead felt with the back of the hand. Blanket tucked twice. Chair pulled to the bedside with a book for the long watch. No stepping out to miss the call-back. No smell to wash before comforting anyone. The small hand holds the big finger and both stay till fever eases and breathing evens. That steady staying through a night watch holds more care than any hurried comfort between absences ever held.

Bills on the mat get opened at the table with a straight back. Sharp words on the phone get heard to their end and answered short and plain. Boss unfair gets faced with clear notes and a calm voice. Rain and tiredness together at six o'clock get met with soup heated and boots dried by the door and the evening taken one hour at a time. Each trouble thins because you face it with the whole of attention staying where you put it.

## AN EVENING WITH LIGHTS AROUND

The late patio with drinks and laughter stays warm and whole, and you stay at the table to its end.

## DRIVES AND WALKS AND SMALL WEATHER

The drive home from those evenings tells its own small story of ease.

Both hands stay on the wheel. The heater warms clean air. Music plays to its end. You park and you step out and you lock the door with the street quiet round you. Houses glow behind curtains. A dog barks once far off. Your breath clouds and vanishes, clean as the night itself. Clothes smell of nothing when you hang them. Hair smells of nothing on the pillow. The chest feels wide when you breathe before sleep.

Walks lengthen as breath lengthens. The hill behind the estate that once left you winded now rises under the feet with air to spare at the top. You stand at the stile and you look over fields to the masts on the far hill. Wind moves through grass in bright waves. Sheep call thin across the valley. You breathe to the bottom and you feel the wideness answer.

Friends notice without being told. You laugh without watching the clock. You taste pudding to its end. You drive home with the car smelling of nothing and the clothes smelling of nothing and the chest feeling wide. You answer pressure with lightness and then you ask about their holiday or their child and you mean the asking. Enjoyment at the table runs fuller than before because you never leave it.

## WHAT TO DO WITH A PASSING THOUGHT

The decision stays shut, and any passing thought gets met with joy while you get on with the minute you are in.

## GET ON WITH ENJOYING YOUR LIFE

I walked through my first free week slowly, half surprised at how little surprise there was. Kettle, queue, desk, plate, phone, friends, bed. Each room a little brighter for being uncut. Each hour a little longer for not being timed. No great battle. No grim holding. Only small bells of relief ringing through ordinary minutes until relief became the weather of the days.

Yours will run the same way. Tomorrow morning the feet will find the floor first again. The kettle will sing again. The shop bell will ring again. The work bell will ring again. Each ordinary hour passed without paper is not a test survived. It is a pleasure reclaimed. Walk through each with joy.

YIPPEE! I'M FREE!

Let that bell ring inside while cups clink and tills beep and talk runs. The crossing, when it comes at all among busy days, becomes the trigger for relief, again and again, until relief is the only association paper has left. Three seconds and the exchange is done. Thought, bell, smile, talk.

Change nothing else to stay free. Eat as you eat. Keep friends and hours and work as they are. The body returns to breathing and tasting on its own without being taught. It knew freedom for years before the first tube ever touched the lips. It knows it again within days.

And rejoice at a dead enemy rather than mourning a lost friend. That single turn of feeling holds these ordinary days together. Same factual loss, two opposite emotions. When you see smokers huddled in doorways while talk goes on inside without them, do not think they have something you have not. Think how wonderful it is to stay inside, warm in talk, breathing clean air to the bottom, getting on with enjoying your life while no hunger calls the hours.

SUMMARY
- I now live mornings that begin with feet on the floor and a kettle singing, and I know the day starts cleaner and wider without a dose in front of it.
- I now stand in shop queues and sit through work breaks with both hands free, and I see that food and drink and talk and air give their gifts whole when nothing cuts them.
- I now meet hard hours and kind evenings with clear eyes, and I feel relief that stepping out is no longer my name on any list.
- I now meet any passing thought with joy and get on with the minute I am in, and I know each ordinary day is simply mine again.
