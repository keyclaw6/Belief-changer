Chapter 13
MORNINGS, SHOPS AND ORDINARY DAYS

IN THIS CHAPTER: the kettle, the shop queue, the break in fresh air.

*You do not get through ordinary days without cigarettes — you enjoy them whole because you are free.*

The ashtray from last night sits washed and clean. The room smells of nothing. Morning light comes thin through the curtain, and for the first time in years there is no small calculation running under it about when to step outside.

You wake as a non-smoker. Not as someone becoming one. As one.

Let me walk beside you through these first ordinary days, because this is where freedom stops being a sentence spoken at a table and starts being a life lived in slippers and shoes and work coats. Nothing new needs proving. Everything old needs enjoying.

## THE MORNING AFTER THE TABLE

Feet find the floor before the hand finds anything. That small order, feet first, tells the whole story of this morning.

In the old years the hand moved first. Before the eyes were open, before the feet had touched anything cold, the hand was already reaching toward the bedside table, toward the pack and the lighter, toward the first draw on empty lungs. We all lived that reach. We all told ourselves it steadied the day. The throat burned, the head swam a little, the chest tightened, and we called that steadiness.

This morning the feet touch the floor and the hands are empty and nothing is missing.

You stand. You put the kettle on. You hear it begin its low talk and rise to its full chatter. You splash water on your face. You notice how much air the first deep breath brings when nothing hot and papery goes in front of it. The mouth tastes of sleep, cleanable with water and toothpaste, not coated with last night. The fingers smell of soap after you wash them, and the smell stays.

I remember my first free morning after years of that bedside reach. I stood in my own kitchen in a dressing gown and waited for the tug I had been promised by every gloomy tale. It came, faintly, a brief emptiness under the ribs at the old minute, the way the stomach growls at a mealtime that has moved. I smiled at it because I knew its name and its size. I poured the hot water and watched the steam rise straight, and by the time the tea was brewed the minute had passed and I was already inside the next one, free and drinking.

Yours will come like that. A thought at the kettle. A glance toward the doorstep where you used to stand in the cold. Let it come and let it pass. Do not scold it. Do not weigh it. Meet it with joy and get on with the hot drink in your hands.

YIPPEE! I'M FREE!

Say it inside your chest while the tea warms your hands. It is not a charm to convince yourself. It is the plain report of what happened last night while you watched with open eyes. The thought says a dose would start the day. The answer says the day has already started, cleaner, without it.

Ask yourself, with the kettle singing behind you, the only honest questions mornings ever asked. If those first draws truly steadied the day, why did the steadiest smokers you ever knew start every day unsteady, coughing and reaching before speaking? If that morning dose truly cleared the head, why did the head always feel dullest at waking and clearest an hour after breakfast when no dose had touched it for a while? A true start does not leave you narrower. Morning air widens. That is your verdict for every dawn from here.

Eat as you eat. Drink what you drink. I ate toast standing up that first morning, butter melting through, coffee beside it tasting sharper than it had in years because no dry paper taste sat across the tongue. Food tastes of itself now. Let it. The tongue was never broken. It was only coated. Clean air and hot drink and cool floor underfoot are enough ceremony for any morning.

Step into the shower and feel the hot water run without a clock running against it. In the old years the wash was timed round the next light, hurried at the end because hunger called from the kitchen. Now the water can run its full course. Dress slowly. Notice the shirt smells of cupboard and soap, and will smell so at noon. Notice the breath in the mirror stays clean after brushing, and stays clean on the walk to the bus. That clean staying is not small. We paid years of mints and sprays and turned heads to hide what one tube left behind. This morning nothing needs hiding.

You do not need to change the shape of the hour to keep it. Keep the chair. Keep the window. Keep the slow minutes before work. Freedom proves itself inside the same kitchen, not in a new one. Walk to work or drive to work by the same road and watch how different the same road feels when no part of the mind counts lamp-posts to the next stopping place. The bus smells of coats and rain. The car smells of itself. Your hands stay on the wheel or in the pockets, steady and warm, belonging to the morning rather than serving it.

## THE SHOP QUEUE

On the second or third day you will need milk or bread or a paper, and you will stand in a queue that once had its own small script.

We all knew that script. Buy the food and buy the pack. Watch the shutter lift and the rows of coloured boxes slide forward. Pay twice, once for living and once for the tax on living. Pat the pocket on the way out to feel the new box sit there, already counting the hours inside it. That pat was never joy. It was checking a chain.

Now you join the same queue with empty pockets and light hands.

The person ahead of you buys cigarettes. You hear the name of the brand, the knock of the pack on the counter, the lighter slipped in beside it. The shutter lifts and shuts. The smell of fresh cellophane and old smoke drifts for a second. Watch what happens inside your chest with clear eyes.

You will not feel envy. You will feel relief with a small ache of memory inside it, the way you feel watching someone hurry for a bus you no longer need to catch. I stood in that exact queue on my third free day behind a young man buying twenty with his sandwich, and I waited for the pang the old pictures had promised me. What came instead was pity, warm and unforced. He was buying his own interruptions for the afternoon. He was paying to cut his own day into pieces. He did not look wicked or foolish. He looked conned, the way we all looked, paying gladly at the same till for the same cutting.

If paper truly added something to shopping, why did every shopping trip with paper in the pocket end with the same hurry home to step outside? If that box behind the shutter truly calmed, why did its buyers always queue a little tighter, watch the clock a little harder, pat the pocket a little oftener than everyone else in the line? A true calm does not need patting. Your pockets stay still now. That stillness is the answer.

Buy your milk. Pay once. Walk out with both hands free for the bags. Feel how light the door feels when it does not lead to the pavement outside for a quick dose before driving. The street air meets you whole. Traffic and rain and cold meet you whole. Nothing needs finishing before life can continue, because nothing was paused.

Walk home the long way if the light is kind. Notice the shop smell stays behind you instead of following in your hair. Notice the coins in the pocket stay coins instead of turning into timing. In the old years every errand carried its second errand inside it, the search for a corner to light, the eye on shelter when rain fell, the small panic when the pack ran thin far from a till. That second errand is gone. One list, one walk, one return. A free errand takes half the time and leaves twice the pleasure, because attention never leaves the street it walks.

Do not preach in that queue. Do not tell the cashier or the young man what you have seen. I know the urge that rises in the first week, the bright urge to hand everyone the key. Swallow it kindly. Visible ease recruits more than any speech. Let them see a face that does not tighten at the shutter, hands that do not twitch toward it, a body that does not hurry. If they ask, tell them plainly that stopping was wonderful and that you enjoy life more. Then talk about the weather or the football and let the seeing do its own work. Arguing reopens in your own mind the very question you have closed. Living keeps it shut.

## THE BREAK THAT STAYS WHOLE

Work breaks worried you more than mornings. I know. Mornings were private. Breaks were public, timed by bells and kettles and other people's feet moving toward the door.

In the old years the break was never a break. It was a serving. The clock reached the hour and the body stood whether the mind was finished or not. Coat on. Door open. Cold step or hot yard. Quick light while talk continued inside without you. Three minutes of hot draw and watching ash so it did not fall on the shirt, then back inside with the mouth dry and the thread of thought snapped, needing ten minutes to find where you left it. We called those minutes rest. They were shifts.

Take the same hour now and keep the whole of it.

When the kettle boils at work, make your drink the way you always made it. Carry it to the window or the step or the bench where the air moves. Feel the cup warm the palms. Both palms, because neither holds paper. Breathe to the bottom of the lungs and feel how much air they want. That wideness is not a technique. It is what lungs do when left alone.

Talk stays whole now. That is the first marvel every free smoker reports in the first week, and you will report it too. A joke runs its full length without a pause at the door. A story does not lose its teller halfway. A difficult task does not lose its worker at the delicate minute. You stay. Staying looks small on paper and feels enormous in the chest. An uncut hour is worth ten cut ones.

After food the same wholeness waits. Meal-end was once the bell for lighting, the plate pushed back, the hand reaching while taste still sat on the tongue. Now the taste lingers. Let it linger. Hot food followed by clean mouth followed by clear breath is a complete sequence. Nothing needs capping. I ate my first free evening meal slowly, bread and soup and cheese, and sat back with the plate empty and the mouth clean, and noticed for the first time in twenty years how the meal ends itself. It settles. It warms outward. It does not call for anything. The call you remember was never the meal calling. It was hunger for the next dose arriving on schedule and borrowing the plate as its excuse.

Ask the honest break questions once and let them settle for good. If stepping out truly rested you, why did you always return a little duller, needing coffee to restart what the step had broken? If lighting truly sharpened talk, why did the best talk always happen before anyone left or after everyone returned, never in the yard with eyes watering? A true rest does not snap the thread. Air rests. Company rests. Warm drink rests. Paper only borrowed their names.

Do not time these breaks to prove anything. Do not count how long you can stay inside or how little you think of doses. Counting keeps the thing alive as something still being measured. Get on with enjoying your life inside the hour you have. One afternoon you will look up and realise the whole morning passed without a single thought of lighting, and you will smile at the noticing the way you smile hearing birds after rain — not because you fought for quiet, but because quiet returned on its own.

The afternoon coffee tells the same story in a smaller cup. In the old years it arrived with its twin already waiting, cup in one hand and tube in the other, taste fighting taste until neither won. Now the coffee stands alone and tastes fully of itself, dark and warm and lingering. Drink it slowly by the window and watch the street below. Hands that once tapped and flicked now simply hold. That simple holding steadies more than any twin ever did, because nothing burns the minute short.

## WHEN THE HARD HOUR KNOCKS

A hard hour will come in these first days. It always does. Not because freedom summons it, but because life contains it. Bill on the mat. Sharp words on the phone. Child ill. Boss unfair. Rain and tiredness together at six o'clock with the house noisy.

In the old years the hand reached before thought finished. Phone down, hand out. Bad news, hand out. Waiting room, hand out. We credited paper with getting us through. Look plainly now at what getting through ever meant. The papers on the desk never moved while the tube burned. The quarrel never softened while smoke rose between sentences. The sick child never cooled while a parent stood outside watching ember instead of forehead. Trouble sat untouched beside every light, then stayed after the light was stubbed, with hunger added on top. That is not help. That is company that charges.

Meet this week's hard hour directly with the nerves you already own. They are steadier without hourly hunger cutting them.

Pour water. Sit down. Deal with the one next thing the trouble asks. Ring the number. Write the short reply. Hold the fevered hand. Pay the part you can pay. Breathe through the minute you are in. You will find, as I found on my fourth free evening with a broken boiler and a short temper, that the mind stays with the matter in hand longer now. No part of attention keeps sliding toward pocket and clock. No sentence needs pausing for service. The whole of you faces the whole of the trouble, and the whole of you is more than enough.

The old voice may speak in that hour. It will use your own tones because it lived in your mouth so long. It will say paper steadies nerves, paper takes care of it, paper gets you through this one evening and you can be free again tomorrow.

Hear it kindly and answer it honestly. If paper fixes pressure, why did the most pressured years of your life smoke most and settle least? If one evening dose truly steadies, why did every steadied evening need another an hour later and another after that, until midnight left you wearier than the trouble alone ever left you? You cannot be steadied by what keeps you unsteady. That single seeing shuts the door without slamming it.

Pity the smoker you see in that hard hour standing in rain outside a doorway to feed hunger between troubles. Do not envy his pauses. His pauses are service breaks. Yours are your own. He is not calmer than you. He is interrupted. You are not missing his relief. He is missing your continuity, the clean run of attention that lets trouble shrink to its true size instead of growing in the gaps between doses.

And when the hour passes, as hard hours do, notice what remains. You met it whole. No smell on the fingers to wash before comforting anyone. No stepping out to miss the call-back. No extra hunger laid over the original worry. The trouble thinned because you faced it. The chest feels tired and light the way muscles feel after honest work. That tired lightness is the true shape of coping. It needs no paper around it.

Sleep that night comes quicker too. In the old years the last dose sat hot in the chest at midnight, mouth dry, heart tapping a little faster, mind replaying the day through a thin haze. Now the body lies down clean. The room smells of bedding. Breathing slows on its own to the bottom and back. You fall into sleep the way a stone settles in clear water, without a last errand on the doorstep. Morning will find the same clean sheets and the same steady chest, proof stacked upon proof while you rested.

## AN EVENING WITH LIGHTS AROUND

The late patio with drinks and laughter will arrive soon enough, and you need not fear it or flee it.

Go as you always went. Wear what you like. Drink what you like. Keep friends and hours as they are. Change nothing else in life to stay free. Freedom that needs new rooms is not freedom. Yours proves itself in the old rooms.

You sit at the table while glasses sweat and talk runs. Someone lights. Then another. A hand extends a pack toward you with a smile and an easy voice, and the old words hang in the warm air for a second. Laughter does not stop. Music does not stop. The night does not lean in. It is only a hand and paper in it.

See it clearly with the eyes you earned at the quiet table. That tube adds nothing to sun and leisure and company. It borrows their glow for seconds and sends the bill in smell and timing. The drink in your hand tastes cleanest with no coating across it. The talk runs whole with no cuts at the door. The night air sharpens senses paper always dulled. If the best night you ever smoked in could not give the dose a true gift, what will this thinner night give?

Answer with joy, not with a speech. No, thanks — I am free now. Then turn back to the story that was telling. That is all the preaching any evening needs.

Watch the others step out to feed hunger in the cold while you stay warm in talk. Feel not a pang of missing but a wave of relief that stepping out is no longer your name on any list. Their lighting is not enjoyment you lack. It is service you left. One will cough and laugh it off. One will study the ember to keep ash from the dress. One will check the pack and calculate whether it lasts the night. You sit without calculation, without watching, without serving. Your evening is uncut from first glass to last laugh.

YIPPEE! I'M FREE!

Let that bell ring inside while glasses clink. The intruding thought, if it comes at all among friends, becomes the trigger for relief, again and again, until relief is the only association paper has left. Three seconds and the exchange is done. Thought, bell, smile, talk. No argument needed because no doubt stands behind the thought. It is only memory walking an old path that grass already covers.

I sat among smokers on my fifth free night, a birthday with candles and smoke both rising, and waited to feel the old pull toward belonging. What I felt instead was belonging itself, fuller than before, because I never left the table. I laughed without watching the clock. I tasted pudding to its end. I drove home with the car smelling of nothing and the clothes smelling of nothing and the chest feeling wide. Belonging never needed paper. Paper only ever borrowed belonging and cut it in two.

The drive home tells its own small proof. In the old years the car was an ashtray on wheels, windows cracked in winter, one hand always busy, eyes always half on the road and half on ash. Now both hands stay on the wheel. The heater warms clean air. Music plays to its end without a pause at the lights for lighting. You park and step out and lock the door with nothing to hide under the seat and nothing to carry for the morning. The street is quiet. Your breath clouds and vanishes, clean as the night itself.

If anyone presses you, meet pressure with lightness, not with battle. I smoked for years, I loved it as you love it now, and I am marvellous without it. Then ask about their holiday or their child and mean the asking. You are not there to convert the table. You are there to enjoy it. Enjoyment converts more than pleading ever did.

## WHAT TO DO WITH A PASSING THOUGHT

You will think of cigarettes in the days ahead. At the old hours. After food. With coffee. At the pause in work. When the car turns toward home. When joy spikes or worry dips.

Do not mistake that crossing for danger or for failure. It is memory walking an old path. Do not scold the thought. Do not weigh it. Do not measure each wave or clock it or ask whether this wave proves something is wrong. When the emptiness stirs, know what it is — an unfed echo losing strength — and get on with the minute you were in. Drink water. Breathe to the bottom of the lungs. Keep hands busy with the ordinary thing they hold. The moment passes quicker when left alone than when watched.

Never mistake that stir for proof the dose gave something real. If it proves anything, it proves the dose caused the very unease it then paused. A person who never smoked never carries that hourly emptiness. You will not carry it either now nothing feeds it. Each wave unfed is a little weaker than the last, until one evening you notice the hour passed without a thought and you smile at the noticing.

Keep the decision shut the way you shut a door on leaving a room you no longer rent. Do not reopen it to check whether it is still shut. The question whether to smoke is finished. Finished questions need no guarding, only leaving alone. If confusion ever puts paper to lips in some unguarded minute, do not fall to your knees and declare yourself chained. Stub it at once. Take nothing after. See the stir for the woken echo it is. Let the stumble light the path straighter. But never take one to test yourself, never take one because you once got away with one, never take one to prove anything about this book. The proof already stands in days lived free, in mornings that begin without reaching, in meals that end without lighting, in breaks that stay whole.

Change nothing else to stay free. Eat as you eat. Keep friends and hours and work as they are. The body returns to breathing and tasting on its own without being taught. Trust it. It knew freedom for years before the first tube ever touched your lips. It knows it again within days.

And rejoice at a dead enemy rather than mourning a lost friend. That single turn of feeling is the whole secret of these ordinary days. Same factual loss, two opposite emotions. Mourning keeps paper alive as something precious missed. Rejoicing tells the truth about what died: a tax, a cutting, a serving. When you see smokers huddled in doorways while talk goes on inside without them, do not think they have something you have not. Think how wonderful it is to stay inside, warm in talk, breathing clean air to the bottom, getting on with enjoying your life while hunger no longer calls the hours.

I walked through my first free week slowly, half surprised at how little surprise there was. Kettle, queue, desk, plate, phone, friends, bed. Each room a little brighter for being uncut. Each hour a little longer for not being timed by ember. No great battle. No grim holding. Only small bells of relief ringing through ordinary minutes until relief became the weather of the days.

Yours will run the same way. Tomorrow morning the feet will find the floor first again. The kettle will sing again. The shop bell will ring again. The work bell will ring again. Each ordinary hour passed without paper is not a test survived. It is a pleasure reclaimed. Walk through each with joy. The door behind shut on its own. It needs no holding.

SUMMARY
- I now live mornings that begin with feet on the floor and a kettle singing, and I know the day starts cleaner and wider without a dose in front of it.
- I now stand in shop queues and sit through work breaks with both hands free, and I see that food and drink and talk and air give their gifts whole when nothing cuts them.
- I now meet hard hours and kind offers with clear eyes, and I feel relief that stepping out is no longer my name on any list.
- I now meet any passing thought with joy and get on with the minute I am in, and I know each unfed wave thins until ordinary days are simply mine again.
