Chapter 2
ARE YOU REALLY CHOOSING THIS

IN THIS CHAPTER: the garage doorway after midnight, the bathroom fan and the coat by the back door, the hand moving before the thought.

*You did not choose to need cigarettes — you were conned into a trap that took the choice away.*

It is half past eleven and the house is quiet. You tell your partner you are taking the rubbish out. You pick up a small bag that does not need taking out. You step into the garage and pull the door almost closed behind you, leaving a gap for the air, and you light up with your back to the house. You believe you came out here for a quiet moment to yourself. You count it as your choice.

Watch what you do next, because I know it by heart. You smoke quickly, with one ear on the door. You grind the end under your shoe and you check the floor for ash. You stand in the cold for an extra minute, waving your hand in front of your jacket. You go back inside and you wash your hands with extra soap. You open the bathroom cabinet and you spray. You Febreeze the hell out of your coat and you chew a mint, and you stand for a second in the hallway wondering if the smell still clings to your hair. You know this dance because you dance it most nights. We all learned the same steps.

I ask you to look at that dance with clear eyes, the way an investigator looks, not the way a judge looks. I speak to you with warmth, because I stood in that same garage and told the same small lie about rubbish. I speak about the pattern without mercy, because the pattern made you lie.

## The Garage Door

If smoking were choice in the plain sense, why would choice need a hiding place?

Think of the other choices you make in a day. You choose coffee or tea and you drink it at the table. You choose a coat and you wear it out of the front door. You do not apologise to the hallway. You do not spray the curtains after a sandwich. Choice walks in daylight. Choice does not check over its shoulder.

Yet we hide to smoke and we believe we hide from other people. We tell ourselves the hiding is politeness. We tell ourselves the neighbours complain and the kids should not see and the partner hates the smell. There is truth in each excuse, and each excuse misses the point underneath. You did not hide your first sweets as a child with this kind of care. You did not stand in a cold garage to eat a biscuit. The hiding began when you knew, without putting it into words, that what you were doing no longer felt like choosing.

Ask yourself what a person who truly chooses needs to hide. A person who chooses a walk does not need to wash the walk off his hands. A person who chooses a book does not need to chew a mint after reading. We hide the smoking because a part of us already understands that the hand is no longer following orders. We call ourselves closet smokers and we laugh when we say it, and the laugh is thin because we know what closets are for. Closets are for things we cannot show.

I hid in all the usual places. The garage with the paint tins. The bathroom with the fan running. The end of the garden in rain that soaked my socks. The car with the window cracked an inch, wiping the inside of the windscreen where the film builds. I believed I was being considerate. I believed considerate people step outside. Then one night my son asked why I always needed fresh air at the same hour, and I heard my own answer come out smooth and false, and I understood that I was not protecting him from smoke. I was protecting my story about choice from his plain question. That night sits in me still, because it taught me what hiding proves.

Consider the arithmetic of the hiding. You pay good money for the pack. You carry it with you everywhere, patted into the pocket like a phone. You tell yourself you prize it. Then you use it like something shameful, in corners, in cold, in haste, with one eye on the door. Does that look like prizing to you? If a man told you he loved long baths and then told you he takes them crouched behind the shed in January, you would know at once that something else holds him there. You would not argue about love. You would look at the shed.

Look at your shed. Look at the garage door and the fan and the coat by the back door that smells no matter how much you spray. Those objects know more about your smoking than your explanations know. They say what your mouth will not say yet. They say the choice moved.

You say, "Nobody makes me light up. I light up because I want to." I hear that voice with respect, because it was my voice for years. Hold it up against the garage and see which one tells the truth. Wanting does not smuggle itself. Wanting does not lie about rubbish. When you watch your own hiding without flinching, you see the first crack in the story. Choice does not need Febreeze.

## The Hand That Moves First

Tomorrow, watch your hand. That is all I ask in this part. Do not change what you do. Watch.

You know the moment I mean. The phone goes down after a hard call. The kettle clicks off. The car stops at lights. The meal ends. The argument ends. Before you have finished the thought that says you might smoke, your hand has already moved toward the pocket. The fingers know the place. The thumb knows the lid. The body has started the walk to the door while the mind is still deciding whether to go. You believe you decided and then moved. Watch closely and you will see it happen the other way around.

I watched mine on a Tuesday at my desk. The letter on the screen was difficult. My jaw tightened. My shoulders rose. My right hand left the keyboard and opened the drawer where I kept the pack, and I sat there with the drawer open, staring at my own fingers as if they belonged to someone else. I had not said yes. I had not weighed anything. The hand had gone shopping without me. I closed the drawer slowly and I felt a tug of irritation at being interrupted, and that irritation frightened me more than the letter had, because it showed me who was giving orders.

If you doubt what you see, put it to the test with three plain questions and answer them in your own head with the honesty you use when no one listens.

If you choose each cigarette, why does the hand move before the choice? If you smoke because you enjoy it, why does the reaching feel like answering a bell? If you could take it or leave it, why does leaving it for an hour make your attention circle back to the pocket like a dog to a gate?

You already know the answers. You move first because the pattern lives in the body now. You answer the bell because the bell was installed. You circle back because circling back is what the pattern trained you to do. None of that makes you weak. It makes you trained. We were all trained by the same teacher, dose by dose, until the arm learned the road.

People tell me, "I could stop if I wanted to. I just do not want to." I answer that the way I answer a friend, straight and without scorn. If you do not want to stop, why are you reading a book that shows you how to stop? If the hand truly obeys, why did it open the drawer before you spoke? A person who can take a thing or leave it does not carry it into every room and count how many remain before the shops shut. A person who chooses does not need to tell himself, each morning, that today he will keep it under control.

The fact is, the question of who gives orders has already been answered by your own pockets. The pack goes where you go. The lighter sits by the bed. The coat smells. The car ashtray fills. The day is planned around small exits. That is not how people treat a casual preference. That is how people act when something has taken charge of the timetable and they have agreed to call the timetable their own.

It is not your fault that the orders changed hands. We all began the same way, copying older boys and girls, coughing, laughing, wanting to look grown. Nobody explained the terms. Nobody said the first doses teach the body to ask and the later doses teach the mind to call the asking by your name. We signed nothing. We copied. The copying set a hook, and the hook learned to speak in our voice. When you hear yourself say, "I choose this," listen for the hook underneath, doing the talking.

You are not choosing. You are obeying.

## Heaven For A Moment

"I actually enjoy it," you say. "You do not understand. The moment I light up I'm in heaven."

I understand that sentence better than you think. I said it myself for years, and I meant it each time I said it. I want to treat what you feel with care here, because mocking what you feel would close your mind, and I need your mind open.

Tell me exactly when the heaven arrives. It arrives with the scratch of the lighter and the first draw, with the shoulders dropping and the breath going out long. For a few seconds the noise in the head quiets. The hands have something to do. The world narrows to the glowing end. Then the stick burns down, the taste turns, the throat dries, and you stub it out and you feel shitty afterwards. You wave the smoke from your clothes. You notice the sour mouth. You think about the money and the stairs and the garage last night. Whilst you love smoking you hate being a smoker. Those two truths sit side by side in the same chest, and you believe they contradict each other. They do not. They describe the trap from the inside.

Hold the timing honestly. The lift you prize lasts for the lighting, not for the day. Nobody walks around for hours in heaven after one dose. Ask any smoker you know, including the ones who brag, what they feel twenty minutes after stubbing out, and watch their face change. They feel the same flat need rising again. They feel the attention sliding back to the pocket. If the heaven were real and lasting, one dose would be enough. You would light up in the morning and sail through to bed. You never do. You light and fade and light again, all day, every day, chasing a brightness that dies while the stick is still warm in your fingers.

Does that look like pleasure to you? Real pleasures do not behave like that. A good meal holds you for hours. A good walk leaves your legs pleasantly tired and your head clear into the evening. A good laugh with friends warms the drive home. Those things give and then leave something behind. The dose gives for seconds and then takes, leaving the mouth stale and the chest tight and the mind already counting toward the next one. We call that enjoyment because we have no other word ready, but the word misleads us.

I do not say you lie about the seconds. The seconds feel good. The shoulders do drop. The noise does quiet. I say you misread what those seconds prove. You believe the seconds prove the dose adds something fine to your life. The seconds prove only that the lighting answers a need that was nagging a minute before. The quiet you feel is the quiet of the nagging pausing, not the quiet of a gift arriving. A man who squeezes his own wrist all day feels heaven when he lets go at night, but he would be a fool to say the squeezing gives him pleasure. He would be telling the truth only if he said the letting-go pauses the pain his own grip caused.

So keep what is true and set down what is false. Keep the honesty that says the lighting feels good for a moment. Set down the story that says the moment proves smoking enriches your day. The heaven you remember is real and brief, and it lives only at the lighting. Everything after it — the aftertaste, the shame, the counting, the hiding — belongs to the same dose. When you weigh the whole dose, not only the first three seconds, the scales move.

You love the lighting. You hate the smoking. Both can be true, because they are true of all of us who lived inside it.

## Taken Care Of

There is another sentence smokers say that I want to hold gently before we move on. "If anything stressful happens in my life, a cigarette takes care of it."

I know that taken-care-of feeling. The phone slams down. The inbox fills. The child screams. The bills spread across the table. The hand is already reaching, and with the first draws a warmth spreads and the shoulders unlock and for a minute the storm seems smaller. You believe the dose handled the trouble. You feel handled yourself, as if someone capable had stepped into the room.

I do not deny that feeling. Denying it would make me a liar and would make you shut this book. The feeling arrives fast and it arrives often, and the same person can feel it fully while knowing the credit is false. I felt it and doubted it in the same minute for years. Feeling and knowing can sit side by side until understanding joins them up.

Look at what the feeling actually covers. The difficult letter still sits on the desk. The money still needs finding. The quarrel still needs mending. The dose did not pick up the phone or write the reply or soften the voice of the person you argued with. What changed for a minute was the clamour inside your own skin. The hand had something to hold. The lungs had something to do. The mind had somewhere to look while the trouble waited. We call that being taken care of because the quiet feels like care.

Ask yourself, with the same plain honesty you brought to the garage, whose care that was. Did the dose bring patience into the room, or did you bring patience and hand the praise to the dose? Did the night patio with friends feel warm because of the smoke, or because friends on a warm night are warm? You know how quickly we pin the good part of any moment onto the glowing end, the way a child pins the good part of a fairground onto the candy he happened to be holding. The fairground did the work. The candy only rode along.

Here I ask only that you notice the shape of the credit. Every hard hour gets pinned onto the dose. Every soft hour gets pinned there too. The dose collects praise from every corner of the day while doing none of the day's work. That collecting is not an accident. That collecting is how the pattern keeps its hold on people who are perfectly capable of handling hard hours, as you have handled a thousand of them with and without smoke in your hand.

You are capable. The pattern borrows your capability and charges you per borrow.

## What Holds It Together

By now two pictures have started to join. The hiding shows the choice is gone. The hand and the heaven show something keeps pulling.

The first keeper is small. When you go an hour or two without a dose, you begin to feel an empty, slightly restless, slightly edgy little tug somewhere under the ribs. It is not agony. It is barely a pain at all. It feels like hunger misplaced, like boredom with an edge, like attention that will not settle. Most smokers never put words to it. They only know the hand wants to move and the mind starts circling. That tug is the body asking for the next feed. It is tiny, and it dies quickly when unfed, the way a whine dies when no one answers it. I call it by its size on purpose. I call the physical asker the Nipper, because that is what it is — a small nipping creature, already starving between doses, with no strength except what your belief lends it.

The second keeper is large. It is the whole set of stories that tells you the tug means you need a dose to cope and to enjoy and to belong. It tells you the dose steadies nerves and marks pauses and oils friendships. It tells you the garage is politeness and the hand is habit and the heaven proves the value. It learned its lines from advertisements and film scenes and older smokers and from years of pinning every good moment onto the glowing end, until the lines sound like your own voice. I call that story-system the Smokescreen, because that is what it does — it lays smoke between you and the plain sight of what each dose costs.

Put the two together and you see the shape whole. The Nipper tugs. The Smokescreen translates the tug as proof you gain something. You light up. The tug pauses for a few minutes. The Smokescreen says, "There, it helped." The pause fades. The tug returns. Round and round, all day, every day, year after year. That round is the nicotine trap. It is not a flaw in your character. It is a con with two moving parts, one in the body and one in the mind, each feeding the other while you pay for the turning.

The fact is, we were all conned the same way. We began for show and stayed for the round. The trap removed the choice while leaving the language of choice in our mouths, so we defend our cage in the words of people still choosing. Once you see the two keepers at work, you cannot unsee them. Tomorrow, when the hand moves, you will feel the tug and hear the translation in the same second, and you will know both for what they are. That knowing is the beginning of the walk out. It asks nothing of your backbone. It asks only that you keep watching.

I do not ask you to fight the tug. I do not ask you to argue the stories to a standstill tonight. I ask only that you keep reading with the door open, the way you agreed at the start, and let each page show you one more turn of the round until the whole shape stands clear. The Nipper is small. The Smokescreen is only stories. The trap holds only while both stay unseen.

And now, at the point where asking matters, I lay down the second rule for our walk. Take it in the same warm and firm spirit as the first:

2. DON'T STOP OR CUT DOWN YET
Smoke as normal until your last cigarette.

That is the whole of it. Do not jump ahead. Do not test yourself by skipping doses. Understanding needs your normal days to work on, and I need you watching the garage and the hand and the heaven as they actually happen, not as you force them to happen. Your last cigarette will come, and you will know it when understanding is complete. Until then, smoke as normal and keep your eyes open.

SUMMARY
- I believed each cigarette was my choice, and I now see hiding and the hand moving first prove a trap took the choice while I kept the language of choosing.
- I believed the heaven at lighting proved smoking enriches my day, and I now understand the lift lives only at the lighting while the rest of each dose takes.
- I believed the tug and the stories about it were me, and I now know a small physical asker and a system of stories keep the round turning together.
