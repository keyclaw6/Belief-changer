Chapter 8
MORNINGS, MEALS AND BREAKS WITHOUT IT

IN THIS CHAPTER: the alarm before light, the kettle and the kitchen chair, the plate still warm, the step outside with air on the face.

*The morning, the meal and the break never needed smoke; lived without it, they are fuller, not emptier.*

The alarm rings in the dark and your hand moves before your feet do.

You know that reach. Eyes still shut. Mouth dry. Feet have not touched the floor and the hand has already found the pack on the chair, the lighter under the pack, the first stick between the lips before the legs have agreed to stand. You shuffle to the kitchen and light before the kettle is filled. The first drag goes down on empty lungs and the chest tightens and the throat complains and the head swims a little, and you call that waking up.

I lived that shuffle for years. I believed the day could not start until smoke started it. I believed the meal could not end until smoke ended it. I believed the break could not work unless smoke filled it. Three small rooms held my whole day hostage, morning and meal-end and break, and I feared that without smoke those rooms would stand empty.

They do not stand empty. Come and live them with me this morning as a non-smoker lives them, with breath and taste and free hands, and see.

## THE FIRST FIVE MINUTES

Stay in that bedroom a moment longer, because the whole con shows itself before the kettle boils.

The alarm rings. The room is cold. The floor is cold. The mouth tastes of last night, stale and dry. The chest feels heavy. The eyes burn. That heaviness and staleness are not the morning. They are last night's smoke still sitting in you. The lungs spent the night clearing ash. The mouth spent the night drying. The body spent the night doing repair work while you slept, and you wake in the middle of that work feeling grey.

And what does the hand do. It reaches for the very thing that made the waking grey, and lights it on empty lungs, and coughs, and feels the head swim, and watches the grey lift half an inch for two breaths, and says thank you.

Do you see the order. The smoke did not cure the morning. The smoke lent the morning its grey and then lifted a corner of it for a minute and asked for thanks.

Now live those same five minutes without the light, because your body already knows how a morning runs.

Feet touch the cold floor. You stand. You breathe. The first breath in the kitchen is thin and cool and entirely your own. You fill the kettle. You run cold water over the face. You feel the floor under the soles and the air at the window cracked open an inch. The chest moves without tightening. The mouth is still stale from last night, yes, but water washes it, and the second breath comes deeper, and by the time the kettle sings the head has cleared without one puff.

Stay with that kettle until it sings, because the whole morning lives in those three minutes when smoke used to stand in the way.

The flame ticks. The water murmurs and then rolls. You wrap the hands round the warm cup and the warmth goes straight to the fingers without smoke drying the throat beside it. Steam rises and you breathe it, clean and plain. The window shows the street still dark with one lamp lit. You stand there with nothing burning, nothing timing you, nothing to tap or stub, and the mind begins to order itself the way it always ordered itself before smoke arrived, one simple thing after another, wash, drink, dress, eat. No grey hangs over the order. No cough breaks it.

I remember how smoke broke it. Tea went down beside ash. The cup tasted of tray. The shirt carried last night into the day. The coat carried it to the bus. The first hour at work began with throat clearing and nose blowing and eyes watering, and I blamed the cold morning for what the night's doses and the morning's first dose had laid there together. Take the light out and the lie thins fast. Clothes smell of nothing. Hair smells of nothing. The cough that greeted the stairs thins. Breath comes without rattle. The body does not need to be coaxed awake with a drug. It wakes because sleep ends, and it loves waking when waking is not taxed.

You say that picture sounds thin. You say the morning dose does something no cold water does. You say without it the hands shake over the kettle and the mind will not order itself and the day starts wrong.

I hear you, because I said the same at that kitchen chair with ash already in the tray before tea. But answer plainly, as you answered at the meter and the bite.

If that first dose truly woke you, why did you wake grey every single morning for years despite never missing it. If it truly cleared the head, why did the head need clearing again in exactly the same grey way the next morning. If it gave the morning its kindness, why does the man who never smoked meet the same cold floor with clear eyes and steady hands and no cough, and call that ordinary.

You know the answer because your own throat gave it each morning while you coughed through the lighting. The dose never gave the waking. Sleep gave the waking. Water gave the washing. Air gave the clearing. Breath gave the steadiness. The dose only quieted for two breaths the stale want the last night's doses left, then left ash on the tongue that water had just cleaned.

Watch the hands in that kitchen, because they tell the same truth as the throat.

Lit mornings trained the hands to one office, find, flick, hold, tap. Free mornings give the hands back. They lift the cup without balancing a burning end beside it. They butter bread without ash drifting over the board. They button the shirt without the smell clinging to the cuffs. Small offices, and they fill the five minutes so completely that the old reach begins to look strange, a third hand that interrupted two busy ones. You do not stand empty-handed wondering what to do with the fingers. You stand full-handed with the day itself, cup and bread and tap and rail, and the fingers have never been busier or happier.

Think of the stairs where you first heard your breath labour, and how quickly breath returns when smoke stops charging it rent. The same return runs in the morning. Two mornings breathed clean and the mouth wakes cleaner. Three mornings and the cough thins. A week and the feet find the floor before the hand looks for anything, because there is nothing to look for. The kettle still sings. The window still lets in cold air. The day still starts. It starts fuller because no grey was laid over it the night before.

That is the first room lived through. The morning does not need lighting. It needs waking, washing, breathing, and it does those by itself, marvellously well, when smoke stops taxing them.

## THE MEAL THAT ENDS TWICE

Leave the kitchen and come to the table at noon, because the second fear sits there with a warm plate under it.

The food is hot. You eat. The taste is there while you chew, rich and full, and for those minutes no smoke is missed because the mouth is busy with real flavour. Then the plate empties. The last mouthful lingers. The tongue still holds butter and salt. The belly feels settled. Talk continues round the table.

And then the hand moves. Before the plates are cleared, before the taste has faded, the pack is out. You light. The first pull rolls over a tongue that held dinner and scorches it away. Within two pulls the lingering taste is gone, replaced by dry smoke and ash. You drink water to wash away what the light put there. Ten minutes later the mouth tastes of nothing but old smoke, and you call that the finish to the meal.

Twice I watched myself do this and praise it. Twice a day, noon and night, I took a meal that was complete and laid a stale end over it and called the stale end the crown.

You say the meal feels unfinished without it. You say the light seals the eating and lets the body rest. You say every good cook knows the pause after the plate needs its smoke.

Check that sealing against the tongue itself, because the tongue keeps honest accounts.

If the light completed the meal, why did it erase the very taste the meal just gave. If it rested the body, why did the chest feel heavier after the lighting than before it. If the pause needed smoke, why does the man who never smoked sit longer at the table, tasting longer, talking easier, rising lighter, with the flavour still in the mouth when he stands.

His meal ended once, when eating ended. Ours ended twice, once when eating ended and again when smoke killed the ending. We mistook the killing for the crown.

Live that same table without the light and watch what stays.

The last mouthful lingers. You let it linger. Butter stays on the tongue. Bread stays in the nose. You sip water and the water carries the taste round again instead of washing ash away. Talk does not thin into lighting and tapping and checking. It holds. The belly settles on its own while breath stays easy. Five minutes pass and the flavour fades by itself the way music fades when the player lifts, cleanly, without a door slamming over it. You rise with a clean mouth and the meal still with you, instead of rising with a stale mouth and the meal buried.

Stay at that table through supper too, because supper was the most praised lighting of the day and the emptiest.

The evening plate is cleared. The kitchen smells of cooking still, warm and kind. Chairs lean back. Someone laughs with mouth full of nothing but evening. Yesterday you left that warmth to stand in cold air with smoke, and returned with the smell of cooking driven from the nose and ash sitting where supper sat, and called the exile the crown. Today you stay. You carry plates with free hands. You breathe the cooking smell until it fades by itself. You take fruit or tea and the tea tastes of tea, whole and plain, not of tray. Talk runs on without the thin pause where lighters clicked. The evening settles because the day settles, not because a dose signed it off. When you rise the mouth remembers supper, not smoke, and memory keeps the meal instead of burying it.

It never fixed the itch. It caused it.

Read that sentence at the table, because it holds here as it held at the meter. The restless stir after the plate was not hunger for a finish the food failed to give. It was the old tug calling at its hour, dressed as appetite. The food had finished its work beautifully. The tug arrived on schedule and borrowed the voice of the meal to get fed. Feed the meal its praise and leave the tug unfed, and the tug thins while the meal shines larger in memory than any lit ending ever did.

And see how the pellet loses its office here. You once paid yourself that little pellet after the paragraph done and the drive done and the plate done, as if the work and the food could not count until smoke signed them. The work counted. The drive counted. The plate counted. The signature added nothing but ash to what was already signed. Tonight the finish rings without signing, and the ring is clearer because no smoke talked over it.

You fear the taste will feel duller without smoke to set it off. The reverse is true, and your own tongue will prove it within days. Smoke never sharpened taste. It dulled it all day and then lifted the dulling for two breaths while fresh flavour died under it. Leave the light out and taste returns like colour after dust is wiped, first the morning tea stronger, then bread fuller, then supper lingering longer than you remember since youth. No teaching does that. The tongue does it by itself when smoke stops coating it.

Watch that return across a week and you will laugh at the old fear. Monday tea tastes brighter than Friday tea did under smoke. Wednesday bread has a crust you had forgotten. Friday supper needs no extra salt because the tongue meets it clean. Each meal proves the last one was not a lucky hour but the body steadying back to its own measure. The non-smoker never marvelled at this because he never lost it. You will marvel for a week, and then you will stop marvelling and simply eat, which is the proof the baseline is yours again.

The meal was always complete. Smoke only taught you to doubt the completion so it could sell you the seal.

## THE BREAK THAT BREATHES

Step outside with me mid-morning, because the third room frightens you most and will free you most.

Work has run two hours. Shoulders ache. Eyes sting. The mind feels full. Someone says break, and chairs push back, and feet find the step outside where air moves. Yesterday that sentence meant lighting. Hands cupped flames in wind. Shoulders hunched round the glow. Talk paused for draws. The pause lasted as long as the stick lasted, and ended when the stick ended, whether the shoulders had eased or not.

You say a break without smoke is no break. You say the hands need something to do and the mind needs something to watch and the pause needs a wall round it or work rushes back in. You say standing empty-handed while others light will feel thin and left out.

Stay on that step and live the break whole, because the step itself answers.

The door shuts behind you. Air touches the face, cool and clean. You breathe, right down, without tightening. The shoulders drop because you set the work down, not because smoke told them to. You roll the neck. You stretch the fingers wide and empty. You watch the street, roofs, dogs, shop glass, the light moving on brick. Talk runs without pausing for ash. Laughter does not break for coughing. Five minutes pass that belong to air and company and limbs moving, and you return inside with eyes clearer and breath easy, instead of returning with throat dry and chest heavier and attention already counting to the next lighting.

Ask the plain questions that strip this room as they stripped the others.

If smoke rested you, why did the shoulders sit higher at the end of the lit break than at its start. If it gathered the mind, why did attention narrow to when the next break could be lit before the work resumed. If empty hands spoil the pause, why do free hands feel marvellous once you stop watching them for a lighter, picking up a cup, resting on the rail, swinging at the side, touching nothing and missing nothing.

Add the afternoon bell to the proof, because afternoon was where lit breaks failed fastest.

Three o'clock comes with eyes heavy and temper short. Yesterday that bell meant two sticks close together and still no rest, the first to answer the heaviness and the second to answer the failure of the first, with throat drier and chest heavier after both. Today the same bell means the same step, the same air, the same stretching of legs gone stiff from sitting. You walk to the corner and back without timing the walk to a burning end. You breathe through the nose until the head clears. You watch faces and weather instead of watching ash length. You return with the heaviness lifted because movement and air lifted it, not because a tug was quieted for minutes. The work that waited looks smaller after true rest. It looked larger after lit rest, because lit rest left the body poorer than it found it.

The break never lived in the stick. It lived in stopping, stepping out, breathing, seeing, talking, letting the mind lie fallow a while. Smoke only stood in the middle of that field and charged entry, then claimed it grew the crop.

I remember my own breaks as a chain of small hungers dressed as rest. The first pulls warmed the throat while the street opened. Then the throat dried before the corner. The legs kept their ache while attention left the sky and fixed on how long the stick had left. The non-smoker beside me took the same step, breathed the same air, watched the same street, and returned rested without signing anything. The street did not favour him. The signing robbed me. We credited the dose with the kindness of open sky and moving limbs, while the dose only lent back for minutes the calm attention the last dose had taken.

What remains on that step when the talk stops is only an empty, slightly restless, slightly edgy little tug, and it passes while you watch the roofs.

Feel it there without fear, because you have met it before and know its size. It sits low, light as hunger before supper. It asks, emptily, for its old hour. Then a breath at the rail thins it. Then talk covers it. Then work reclaims attention and it is gone until the next bell, thinner. In most bodies the bells ring a short while and then loosen day by day while taste and breath and calm attention return to their own hours. I fix no calendar over your skin. I state only what countless mornings confirm once the talk thins. Small. Brief beside a life. Already dying once unfed.

You do not need to guard that step by avoiding it. Meet it. Take the pause without the puff. Let others light if they light. Watch them with clear eyes and keep your own counsel, as you practised among other hands and other lights. Their glow does not measure your rest. Your rest is yours, taken in clean air with free hands, and it is fuller because no meter runs underneath it.

Hear the talk on that step with new ears and the belonging fear dies with the rest.

Yesterday the circle's talk sounded like proof that smoke gathered people. Today hear what gathers them. The joke that lands. The story about the morning's work. The shared complaint and shared laugh. Smoke never wrote one of those lines. It only paused them while lighters clicked and throats cleared. Take the puff out and the talk runs unbroken, warmer for not stopping, kinder for not coughing through the punchline. You belong because you stand there, breathe there, laugh there, not because you burn there. The pause is the company. The air is the refreshment. The free hands hold the cup steady while friendship does the rest.

See how the three rooms join into one day lived through.

Morning wakes without lighting. The meal ends without sealing. The break rests without burning. Feet, tongue, lungs, hands, each does its office without being taught, without being paid, without being signed. The day that once needed twenty lightings to stand where the non-smoker stood at waking now stands there by itself, breathing easy, tasting whole, moving free. No pleasure was lost from those rooms. The ash that covered their pleasure was lifted.

So here, where the morning stands proved and the plate stands cleared and the step stands breathed, take our eighth rule in the same warm, firm spirit as the seven before it. You have lived the ordinary hours without smoke and found them complete. You have felt the tug shrink to its true size while the rooms shone larger. There is nothing left to learn about those rooms except to let the body keep them. It kept them before smoke arrived. It keeps them now smoke leaves.

Think how proud you will feel tomorrow when the feet touch the floor and the hands reach for the kettle first, not the pack. Think how marvellous the noon plate will taste when the last mouthful is allowed to linger. Think how wonderful the step outside will feel when air does the whole office and breath comes easy from the first pull of it. This is not a thin life with three pleasures removed. It is the same life with the ash wiped off, colours brighter, flavours longer, pauses deeper, and a quiet excitement underneath because the hours are yours again.

Change nothing else in these rooms. Eat the same food. Keep the same chair. Take the same step at the same hour with the same people. The proof needs no new stage. The old stage shows it best, morning after morning, plate after plate, break after break, each complete without signing. The body asks for nothing to be added and nothing to be learned. Breath, taste, movement, talk, rest, all arrive on their own the moment smoke stops standing between you and them.

8. TRUST YOUR BODY TO BREATHE AND TASTE
Your body knows freedom without being taught.

Carry that trust as warmth into tonight and tomorrow. When the morning bell rings, breathe before you think. When the plate empties, let the taste linger. When work pauses, step out and take the air full. Each ordinary hour met without smoke wins back the hour whole. Each hour won whole leaves less static and more of yourself, rejoicing to be free in clean air, getting on with enjoying your life.

SUMMARY
- Mornings wake by themselves through sleep, water and air, and smoke only lent the grey it then lifted for a minute.
- Meals finish when eating finishes, and the lingering taste stays fuller when no light scorches it away.
- Breaks rest through stopping, stepping out and breathing, and free hands and easy breath make the pause complete.
- My body breathes and tastes without being taught, and ordinary hours lived without smoke prove freedom is fuller, not emptier.
