### Voice Gap 1: Epidemiology replaces lived recognition

**Our passage:** "People who study this pattern with self-report scales find it is common — roughly one in seven adults and one in twelve children describe an addictive-like relationship with highly palatable food on those questionnaires, at rates broadly similar to what the same kinds of scales find for alcohol and tobacco. Those are questionnaire results, not a doctor's diagnosis. They do not label you. They simply tell you, warmly and plainly: you are not uniquely weak."

**Real passage:** "The person who is tempted by one biscuit and ends up devouring the whole packet; the person who finishes the leftover cake on the children's plates after the birthday party; the person who takes a box of chocolates to bed and scoffs the lot – they all end up feeling the same way: bloated, dissatisfied and ashamed."

**What Carr's voice does here:** Carr creates recognition without shame through specific, bodily scenes the reader has lived. The effect is immediate intimacy — *that is me* — and absolution follows as one person who has seen the pattern speaking to another, not as a cited finding.

Even with hedges, the reader is briefly addressed as someone being briefed on evidence, not someone being seen. The warm landing ("you are not uniquely weak") arrives after a clinical detour; the recognition beat lands later and thinner than Carr's vignette-first absolution.

### Voice Gap 2: Lab register where Carr uses human binge misery

**Our passage:** "In animal research, when access to sugar is intermittent — on, then off, then on again — the animals escalate and binge. Their intake climbs. When access is steady and unrestricted, that escalation does not appear and signs of dependency do not develop. The schedule itself is part of the trap's form."

**Real passage:** "The more precious the food becomes, the more deprived we feel because we can't have it. It is an ever-increasing cycle of misery that has to come to an end and, sure enough, sooner or later, our resistance gives out and we devour that precious taboo with a vengeance. We binge."

**What Carr's voice does here:** Carr names the restrict–binge cycle from inside the reader's experience — precious taboo, vengeance, misery — with anger at the trap's design. Trust comes from shared humiliation turned outward onto the method, not from an experimental abstract.

**Where our voice fails:** "In animal research" shifts register to a research report mid-confrontation. The yo-yo reconnection that follows helps, but at the exact moment the trap's intermittency should feel like personal capture, the voice sounds like a coach citing papers. Distance replaces the trap-directed force Carr delivers through binge imagery.

**Initial suspicion (speculative):** plan — one sentence. The control/moderation beat was planned to carry scientific support for intermittency rather than Carr-style experiential escalation.

### Voice Gap 3: Announced warmth instead of embodied warmth

**Our passage:** "Let me make this warm and plain."

**Real passage:** "But overeaters should not feel ashamed, because the "choices" they make when it comes to eating are not free choices at all."

**What Carr's voice does here:** Carr removes shame by flat verdict — no preamble, no tone label. The reader feels understood because the writer simply stands on their side and names the truth; warmth is in the absolution itself.

**Where our voice fails:** Announcing that the next lines will be "warm and plain" breaks the direct-address illusion. The reader notices the writer managing register rather than receiving it. That creates a faint coaching/AI distance at the chapter's first major shame-lift beat, even though the sentences that follow are strong.

**Initial suspicion (speculative):** style-guide — one sentence. A meta-register cue ("warm and plain") was treated as acceptable voice scaffolding instead of Carr's unlabeled side-taking.
