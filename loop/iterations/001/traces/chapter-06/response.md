### THE CONNOISSEUR IN THE MIRROR

We have watched two illusions dissolve.

You saw the rollercoaster. The lift was not fuel. It was relief from a low BAD SUGAR created. You saw the kindness at 7pm. The warmth was not sugar. It was the moment, the person, the pause — BAD SUGAR sneaking a ride on love.

Now the next witness steps forward. It speaks more confidently than the other two. It sounds like expertise.

"I love the taste."

You say it with certainty. You can name your favourites. The biscuit that snaps just so. The chocolate that melts at exactly the right speed. The ice cream you drive across town for. The white bread that is soft as a pillow.

If you are P-01 — the loss-of-control binger — this is the line you say after the box is empty to explain the box. If you are P-02 — the in-denial moderate — this is the line you say to explain why the box stays in the house at all. "I don't keep it in the house" — until you do. "once a week is fine" — until it is twice. "just one won't hurt" — you say it like a connoisseur, not a craver.

Do you hear your own dialect in there?

The fact is, I have heard it from every reader who reached this page. And I understand why. Of all the stories the Sweet Brainwashing tells, the taste story feels the most personal. Energy can be measured. Love can be questioned. But taste? Taste feels like yours. Who can argue with your tongue?

I can. Not by telling you what you taste. By asking you to look at what you do.

Do you love the taste of BAD SUGAR? Or do you love the taste of the company BAD SUGAR brings with it?

Is BAD SUGAR a genuine pleasure or treat at the level of taste itself? Or is it a lonely, almost tasteless carrier that has learned to invite more interesting guests to make itself seem interesting?

You have the answer already in your own kitchen. You have been confessing it for years without hearing yourself.

Shall we listen?

### WHAT YOUR OWN KITCHEN PROVES

Let us start with your own behavior as evidence. Not my claim. Your actions.

Answer quickly and honestly, to yourself, not to me.

Do you put sugar in coffee because you love coffee? Or because without sugar, coffee is bitter and you do not like bitter?

Do you put sugar on grapefruit because you love grapefruit? Or to mask a sourness you do not enjoy?

Do you sprinkle sugar on porridge you were told is healthy because porridge alone tastes wonderful? Or because plain porridge is bland and you need to make it palatable?

Do you eat plain white flour by the spoonful for its taste? Do you chew white rice, dry and alone, and think, marvellous? Do you eat white bread with nothing on it and call it delicious?

You do not.

We do not love the taste of BAD SUGAR. We love what we add to it, or what we add it to, to make it edible.

The fact is, BAD SUGAR on its own is clumsy. Refined sugar is just sweet. Not complex. Not deep. Just sweet. Past the first seconds, it is sickly. White flour, white rice, white pasta — stripped of fibre, stripped of flavour — are close to tasteless. Ask your tongue without the company and it tells the truth. It says, not much here.

So what do we do? We invite company over.

We invite butter to make the bread seem worth eating.

We invite chocolate to make the biscuit seem worth eating.

We invite cream, salt, fat, crunch, cold, melt, crumb, aroma — the whole party — to do the tasting for the lonely carb.

Does that sound like love for BAD SUGAR? Or love for the guests?

Trap question. Answer it honestly.

If you loved BAD SUGAR itself, why do you never eat it alone?

If you loved the taste of sugar itself, why do you need chocolate to love chocolate? Why do you need butter to love bread? Why do you need fat and salt to love crisps?

Why does every BAD SUGAR you claim to love come dressed in something else?

You know. You have always known. You were just taught to credit the wrong guest.

Isolate the variable. It is the same move that freed you from energy and from reward. Remove BAD SUGAR and keep the company. Then keep BAD SUGAR and remove the company. Which still tastes good?

Toast without butter but with sugar sprinkled on it. Do you want it?

Butter on toast without sugar. Do you want it?

You want the butter. The sugar was sneaking a ride.

Chocolate without sugar is bitter. Do children love bitter? No. Sugar was added to hide the bitter and then took credit for the commodity that made bitter palatable — the fat, the cocoa butter, the milk, the crunch.

You love the company. Not the gatecrasher.

### THE HOUSE-PARTY GATECRASHER

Let me give you the picture that makes this click so you cannot unsee it. I will use it once, fully, as your card assigns, because it does the whole argumentative job in one image.

Picture a house party.

The house is your mouth. The host is your taste. The party should be good. There is music, laughter, warmth.

A lonely guest arrives. Call him BAD SUGAR. He is dull. He has nothing to say. He stands by the wall. No one talks to him. He knows he will be asked to leave if he stays like that.

So what does he do? He invites company over. He calls Butter. He calls Chocolate. He calls Cream, Salt, Crunch, Melt, Vanilla, Caramel, Fat. They are charming. They are warm. They are interesting. The room lights up when they arrive.

Everyone says, what a great party! What a wonderful guest!

But who made the party wonderful? The lonely guest who stood by the wall? Or the company he invited to speak for him?

We credit the gatecrasher for the party the company made.

That is BAD SUGAR. The lonely carbs — white bread, white rice, plain pasta, naked sugar — are tasteless without the company. They invite fat and flavour to make themselves palatable. Without the company they would be shown out. With it, we think we love them.

We say, I love the taste of biscuits. Do you? Or do you love butter and chocolate that made a bland flour disc worth eating?

I love pasta. Do you? Or do you love oil, cheese, salt, tomato, herbs that rescued a bland sliver of white starch?

I love sugary drinks. Do you? Or do you love cold, fizz, aroma, ritual, the sweet masking a chemical tang you would otherwise spit out?

Look again at your favourites and name the company.

Your chocolate bar. Name it. Sugar plus fat plus cocoa plus milk plus vanilla. Remove the fat. Keep the sugar. Would you eat a spoon of sugar and a square of raw bitter cocoa and call it lovely? No.

Your ice cream. Sugar plus fat plus cream plus cold plus vanilla. Remove the fat and the cold. Keep the sweet syrup. Lovely? No.

Your pastries. Sugar plus butter plus butter plus butter plus flake plus salt. Keep the sugar. Remove the butter. Eat sweetened flour. Lovely? No. Tasteless and slightly sickly.

That is the house-party gatecrasher demonstration. Lonely carbs invite company to make themselves palatable. Without the company they are tasteless. The taste you love is the company.

Killer line for this. Hold it.

You never loved BAD SUGAR. You loved what BAD SUGAR brought with it to pretend it was lovable.

Read it again. It was never BAD SUGAR you loved. It was the company that made BAD SUGAR seem worth loving.

Does that not explain why you add sugar to things you do not like and add things you do like to sugar to make it likable? Both ways, the truth is the same. BAD SUGAR needs help.

And if it needs help to taste good, how can it be a genuine pleasure or treat on its own?

It cannot. The credit was misassigned.

It was sneaking a ride.

### THE NON-USER NOSE TEST

There is a second isolation test. Faster. Sharper. You can do it tonight.

The smell test.

Think of the smell that hooks you. Fresh bread. Warm biscuits. Hot pastries. Chocolate opening. Popcorn at the cinema.

Now ask: does that smell hook someone who never eats BAD SUGAR?

Does a person who does not eat sweets find the sweet aisle ambrosial? Or cloying?

Does a child who has not yet been trained on biscuits lean into the biscuit tin smell with longing? Or do they prefer the smell of real food — oranges, soup, bread with cheese?

The fact is, the aroma is not universally delicious. It is learned deliciousness. The brainwashing taught your nose what to call lovely.

Think of your own non-user moments. Have you ever been full, rested, happy, outdoors, after a good meal made of real food — and walked past a bakery and thought, no thank you, that smell is too sweet, too heavy? You have.

Have you ever been empty, tired, dipping at 3pm, faintly restless, told yourself "I need something right now," "sugary somethings to get me through," and then the same bakery smelled like heaven?

Same bakery. Same molecules. Two different states. The smell did not change. You did.

What changed? The Gremlin whispered and the Brainwashing translated. The faint, empty, slightly restless feeling rose, you called it need, and the nose obeyed the story.

That is cue reactivity. Science has a careful phrase for it that I will keep careful for you. Imaging work suggests refined carbs and fats in combination evoke patterns of striatal signalling akin to addictive substances, and the fat-plus-sugar combo seems to have a supra-additive effect. I use "seems to" deliberately because that part is interpretation, not proven fact — it is consistent with the brain images, not proven by them. It also describes two things that make sense of your experience: with repeated hits, the hit needs to get bigger to feel the same, and cues — the wrapper, the smell, the aisle — fire before you taste anything.

Do you hear how that helps without scaring you? It says: of course the smell fires. Of course you now need the family size where the small once did. That is not proof you are weak. It is a plausible picture of escalation and trigger. It is not a diagnosis on you. And it does not mean you are compelled. It means the trap fitted a trigger to your nose and a blunting to your hit, so you chase more.

But here is the key: the trigger proves nothing about pleasure. A bell can make a dog salivate without proving the bell is delicious.

Your nose learned a story. Stories can be unlearned when you reassign the credit.

Trap questions to close this:

If the taste were genuinely wonderful in itself, why does it need a cue — a wrapper, a smell, a 3pm dip — to make you want it?

Does a truly wonderful taste need you to be low to seem wonderful?

Do you need to be tired to enjoy an orange? Do you need a cue to want water when you are thirsty for water? No. Real pleasure does not need a low to sell it.

BAD SUGAR does. That tells you everything.

### WHAT YOU ACTUALLY LOVE

So let us reassign the credit fully, with warmth and with amusement, not shame. We were all conned. The industry engineered the gatecrasher to look like the life of the party. You are not foolish for applauding the party. You just applauded the wrong guest.

What do you actually love when you think you love BAD SUGAR?

You love fat. Fat is mouthfeel. Fat carries flavour. Fat satisfies. The industry knows this. That is why almost no BAD SUGAR you love is eaten without fat. Biscuit is flour plus fat plus sugar. Chocolate is fat plus sugar. Ice cream is fat plus sugar. Pastry is fat plus sugar. Crisps are fat plus salt plus starch. Take the fat away and the "love" vanishes. Keep the fat and reduce the sugar and you still have something you enjoy. That inversion is the tell.

You love texture. Crunch, snap, flake, chew, melt, cream, crumb. BAD SUGAR itself has one texture: sweet grit or sweet sludge. The texture you love is built by fat, baking, freezing, whipping — not by sweetness.

You love aroma and context. Warmth, kitchen, cinema, sun, friends laughing, celebration, leisure, break, music. You proved this in the last chapter. Remove the celebration and eat BAD SUGAR alone at a bare table. Flat. Add the celebration and remove BAD SUGAR. Still wonderful. The context was the taste. BAD SUGAR was sneaking a ride.

You love salt to balance sweet and sweet to balance salt. You love contrast, not sugar alone. Pure sugar without contrast is cloying. You know this because you have tipped too much sugar into tea and spat it out. Your tongue said, too much. That is your tongue telling the truth.

Ask your tongue without the Brainwashing.

Eat a raw apple. Slowly. Notice the sweet, the sour, the juice. That is sweetness with structure, with fibre, with water. Your tongue knows it. Eat a bite of cheese or plain yoghurt. Notice the depth. Eat butter on rye. Notice the richness without any sweet.

Now eat a spoonful of granulated sugar. Just sugar. No company. What do you feel? Sweet, then sickly. Wanting water. Wanting it to stop. Is that love?

Or eat a mouthful of plain white bread with nothing. Chew. What is there? Paste. Air. A faint sweet that you would never choose to savour.

Your tongue has always known. The Brainwashing shouted over it.

I know this is hard to accept if you have called yourself a sugar lover for years. But notice the amusement that arrives when you see the gatecrasher. It is not heavy. It is light. A trick exposed is funny. You were not weak. You were loyal to a party you were told the wrong guest made great.

Is BAD SUGAR a genuine pleasure or treat when it cannot taste good alone?

Is it love of taste when you must hide the taste of the thing itself with chocolate, butter, cream and salt to enjoy it?

You know the answer. Say it flatly, as settled fact, and feel the weight lift.

BAD SUGAR has no taste you love. The company it brings has the taste you love. BAD SUGAR was only sneaking a ride.

Once you see that, the "connoisseur" story collapses.

You are not a connoisseur of BAD SUGAR. You are a connoisseur of fat, texture, aroma, company, occasion — human, natural things — and BAD SUGAR rented their reputation.

Take the reputation back.

### THE FIREWALL THAT KEEPS THE TASTE CON ALIVE

There is one reason this taste con survives even after you see it. It is not your tongue. It is the noise around you.

The world will keep telling you BAD SUGAR is taste. Ads, wrappers, friends, family, magazines, diets, influencers, the person at work who quit by suffering and wants you to suffer too. They will say, life without sweetness is bland. They will say, you must resist. They will say, discipline, stay strong, abstain, one day at a time. They will say, cut down, keep the good chocolate, just have one, once a week is fine.

That is the willpower method talking. Not truth. The willpower method is the anti-method that kept you trapped long before this book. It teaches you to white-knuckle a deprivation that never needed to exist. It tells you taste is sacrifice. It tells you freedom is willpower. It is wrong. It has always been wrong. And every time you failed with it, you blamed yourself when you should have blamed the method.

Do you want to stay free? Then you need a firewall for your mind. Not for your mouth. For your beliefs.

Because your body can cope with an accidental blip — a sauce you did not expect, a bite you did not intend. Your body is forgiving. Your mind is not. Your mind will try to reinterpret a blip as permission. "I got away with it, so I can do it again." That is the Sweet Brainwashing trying to re-invite the gatecrasher after you showed him the door. Guard the belief, not the behaviour.

So here, at the climax of this chapter where taste is reassigned, I give you the epistemic firewall that protects every taste reframe that follows. This is instruction five. It is not an apology. It is a command, given with warm, total confidence, because following it is how you stay free easily.

Read it again and hear the rationale, because I never give you a command without the warm reason.

Ignore diets. Why? Diets are the willpower method in costume. They keep BAD SUGAR precious by making it forbidden. Forbidden fruit intensifies fantasy. Fantasy intensifies the hit when you do have it. That is not freedom. That is the trap tightening.

Ignore willpower tips. Why? Willpower assumes a genuine pleasure or treat is being sacrificed and you must resist. We have just proved there is no taste to sacrifice. Willpower answers the wrong question. It polices the mouth while the belief stays intact. You do not need policing. You need seeing.

Ignore stories from people who quit by suffering. Why? Their suffering proves they kept the taste illusion alive while they stopped the behaviour. They still believe they gave up a pleasure. So they feel deprived, they count days, they warn you it is hard so you will expect it to be hard. Expect hardship and you create it. I want you to expect freedom, because freedom is the truth. Their story is not your story. Your story is escape.

This book is belief-change, not medical care. Continue to follow your clinician's advice on food and medication. That box stays with you for the rest of this book. We can be warm and firm about BAD SUGAR and still be safe and respectful about medicine.

With that firewall in place, you can hear the taste truth without the world talking you out of it overnight.

You can also hear it without fear. I used one careful piece of science in this chapter — that imaging picture of escalation and cue firing, where the fat-plus-sugar combo seems to have a supra-additive effect — and I kept its limit in plain sight: seems to, plausible, consistent with images, not proven. I do not need you to believe a lab to see the gatecrasher. Your own kitchen proved it. The science only says, if you noticed you need more to feel the same, and the wrapper fires you before the bite, you are not imagining it. The belief and the schedule do that together. That is perception work, not scare. No fear needed. No willpower needed. Just seeing.

So see it. Now. Tonight.

At your next meal, do this small homework. It takes sixty seconds.

Pick one BAD SUGAR you think you love. Hold it. Smell it. Look at the company list on the label — fat, oil, butter, cream, salt, flour. Separate the gatecrasher from the guests in your mind. Now imagine the guests without the gatecrasher: the fat, the crunch, the aroma, the cold, the company of people you like. How much remains? Almost all.

Now imagine the gatecrasher without the guests: sweet grit, sweet syrup, plain paste. How much remains? Almost nothing.

Who made the taste?

You know. It was never BAD SUGAR you loved. It was the company that made BAD SUGAR seem worth loving. BAD SUGAR was only sneaking a ride.

Let that land as the settled verdict it is.

And because it is settled, the next time a voice says, but the taste! — you will smile. You will hear the old translation fail in real time. You will not need to argue with your tongue. Your tongue already told the truth.

You can get excited about this. Really.
