Chapter 10
NO SPECIAL DOSE

IN THIS CHAPTER
- Office birthday cake and the negotiated sliver
- Kitchen drawer late in the evening
- Cliff edge and the smiling guide
- Back seat belt on an ordinary drive

*If one sweet never gave you anything, one occasional sweet will never keep anything.*

YOU ARE ALREADY FREE OF THE BEST NIGHT

You have seen it clearly now.

The best night was the friend and the film and the relief of Friday, never the box on the armrest. Lift the box out and nothing is lost. That is yours forever, and I celebrate with you.

Yet the old voice is clever. It changes its clothes. It no longer says the sweet makes the night wonderful. It says, very reasonably:

“All right. No credit to sugar. But surely I can keep just a sliver? Just birthdays? Just cut down? Just one?”

I love that question because it is honest. We all asked it. We thought a small kept pleasure would be stable, a safe compromise between trap and freedom.

It is not stable. It is the trap itself, wearing a smaller hat.

Let us look, together, with open eyes.

THE SLIVER THAT TALKS ALL EVENING

See us last Tuesday.

Office. Three o’clock. Birthday cake on the table, candles, laughing. You say to yourself, “I’ve managed to be good this week. One thin sliver cannot hurt. I don’t keep it in the house, so this is controlled.”

You take the sliver. You eat it politely. You smile. No binge. No shame. Proof, you think, that you can handle just a treat.

Then watch what happens.

Four o’clock, the taste is gone but the thought is not. Five o’clock, you are brighter and then flatter. On the bus home you are picturing the shop. By nine you are at the kitchen drawer you swore was empty, finishing what was left from the weekend, staring a little dazed and asking, “How did I get here again?”

We have all lived that evening. One bite becomes the box, not because you are weak, but because the craving, once triggered, carries the binge.

Ask yourself, kindly, with your own evening as witness:

If the sliver satisfied you, why did you think about sweets all evening?

If just one is stable, why has just one never stayed just one?

If control was proved at three o’clock, who emptied the drawer at nine?

There is only one honest answer. The sliver did not close the matter. It reopened it.

CUTTING DOWN MAKES THE CALL LOUDER

“But I will cut down,” you say. “Less often. Smaller. Once a week is fine.”

I understand. It sounds sensible. Less poison, less problem.

It works the other way around.

When we grazed all day we were dull and topped up. When we restrict and allow ourselves a scheduled little dose, every dose becomes precious. We wait for it. We fantasise about it. We hear the wrapper before we feel hunger. The volume of the evening then grows to make up for the restraint of the day.

That diet-binge yo-yo is no accident. Intermittent treats drive bigger binges than steady grazing ever did. We lived it every January: strict days, dreaming nights, then the whole box gone on Friday.

So ask:

If cutting down made sweets smaller in your life, why did they grow larger in your mind?

If “once a week” was freedom, why did the week bend itself around that one day?

The answer never changes. Restriction does not loosen the knot. It tightens it.

That is how the sugar trap keeps you negotiating.

You are not feeding hunger, you are feeding the Nibbler.

What returns at nine o’clock is not hunger but an empty, gnawing, slightly restless craving.

THE GUIDE AT THE CLIFF

Let me show you how strange the bargain sounds when we strip the sugar words away.

Imagine we walk together to a bright cliff on a summer day. Gulls. Sea far below. A smiling guide in a neat uniform gestures to the edge.

“Jump,” he says, “but jump safely. Just fall a few metres. We have limited the fall. Once a week is fine.”

You stare at him. “If falling a few metres is safe, why jump at all? And if jumping is wonderful, why limit the fall?”

He smiles wider. “We have special small jumps. Birthday jumps. Just-one jumps.”

You would laugh, and you would walk away, because the offer is nonsense. You cannot arrange a safe way to do an unsafe thing that gives you nothing in the first place.

Yet that is exactly what we say when we say, “I will keep only the special ones.” The special birthday sliver. The delayed start on Monday. The weaned-down smaller bar. The swapped honey spoon or extra-large biscuit instead of cake.

If no daily dose gives you anything, what can an occasional dose keep except the fall?

Would you thank a strong arm for pulling you gasping from the river, then learn the same pusher had shoved you in upstream to sell you the rescue? You would be warm to any swimmer who was fooled, and furious at the pusher. Be so here. The rescue you felt from the sliver was relief from the gnaw the last dose lit.

Do you want to pay that price for the rest of your life?

ALL THE CLEVER VERSIONS ARE ONE VERSION

Let us take them all, because the trap loves variety, and show they are one door.

“I will quit tomorrow.” Tomorrow never eats, tomorrow never craves, tomorrow never decides. Only today does. Postponing keeps today’s dose safe and guarantees tomorrow’s gnaw.

“I will wean down.” Tapering stretches the waiting, feeds the fantasy between hits, and makes each hit flare brighter. Weaning is not softening the landing. It is lengthening the fall.

“I will substitute.” Swap cake for biscuits, bar for sweet drink, white for brown, sugar for syrup — still a shot of sweet, still feeding the same little creature, still lighting the same evening call. Change the uniform and the pusher still pushes.

“I will allow just one when it really matters.” But when does it not matter? Birthdays matter. Tired evenings matter. Fridays matter. The rule breeds a hundred exceptions, and one exception is enough to keep the belief alive: that there is still something in it worth keeping.

“I live for dessert. Food would be flat without the sweet finish.” We believed that until we watched the finish itself: cloy, stick, rush, then the call for more. The meal glowed before dessert arrived. Dessert only pasted its label over the glow and sent us back to the drawer.

See them plain. Tiny specials. Cut-down. Delay. Substitute. Just-once. They are not five safe paths. They are five names for keeping the whole trap alive so only zero BAD SUGAR is stable.

And zero is not harsh news. Zero is the easy news.

THE HUMP IS SMALL AND IT PASSES

“But will zero not be hard? Will I not crave for the rest of my life?”

No. Listen, because I promise you this from escape, not theory.

Keeping a little dose means craving for the rest of your life. A little gnaw kept alive, fed on schedule, never allowed to die, calling every evening.

Taking no dose means a small rough patch for a few days that fades. The little creature complains, then starves. The mind stops hearing the wrapper. The drawer stops talking.

I will not give you a calendar for your body or threaten you with a hard withdrawal — this is no mighty ordeal, only a dying flutter. For most of us it is days, not weeks, and small, not terrible. Then it is gone.

Which would you choose? A small hump once, or a small gnaw forever?

You have nothing sweet to lose and everything to gain.

THE BELT IS NOT PERMISSION TO CRASH

Now hear the kind part, because I want you free, not frightened.

Life will blip. A sauce sweetened without your knowing. A bite taken before you thought. A well-meaning aunt pressing cake into your hand. Your body is marvellous. It handles an accident and moves on.

That is what a seatbelt is for.

You click the belt across your lap on every ordinary drive, not because you plan to steer into the wall, but because a jolt can happen. The belt does not give you permission to drive erratically. It quietly protects you if someone else swerves.

So it is here. An accidental taste revives nothing unless you let the old thought back in. Guard the belief, never the behaviour. Do not count, do not confess for days, do not turn a jolt into a crash by saying, “I have blown it, so I might as well carry on.”

Your body can cope with a blip, but your mind must never reopen the door.

Read that as comfort, not warning. The door stays shut because there is nothing behind it you want. The belt sits there for accidents, never as licence to keep a special dose on purpose.

WHAT YOU WILL FEEL NEXT

Let me tell you what happens when no dose is kept, so you can look forward with excitement, not dread.

Soon, on an ordinary morning, you will walk past the supermarket aisle where the bright packs wall the way, and feel nothing pulling. No argument. No tug. Just relief that you are walking past to real food you love.

Soon, on a quiet evening, you will sit before the television with the sweet drawer empty and feel calm spread where the nine o’clock gnaw used to start. No loss. No missing. Just yourself, present and free.

Soon, at three in the afternoon, you will notice the old dread is gone — no lift to chase, no fog to pay for, just a steady day moving toward evening.

That supermarket pass. That calm TV evening. That clear morning without the afternoon dread. They are coming. They are not far. They are the proof freedom gives, and you will rejoice in them.

There is no safe sliver because there was never a genuine gift in the sliver. There is only escape, and escape is whole or it is not escape.

10. TAKE NO SPECIAL DOSE IN ANY FORM
If no dose gives, no occasional dose keeps.

SUMMARY
- The office sliver felt controlled and still led to the evening drawer
- A triggered craving carries the binge, which is the trap working, not weakness
- Cutting down makes each dose more precious and the evening bigger
- Delay, weaning, swapping and just-one are the same kept door in different clothes
- No dose means a brief small hump that fades, not craving forever
- An accidental taste is a jolt the body rides through, never a reason to reopen the door
