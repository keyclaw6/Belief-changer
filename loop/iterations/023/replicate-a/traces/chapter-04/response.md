Chapter 4
THE AFTERNOON LIE

IN THIS CHAPTER
- The office drawer at three o'clock
- A biscuit eaten standing at the desk
- Fog over the keyboard at four
- The market lunch remembered in the belly

*That lift at three o'clock was never fuel — it was a loan that made the fog.*

THE DRAWER THAT PROVES IT

I know where you are, because we have all lived there.

You have seen the empty benefit side. You remember the slow lunch that left you clean and full for hours. And still, at half past three, the drawer speaks louder than memory.

The eyes go gritty. The brain goes woolly. A voice says:

“I need sugary somethings… to get me through.”

Then that I-need-to-eat-something-RIGHT-NOW feeling rises up the chest. You take the biscuit, the bar, the shot of sweet from the drawer. You eat it standing at the desk. For ten, fifteen minutes there is a brightening. The shoulders drop. You think, there, that proves it. I needed fuel and I got it.

Then at four the fog comes down again, thicker. You stare at the screen. You reread the same email. You feel tired and cross and a little sorry for yourself. And you think you need another dose.

My friend, I am warm to you and vicious to what did this to you. You are not weak and you are not imagining it. You are caught on the roller coaster and mistaking the ride for energy.

Let us look at that afternoon together, hour by hour, because your own day will tell you the truth if you watch what follows what.

THE LOAN, NOT THE FUEL

Picture a money-lender on your street.

At noon he knocks and presses a crisp ten-pound note into your hand. Wonderful, you think. What a kind man. At four he knocks again and takes twenty pounds back, plus a kick to your confidence on the way out. At seven he offers you another ten to soften the bruise.

Would you call that man your fuel? Would you thank him for keeping you going?

Of course you would not. You would see him for what he is. He creates the hole he pretends to fill.

We did exactly that with BAD SUGAR every afternoon, you and I.

A morning dose sets the pattern. The lift you feel is your body rushing to deal with the sudden flood — insulin working hard to clear it. That rush is the brightening. The same rush can overshoot and leave you dipped and dim an hour later. That dip is the fog.

I am not diagnosing your blood sugar and I am not talking about illness. That common three o'clock heaviness is not the same as clinically low glucose. It is simpler and sadder. Lift, then sag. Brightening, then dimming. All set in motion by the last dose.

Ask yourself honestly now, as an investigator, not as a judge.

If that biscuit was fuel, why did it never carry you cleanly to supper the way the market lunch carried you for hours?

If it gave real energy, why did you feel dimmer at four than you felt at three before you ate it?

If it fixed tiredness, why do the sweetest afternoons end in the flattest evenings?

There is only one honest answer. It never fuelled you. It borrowed brightness and charged you fog.

That shiver of relief was never a genuine treat or lift.

It was relief from a restlessness the previous dose had left behind, sold back to you as help. A loan, not a wage. And every loan tightens the next craving.

WHY THE FLATNESS FOLLOWS

Watch further than four o'clock, because the trap does not stop at the desk.

Those who get free describe it the same way, in their own words: a quick lift that is inevitably followed by a fast drop. Not a theory. A lived pattern. Sweet, bright, gone, grey. Sweet again to lift the grey.

And there is a longer shadow too. In one long look at men, those who ate sweets day after day were more likely to feel low and flat in the months and years after. Not lifted. Lower. I say it carefully: for men, day after day, the daily treat predicted later low mood, not relief. I am not saying sweets doom everyone to misery, and I am not diagnosing you. I am showing you the direction of travel. The thing sold as a mood-lifter points the mood down.

Does that surprise you? It should not. You have felt it on a small clock every day. What borrows at three charges at four. What borrows day after day charges mood after mood.

Think of a torch that burns its own house to make light. Flare, then smoke. Flare, then darker. You would not call that torch a power station. You would put it down and open the curtains.

Remember the curtains. That morning belly growling cleanly. The market stall, steam rising, food eaten slowly on a bench. No rush, no spike, no bargaining. Then fullness spreading, steady and quiet, work done without thinking about food again. Your body knew hunger. It knew enough. It knew done.

Compare it. The market lunch left you free for hours. The drawer dose left you hungry for another dose within the hour. Which one behaved like fuel? Which one behaved like a lender collecting?

The answer ends the argument. The drawer proves nothing except that the last dose creates the need for the next. It never takes you above the steady ease of a person who never took the dose. It drops you below, then briefly stops dropping you, and calls that kindness.

See it, and the dread of the slump changes shape. You do not face an afternoon you cannot survive without sweets. You face an afternoon that sweets themselves made unbearable — and that ordinary food and a clear body already know how to carry.

4. NEVER JUDGE THE AFTERNOON BY THE LIFT
Watch what the crash really follows.

SUMMARY
- The brightening after a sweet is a short loan, not steady fuel
- The same rush that lifts can leave the dim fog an hour later
- Day after day the sweet points mood down, not up
- The market lunch gave hours of clean fullness, the drawer dose gave minutes then hunger for more
- The slump does not prove need, it proves the last dose is collecting
