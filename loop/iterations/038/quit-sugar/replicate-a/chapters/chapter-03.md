Chapter 3
WHAT DOES IT DO FOR YOU

*Forget what it costs you for a moment — tell me what it ever actually gave you.*

You Already Know It Harms You

I am not going to lecture you about harm.

You already know the harm. You have read the packets. You have heard the warnings at the dentist with the light in your mouth. You have felt the heaviness after the evening box is empty and the flatness the next morning. You have done the quiet arithmetic that we all do.

"I know I eat too much sweet stuff. I know it rots teeth. I know it piles weight on. I know men in my family got ill from it."

That voice is familiar because we have all spoken it. We say it standing at the cupboard. We say it sitting in the dentist chair. We say it tugging at a waistband. The harm is not news to you.

And yet you still reach. I did. We all did.

That should tell you something enormous about the way the trap works. If harm were enough, you would have walked away years ago. You were frightened enough. You were warned enough. You promised enough on January mornings. Fear of harm never once stopped the hand at ten at night.

So I am going to change the question entirely. I do not want to argue harm against benefit with you, because that argument keeps you trapped. It leaves you saying, "Yes, it harms me, but the benefit outweighs it." It leaves you weighing and bargaining and hoping a smaller harm can be bought for the same glow.

I am asking a different question, a plainer one, and I want you to stay with it.

Forget what it does TO you for now. What does it do FOR you?

Name one real thing it adds to your Tuesday that Tuesday did not already own.

"I know it's bad for me, but I enjoy it. But it gets me through. But life would be grey without a little something sweet."

Do you hear that? I hear it because I said it. That is the whole defence in three sentences. It concedes the harm and clings to the glow.

Very well. Let us put the glow on the table and look at it.

An Ordinary Tuesday, Counted Honestly

Bring me an ordinary Tuesday, not a bad day. Not Christmas. Not a birthday. A plain working Tuesday where you were, in your own words, "being quite good."

Morning. Tea with two sugars because that is how the mug was handed to you years ago and you never questioned it. A sweetened cereal in a hurry, or toast with spread that lists sugar second. You do not count this as a treat. You count this as breakfast. The packet would count it as three or four small doses before you have left the house.

Mid-morning. The drawer opens. Biscuits from the shared tin. You take two because one looks mean and three looks greedy. You eat them standing by the printer, tasting hurry more than taste, crumbs on the keyboard. Mood lifts for ten minutes, then thins.

Lunch is plain enough. Then the shop on the way back. A bottle of something sweet because water feels dull beside bright labels. You drink half without thinking and leave half warm on the desk. That bottle alone can hold more free sugar than the whole day is meant to hold.

Three o'clock. The hour we all know. Eyes heavy. Work slow. Chocolate from the machine. The wrapper rustles. The first square is fast. The second is faster. For minutes the fog lifts a little. Then the fog returns with interest and the hand thinks about the drawer again.

Evening. Dinner eaten properly. Then the sofa. Then the hour we met in the last chapter. The biscuit meant as one becomes four, becomes six. The ice cream tub with the lid left off too long round the edges. The late spoon straight from the jar because a bowl feels like an admission.

Now lay that day out the way an investigator would, not the way a judge would. I do not want guilt. I want sight.

Mood across the day: up for minutes, down for hours, up for minutes, down for hours. Never level. Never settled.

Teeth: bathed again and again in sweet liquid and crumbs. You brushed morning and night, as you should, and still the acid had hours to work between. Brushing matters, it matters a great deal, and still frequent doses between brushing give plaque the very feast it turns into acid.

Wallet: coins and notes gone in small forgettable amounts. Two pounds here. Three pounds there. Nothing that feels like spending, everything that adds up when you keep the receipts for a week.

Time: minutes here and there thinking about it, queuing for it, unwrapping it, hiding the proof of it, brushing after it, planning to be good tomorrow because of it. An hour a day easily, given to something you call a small pleasure.

Look at that Tuesday as a whole. You were careful. You were moderate by your own count. You never sat down and gorged in the way shame pictures it. And still the doses stacked from morning to night without a single calm hour that needed them.

So I put my three questions to you, and I want honest answers in your head.

Where in that list is the hour that was clearly better because BAD SUGAR was in it?

What did those eight or ten small hits add that plain food, drink, rest, laughter, work done, would not have given you on its own?

And if each one was truly a gift, why did the day end flat, foggy, tired, with teeth aching faintly and the packet light again?

You know the answers. A gift does not need hiding. A gift does not leave you chasing the next gift within the hour. A gift does not charge you mood, teeth, money and time and hand you hurry in return.

What Did You Call It

Let us speak the defence out loud, because it cannot survive being spoken.

You called it a little treat. You called it fuel to carry on. You called it "sugary somethings to get me through" and "a treat when you feel you deserve one" and "celebrating with chocolate" and "a short term lift then fast drop" you forgive because the lift feels kind at the time.

I understand why. We all used those words. They were handed to us before we could weigh them. Sweet talk at birthdays. Sweet talk for being brave at the dentist. Sweet talk for getting through exams, for getting through a break-up, for getting through a plain Thursday. The words arrived with love, so we never doubted them.

Strip the love away for a minute and look at the object.

A treat is something that adds. It leaves you richer than it found you. It does not ask to be repeated within the hour. It does not punish the teeth that tasted it. It does not empty the packet while you watch.

Fuel is something that powers. It is taken once and burns steady. It does not spike and vanish and demand more fuel to fix the lack of fuel. A car does not need petrol to cure the lack of petrol caused by petrol.

Did your Tuesday treat behave like a treat? Did your Tuesday fuel behave like fuel?

You took a dose to lift a dip. The lift lasted minutes. The dip returned. You took another dose to lift the dip the last dose left behind. The packet lightened. The mood never levelled. The teeth never thanked you. The wallet never thanked you.

That is not the behaviour of a gift. That is the behaviour of a loop reading its own echo as a gift.

We have a name for that echo when it rises in the body, and we will look at it fully together in its own place. For now I want you to see the belief part, the story laid over the echo that tells you the echo means "this helps me." That story says BAD SUGAR is a genuine treat or fuel, a kind helper that sweetens life and carries you through.

Say that phrase slowly in your head and test it against Tuesday.

A genuine treat or fuel would not need secrecy. It would not need the bin lid lifted to hide the wrapper. It would not need the morning promise and the night film that contradicts it. It would sit in daylight and bear looking at.

Your Tuesday doses do not bear looking at. They bear hiding. That tells you what they are.

Teeth And Guidance, Plainly Stated

Let me give you two hard facts, flatly, because you need the ground under you.

First, the teeth. The fact is, frequent free sugars feed the film on teeth, the film turns them to acid, and the acid eats the surface. That is how decay starts. It is not the only cause — brushing, fluoride, saliva, how often you eat, all shape it — and I am not giving you dental orders. I am stating the mechanism as it is. A mouth washed often in sweet liquid, with crumbs held between teeth for hours, is a mouth kept in acid longer. The dentist sees the result months later. The cause lived in ordinary Tuesdays.

Look in the bathroom mirror tonight. Pull the lip back. See the faint chalk edge near the gum, the filling that catches the light, the tooth that sings with cold water. That is not character. That is chemistry repeated daily. BAD SUGAR did that TO the tooth. What did it ever do FOR the tooth?

Second, the measure. The guidance for free sugars sits below a tenth of daily energy, about fifty grams, with a lower mark below five percent where extra benefit shows. Ordinary eating now sits far above both, every day, without feeling excessive. Feeling fine does not mean intake is low. Feeling normal in a sweetened market only means the market moved while you were not watching.

I tell you this not to frighten you into changing. If you live with diabetes, take medicine that moves blood sugar or appetite, are pregnant, live with an eating disorder past or present, or carry any medical risk where food changes matter, follow your clinician first and use this book for sight alone.

Take the guidance as what it is: a population mark showing how far normal has drifted, not a sentence on you. One bottle, one box, one "harmless" Tuesday can sail past the mark before dinner. A moderate who counts treats as harmless is often past the mark by three in the afternoon and still calling himself moderate at ten.

Ask again, with that measure in view.

If the doses were gifts, why would guidance from every sober quarter say take less of them, not more? When did a genuine helper ever need a ceiling?

And if they were fuel, why would the body that makes its own steady fuel from plain meals need constant top-ups from a packet? Your body already knows how to hold you level between meals. It did it when you were small. It does it when you sleep through the night without a biscuit at 2 a.m.

The Till Receipt

Stay with Tuesday and open your pockets, because the coins tell what memory softens.

Morning till first. The box of sweetened cereal, three pounds eighty, gone in six hurried bowls. The jar of spread, two pounds forty, sugar listed second in small print. The bag of sugar for tea, one pound sixty, the spoon you no longer measure. None of it feels like buying sweets. All of it is doses bought as staples. Divide the boxes by the days they last and Tuesday alone carries a pound of that shop before you have left the kitchen.

Mid-morning till. You tell yourself the shared tin cost nothing because someone else brought it. Count it anyway. Someone paid. The tin at the corner shop is two pounds twenty. Two biscuits from it are thirty pence of sweet crumb eaten standing by the printer. You taste hurry, not value. The crumb sits on the keyboard and you brush it off. Thirty pence for ten minutes of lift and hours of thinness. What did those thirty pence build? What hour did they improve?

Lunchtime till. The shop on the way back. One tall bottle, one pound eighty. Bright label, cold sweat on the plastic. You drink half walking and leave half warm on the desk, then pour the warm half away at four because it tastes flat. That is one pound eighty for six mouthfuls and a warm remainder tipped into the sink. Keep the receipt and look at it at night. The paper says what the throat forgot. Sweet liquid bought, half wasted, teeth washed in it between.

Three o'clock till. The machine in the corridor. One bar, one pound ten. The coil turns slowly and drops it with a thud. The wrapper rustles too loud in a quiet office. You break one square and then another at the desk. The foil goes in the drawer because the bin feels too public. One pound ten for minutes of buzz and a drawer that smells sweet for days. The drawer still holds yesterday's foil and the day before's. Add them. Three days of foil is three pounds thirty for fog followed by more fog.

Evening till. The packet of biscuits, two pounds twenty for twelve, six gone after dinner while the kettle boils. That is one pound ten eaten standing up without a plate. The tub of ice cream, four pounds fifty, the lid left off till the edge melts and refreezes into crystals. Two large spoonfuls are a pound of that tub. The spoon straight from the jar late at night, the jar three pounds, the spoon perhaps fifty pence of thick sweetness taken because a bowl felt like an admission.

Lay the slips side by side on the kitchen table at midday tomorrow. Morning pound. Mid-morning thirty pence. Bottle one eighty. Bar one ten. Evening packet share one ten. Tub share one pound. Late spoon fifty pence. That is more than seven pounds for one careful, moderate, being-quite-good day.

Run that day across a week. Not a bad week. A normal week. Seven pounds a day is forty-nine pounds. Forty-nine pounds is a shelf of plain food that would feed hunger for days. It is a pair of shoes. It is light and heat and phone credit. Given gladly, it would buy something you could name. Given to BAD SUGAR, it buys wrappers pressed under peelings and bottles poured half away.

Run it across a month. Near two hundred pounds moved from purse to till to bin in small forgettable amounts that never felt like spending. No single purchase frightened you. That is how the tax works. It never sends one large bill. It sends a hundred small ones you throw away.

Put the month of receipts in a pile and stand over them. Coins and notes gone. Foil and card and plastic gone to the bin. Teeth acid-washed. Mood yo-yoed. Packet light again. That is plenty TO you in hard paper. Now show me the FOR side on that table. What single receipt in that pile names a benefit that plain food, water, rest, company would not have handed you without charge? Which slip bought an hour distinctly better than the same hour without it? You cannot find it, because the pile charges and does not give.

The Clock

Now count Tuesday by the clock, because money is only half the tax.

Seven forty-two. You stand at the cupboard thinking about breakfast while the kettle boils. What to have that feels quick and kind? Two minutes deciding, then sweetened cereal because it is there. Three minutes eating standing up, scrolling messages, tasting hurry. Two minutes wiping the spill and rinsing the bowl. Seven minutes given before work has started.

Ten fifteen. The drawer thought arrives before hunger. Shall I? I have been good. Perhaps just one. Three minutes of talk in the head while work waits. Two minutes at the printer eating, crumbs brushed from keys. Five minutes circling back to the drawer to check what is left and to straighten the tin so it looks untouched. Ten minutes for two biscuits.

Twelve forty. The shop walk. Eight minutes queuing behind two people with meal deals, eyes on the bright fridge while you wait. Two minutes choosing between bottles that promise the same lift in different colours. One minute paying, coins counted, card tapped, receipt pocketed and forgotten. Six minutes walking back drinking half without tasting it. Seventeen minutes to buy a thirst you did not have and leave half of it warm.

Three o'clock. The machine hour. Four minutes feeling the eyes go heavy and arguing whether to go. Three minutes standing at the machine feeding coins, watching the coil turn. Five minutes at the desk unwrapping quietly, breaking squares, chewing quickly between emails. Six minutes after, restless, checking the drawer again, brushing teeth with tongue, drinking water to clear the sweet coat. Eighteen minutes for a lift that lasted less than the buying of it.

Eight forty. The sofa hour. Five minutes thinking about the biscuit while the programme plays, the packet glowing from the kitchen though you cannot see it. Three minutes getting up, filling the kettle, lifting the packet down. Six minutes eating standing up, one becoming four, tasting hurry and sugar coat. Four minutes tidying — folding the top flat, pushing the packet behind the oats, shaking crumbs into palm, lifting the bin lid and pressing the wrapper down. Three minutes brushing hard to remove the smell. Twenty-one minutes to take a gift that needed hiding.

Ten fifty. The late spoon. Two minutes hearing the gnaw and calling it hunger though dinner was full. Two minutes at the fridge with the door open, light on face. One minute with the spoon in the jar. Two minutes at the sink, rinsing, wiping, checking the bin. Seven minutes to quiet a tug for minutes and to wake it again for tomorrow.

Add the clock-trail. Seven plus ten plus seventeen plus eighteen plus twenty-one plus seven. Eighty minutes. Near an hour and a half of a plain Tuesday spent thinking about it, queuing for it, unwrapping it, hiding the proof of it, brushing after it, planning to be good tomorrow because of it.

Eighty minutes a day is nine hours a week. Nine hours is a working day given to unwrapping and hiding. It is a walk taken slowly. It is a meal cooked properly and eaten sitting down. It is sleep. Spent dose by dose, it never feels like hours. Counted by the clock, it is hours.

And what did those hours buy? Hurry. Rustle. Foil in the drawer. Wrapper under peelings. Teeth brushed hard to remove the witness. A morning promise and a night film that contradict each other. That is plenty TO you in minutes. Show me the FOR side on that clock. Which of those eighty minutes added love to company, taste to hunger, rest to tiredness? The programme owned the laughter. The dinner owned the fullness. The work owned the completion. The dose rode along and took the credit, then charged you the minutes for the ride.

The Hand That Pushes And The Hand That Lifts

Let me put a question to you, only as a question for now.

What would you call a man who trips you every morning on the same step, then rushes forward to lift you up, dusts you down kindly, and accepts your thanks?

Would you call him a helper? Would you pay him for the rest of your life for the service of his hand?

Or would you, once you saw the foot move out, understand in a flash that the rescue and the fall came from the same boot?

Hold that picture beside Tuesday. Do not chase it yet. I will walk you fully into that ditch and out of it when we have killed the belief that feeds the tug. For now let the question sit beside the empty benefit column and do its quiet work.

We were conned, not foolish. We thanked the hand because we never saw the foot. We called the lift a gift because we never counted how often the lift followed a fall the last gift had dug.

A gift that first creates the need it then meets is not a gift. It is a debt sold as kindness.

For The Rest Of Your Life

Now widen the lens, because Tuesdays become years without changing shape.

Take your ordinary Tuesday and run it forward. Same drawer. Same shop. Same sofa hour. Same ten minutes up, same hour down. Same teeth bathed, same coins gone, same minutes given to thinking about the next dose.

Run it forward a year. That is three hundred and sixty-five Tuesdays of small falls and small lifts, none of them adding anything you can name, all of them charging mood, teeth, money, time.

Run it forward ten years. That is thousands of packets, hundreds of hours unwrapping and hiding and promising, fillings and bills and waistbands moved notch by notch while you called yourself moderate.

Is that what you want to buy with your days? To keep paying in fog and money and tooth surface for the rest of your life for a glow you cannot name when asked?

Or picture the other side, which I lay before you now only as sight, not as effort. Picture Tuesdays where no hand moves before thought, where no packet needs hiding, where mood is not yo-yoed by doses, where teeth are not bathed hourly in acid, where money stays and time stays. Picture walking clear of that small daily tax for the rest of your life, with eating left to hunger and satisfaction where it belongs.

Both futures run for the rest of your life. One is a tax paid for nothing. The other is the same days with the tax removed.

I know which one you want. Of course you want the second — that is why you are holding this book.

"But I like it." Do you? Or do you like the minute when a nagging tug goes quiet, a quiet the last dose made brief? "But it's only small." Is it small when it repeats ten times a day for decades? "But everyone eats it." Does everyone being in the same ditch make the ditch a garden?

Answer honestly. When did BAD SUGAR ever give you an hour distinctly better than the same hour without it? When did it add love to company, taste to hunger, rest to tiredness? The hour owned those good things. The dose only rode along and took the credit.

Here is the verdict Tuesday forces, and I state it as settled fact because Tuesday proves it.

It does plenty TO you. It does nothing FOR you.

Read that again and feel the weight shift. Plenty TO you — mood yo-yoed, teeth acid-washed, money thinned, time eaten, packet light, shame planted. Nothing FOR you — no steady power added, no joy created that the moment did not already own, no benefit you can name that survives honest looking.

That is the axis this whole book turns on. Not harm against benefit. Benefit or no benefit. And the benefit column is empty.

3. JUDGE ONLY BY WHAT IT DOES FOR YOU
Forget harm versus benefit and ask for the benefit.

SUMMARY
- Harm warnings never stopped the hand, so the question has changed from harm to benefit.
- An ordinary moderate Tuesday stacks dose on dose from morning to night and ends flat.
- Mood, teeth, money and time are all charged across the day with no gift to show.
- The till receipts for one careful day run to pounds, and to hundreds across a month, for hurry and wrappers.
- Eighty counted minutes a day go to thinking, queuing, unwrapping, hiding and brushing, and buy nothing the hour did not already own.
- The story that sweet hits help is a belief reading a brief quiet as kindness.
- Frequent free sugars turn plaque to acid, and normal intake sits far above guidance without feeling large.
- The benefit column, honestly counted, is empty.
