- `INSTRUCTION` — a card-assigned new instruction is not present verbatim

- `RESERVED-REACH` — the draft performs a later chapter's primary job. Name that later card and cut the overreach to at most one sentence.

Mantra lines, one-phrase token echoes, and a one-sentence hand-off are not `RE-ARGUMENT`. - `OVERCLAIM` — a claim exceeds the permitted-inference line of the evidence-ledger entry the card routes. Quote the overclaim and the bound in the review. Require the writer to delete the unsupported claim or narrow the sentence to what the evidence actually supports, rather than append commentary about the evidence, its scope, or what it can establish. The bound governs the repair; it is not text to transplant into the chapter. Retain any qualification needed for factual accuracy or reader safety, expressed directly in ordinary language; never remove a necessary limitation merely to sound certain, and never print the ledger ID or grade in prose.

Chapter 6
HUNGER, SATISFACTION AND REAL FOOD

IN THIS CHAPTER
Dawn market stalls, cold air and warm loaf, soup steam, orchard fruit, the clean plate pushed back

*Hunger answered by real food is the favourite itself — BAD SUGAR only ever stood in the way.*

THE GREY PLATE YOU FEAR

You have killed the fuel lie. You have watched the ten-minute buzz borrow from the next hour and leave fog behind it. And still, underneath, one cold fear sits quietly and waits.

Without sweets eating will be grey.

Say it plainly because it is there. Morning porridge without sugar on top. Tea without biscuits beside it. Dinner without dessert to look forward to. A birthday table with no bright box at the centre. Life stretching out as plain chewing, dutiful mouthfuls, joy removed. Who would want that? Who would walk toward that with a light heart?

I understand that fear. We all lived it. We called dessert love. We called the evening box comfort. We called chocolate reward. We told ourselves food without a sweet hit would be punishment food, prison food, cardboard days. The belief felt like common sense. Take away the sweet and take away the pleasure.

The fact is, that belief has it backward. BAD SUGAR never made eating joyful. BAD SUGAR made eating joyless and then sold back ten minutes of noise as joy.

Look at your own diary as an investigator. When you dosed through the day, did plain meals taste brighter or duller? When the tongue was coated from morning dosing, did lunch sing or sit heavy? When dinner was eaten on top of grazing, did the first mouthful land or slide past? When the evening box was emptied, did the mouth feel clean and grateful or sour and still talking?

You know. Coated tongue tastes less. Grazed belly welcomes less. Sour mouth enjoys less. The dosed week did not heighten food. It blunted it.

Think of theMonths when dosing ran hardest. Did you linger over bread? Did you close your eyes at soup? Did you chew slowly and put the fork down with a sigh? Or did you eat fast, half-tasted, standing, scrolling, planning the next dose while chewing this one? We did. We, good people who love food, ate like couriers — fast, distracted, already gone.

That is what a sweet-blunted palate does. It rushes the meal to reach the hit. It pours sweetness over hunger so hunger cannot speak. It leaves the belly full and the mouth still nagging. It turns dinner into a preamble and breakfast into rescue.

And then it points at the grey plate it created and says, you see, without me eating is grey.

Do you see the trick? The trap dulls taste, creates hurry, blocks satisfaction, and then claims credit for any brightness left.

Ask what remains when the interference stops. Ask it as a straight question with no poetry in it. If BAD SUGAR gave eating its joy, why do the heaviest dosers report the least pleasure from meals and the most picking between meals? If BAD SUGAR were the favourite, why must each dose be chased by another within the hour? A favourite satisfies. A trap nags.

There is a way out of this grey fear, and it is not a theory. It is a meal eaten hungry. Not imagined. Eaten. Noticed. Finished at satisfied. That one lived hour teaches more than ten pages of argument, and it points to the only escape that lasts — back into the body you already own.

MORNING AT THE STALLS

Come with me to an ordinary morning. No lecture. No rules pinned to the fridge. No paper plan telling you grams and times. Nothing but hunger and food.

It is eight o'clock. The market hall cold at the doors and warm inside. Air smells of earth and yeast and citrus peel. Crates stacked high. Greens dark with water still on them. Carrots with tops on. Apples in paper trays. A baker's table at the end with loaves split open to show the crumb.

You have not grazed since night. The belly is empty in a clean way. Not gnawing. Not urgent. A low hollow that welcomes thought of breakfast. Real hunger speaks low and waits. It does not shout brand names. It does not name a drawer. It says, food will be good.

You buy what looks alive. A bunch of greens. Roots. A small round loaf still warm from the oven, flour on the crust. Some ripe pears. Oil. Salt. Nothing exotic. Nothing virtuous. Nothing to photograph for praise. Shopping as hands and eyes, not arithmetic.

Home. The kitchen cool. You rinse leaves. You slice roots thick. You put a pot on. Water, salt, oil, heat. The loaf torn, not sliced — a rough end piece for now, crumb steaming. The pears set on the table for later, skins tight, smelling faintly of orchard.

While the pot simmers you drink water because thirst is there. You are not bargaining with thirst. No deal of the kind, I will drink this so I do not eat that. Thirst drunk for itself. Hunger kept for food.

Second bowl? Half. The hollow filling. Shoulders dropping. Breath slower. The loaf smaller by a third. Hands warm around the bowl. Talk in the head quiet because no negotiation runs alongside. No small voice counting, have I been good, have I been bad, may I, should I. Hunger asked. Food answered. Both understood.

Then the pause arrives. Not stuffed. Not buzzed. A clear stop signal. The spoon slows on its own. The bread stays in the hand uneaten for a minute while you listen. Belly says, enough is here. Mouth says, taste complete. You put the spoon down. You push the plate back an inch. Satisfied.

Notice that inch. That small push is the whole book in miniature. No force. No argument. The body closing the meal the way an eye closes when sleep comes. Satisfaction is not a decision. It is an arrival.

Later, a pear. Skin snapped. Juice down the chin. Flesh grainy and sweet in the way orchard sweetness is sweet — bound up with fibre and water and chew, arriving slowly, leaving clean. Eaten to the core. Hands rinsed. Mouth clean. No sour talk an hour later. No wrapper to hide. No second pear demanded by the first.

WHAT HUNGER ASKS AND WHAT SATISFACTION DOES

Hunger and satisfaction are not ideas. They are instruments. They ran you for years before dosing taught you to mistrust them, and they still run every night while you sleep without a single packet.

Hunger asks for food. Any plain food. Bread gladly. Soup gladly. Vegetables gladly. It grows slowly, waits patiently, and stops when fed. It does not sulk if the packet is absent. It does not demand a brand. It welcomes the plate in front of it.

The sweet echo asks for a dose. One kind only. Now. It arrives fast, speaks loudly, names packets and places, refuses bread, and returns louder after feeding. Give it bread and it sulks. Give it time and it thins. Give it a dose and it restarts the clock.

Learn the two accents and you will never confuse them again. Belly growl that welcomes bread is hunger. Head chatter that names a packet is echo. Mouth watering at soup steam is hunger. Mouth sour-talking an hour after sweetness is echo. Hands steady toward the table is hunger. Hands restless toward the cupboard after dinner is echo.

“Come on,” you say. “You make it sound simple. My hunger feels broken. I feel hungry all day and never satisfied.”

I hear you. We felt broken too. The fact is, hunger was not broken. Hunger was drowned. Day after day of flood teaches the body to expect flood-clear-flood, so signals arrive noisy and late. Remove flood for a day and the line clears. The body makes its own steady glucose from plain meals quietly, without flood, without crash, the way it did on playground afternoons and the way it does each night between dinner and breakfast. No packet needed in sleep. None needed awake once the noise stops.

I speak of body mechanics in plain terms, pattern not prescription. No menu follows. No times. No good-food sermon and no bad-food shame.

Watch satisfaction do its work when allowed. You eat hungry. You chew. Nerves in belly sense stretch. Gut messengers rise. Palate tires pleasantly of one taste and asks for stop. That pleasant tiredness is the close. Not will, not control. Biology finishing a sentence.

BAD SUGAR blocks that close. Sweet flood arrives fast, without chew, without bulk, without pause. Blood spikes, then clearing begins, then talk restarts before belly ever finished its sentence. So you can be full to the throat and still circling. Full belly, nagging mouth. Dinner eaten, cupboard calling. That is not failed satisfaction. That is interrupted satisfaction.

Eat the market soup hungry and watch close return. Spoon, chew, warmth, pause, less hurry, spoon slower, bread smaller pieces, breath out, spoon down. The meal ends because it ends. The kitchen stays shut because nothing reopens it. Thirty minutes later mouth clean. Sixty minutes later head clear. Two hours later steady. Three hours later hunger low and clean again, on time, like a train that runs.

Does bread eaten hungry taste dull? Does soup eaten hungry need a packet after it? Does a belly fed to satisfied keep shouting? You know from this morning. Bread tastes of wheat and fire. Soup needs nothing after it but a pear later. Satisfied goes quiet.

It was hunger you loved all along, not sweetness. That is the short verdict that ends this section. Hunger makes plain food sing. Satisfaction lets the song end.

FAVOURITE — SURELY A TRICK OF WORDS?

“I knew it. Favourite food. Favourite without sweets. That is a play on words. You mean I must call cabbage my favourite and pretend.”

I expected that objection because I raised it myself on my last night of dosing. Standing in my kitchen, box light in hand, I thought, favourite food without BAD SUGAR — clever phrase, empty plate. Some trick where joy means chewing joylessly and calling it joy.

Hear my answer flat. There is no trick. There is no catch. The pleasure is real.

Favourite does not mean most sugared. Favourite means most welcomed by hunger and most completely closed by satisfaction. By that honest measure, BAD SUGAR never qualified. It arrived welcomed by echo, refused by hunger, and never closed. How can the thing that never satisfies be called favourite?

Ask three questions and answer from lived hours, not slogans.

When did food taste brightest in your life — on dosed days when tongue was coated and belly grazed, or on hungry days after work or sea air or illness passed, when plain bread and soup tasted marvellous in a concrete way that needed no topping?

When did a meal feel most complete — after dinner plus the private hour of rustle and hide, mouth sour and head quarrelling, or after the market soup finished at satisfied, plate pushed back, kitchen shut, mouth clean?

And when did company feel warmest — when half your attention guarded the box and you heard half the dialogue, or when hands stayed on the table and eyes stayed on faces because nothing called from the cupboard?

You know. Hungry days. Closed meals. Eyes on faces. The brightness belonged to hunger, the completeness to satisfaction, the warmth to people and room. BAD SUGAR only ever sneaked a ride on those three and claimed the fare.

Isolate the variable the way any honest investigator would. Take the same table twice. Same people. Same lamp. Same soup and loaf and pears. Once with BAD SUGAR stacked on top — bottle on table, box waiting, grazing through talk. Once without — water, food, hunger, stop. Which evening do you remember for laughter? Which morning do you remember with a clean mouth? Which table made you linger because lingering was good, not because mouth kept nagging?

You know because you have lived both when travel or a bare cupboard forced the second kind upon you. The stacked evening felt bright for ten minutes and split for the rest. The plain evening felt whole throughout. One borrowed. One owned.

That is credit reassignment in one picture. Sun on market crates belongs to sun. Laughter at table belongs to people. Warmth of soup belongs to hunger met. Crust crack belongs to baker and fire. None belonged to the packet. The packet sat among real pleasures and took the bow.

So when I say favourite, I mean this loaf torn warm. This soup that asks for slow spoons. This pear that leaves juice on the chin and then lets go. Eaten hungry. Stopped at satisfied. Remembered cleanly. That is not wordplay. That is the definition hunger wrote before any advert taught you another.

Like the small child who drops a half-eaten peach and runs back to play.

Read that small picture once and keep it. Hunger met, pleasure taken, stop without debate, life resumed. No counting. No guilt. No encore demanded. That is the instrument returning to your hands.

BUT FRUIT IS SUGAR TOO?

“Fine. But fruit is sugar too. Bread turns to sugar. Everything is sugar. So nothing matters. I may as well dose.”

That line is the con talking in a clever coat, and it relies on blurring a line this book drew on page one and never moves.

When this book says BAD SUGAR, it means refined sugar plus added free sugars plus junk processed carbs and starchy snack-carbs eaten as sweet hits — drinks, confectionery, biscuits, cakes, desserts, sweetened cereals and grazing doses of the same. That is the quit target. There is no healthy level sought inside that line.

Natural sweetness inside whole fruit, vegetables and plain meals is not the target. It belongs on the table. It arrives bound with water, fibre, chew, bulk and time. It asks for teeth. It meets hunger. It allows stop. It leaves the mouth clean.

Do you feel the difference in lived time? A pear eaten hungry satisfies and closes. A bar eaten full nags and reopens. A bowl of soup carries for hours without buzz. A bottle lifts for minutes and bills for hours. One is food. One is a hit. The body knows which is which by the close.

“But it rewards me. Dessert is my treat when I deserve one. Celebrating with chocolate. Food is love.”

I hear the tenderness in that. We all tied sweetness to love because love was in the room when sweetness was served. Cinema lights dim, laughter, hand in hand, chocolate passed. Birthday candles, singing, faces lit. After a hard day, a kind hand offering something sweet and saying rest now.

Keep the love. Keep the room. Keep the rest. The love was real. The room was real. The rest was earned. BAD SUGAR only sat among them.

Did the film move you because of wrapper or because of story and shoulder against yours? Did the birthday warm you because of icing or because of voices singing your name? Did the hard day ease because of a bar or because someone saw you were tired and paused with you? You know. Story, voices, pause. The sweet rode along and took credit.

Enjoyment stays. Sweet taste stays in fruit, in milk, in bread crust, in roasted roots, in a meal cooked with care. Company stays. Celebration stays. What leaves is the hit that interrupted hunger and blocked stop. That is not forbidding joy. That is returning joy to its owners.

Ask what love asks of food. Does love ask for hurry, hide, sour mouth and quarrel? Or does love ask for sitting, tasting, lingering, eyes up? You know. Love asks for the table, not the rustle behind it.

EAT AND CLOSE THE PLATE

You arrived fearing grey. You have now lived colour — stalls, steam, torn loaf, pear juice, the inch the plate moves when satisfied arrives.

Hold that inch. It proves the body still knows. Hunger still calls cleanly when flood thins. Satisfaction still closes when chew and pause allow. Taste still brightens when tongue is not coated. Time still returns when no private hour stacks on dinner — minutes for talk, for walk, for hearing a programme fully, for sleep without cotton mouth.

I state this as settled fact from my own table and every honest diary shown to me. Plain meals eaten hungry carry. Sweet hits eaten full nag. The first ends. The second loops. One teaches stop. One teaches repeat.

There is a sentence I want you to carry as spoken speech, the way you would say it to a friend over soup. Eat what you want, when you are hungry, stop when you are satisfied, and never punish yourself again.

Hear how different that is from every diet week you ever lived. No forbidden-must-have rebound. No Monday restart. No arithmetic while work waits. No purse thinned in small slips. No bag heavy with wrappers to hide. Hunger decides start. Satisfaction decides stop. Punishment has no seat at this table.

Live it tonight in miniature so tomorrow owns it fully. Wait for low hollow, not head chatter. Cook plain food you like. Sit. Taste first spoon fully. Chew. Put fork down between mouthfuls once or twice to hear belly. When spoon slows on its own and bread waits uneaten in the hand, stop. Push back an inch. Let kitchen stay shut. Notice mouth clean an hour later. Notice head usable. That lived close is worth a shelf of advice.

You will feel the old translation try once — dip means need a hit. You will then live the new translation for one hour — dip means eyes need distance, lungs need air, legs need standing, attention needs slower pace. Give those true things. Watch dip thin without flood. One hour teaches the body is authority again, not by slogan, by afternoon.

The moment will come, perhaps tomorrow at three, perhaps at nine on the sofa, when a bright thought of a dose crosses. Do not argue. Do not bargain. Smile at how thin it sounds next to soup steam and pear juice. It arrives pre-labelled as old script. Let it pass like weather. Hands stay where they are. Mouth stays clean. The thought thins because nothing feeds it.

You are not white-knuckling toward a distant prize. You are eating and closing and living. The hands are yours. The head is clearing. The table is favourite again in the honest sense — welcomed, savoured, finished.

Let this land as a brilliant fact in the body, not as a hope in the head. Hunger met by real food satisfies. BAD SUGAR interrupted and called the interruption pleasure.
