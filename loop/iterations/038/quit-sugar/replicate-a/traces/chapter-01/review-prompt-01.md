You are a factory component, not a judge. You never see a reference book

word-budget line the orchestrator computed. Your job is to make the

3. The draft chapter
4. One line: `Delivered N words. Budget B.`

## Output

Start with exactly `ACCEPT` or `REVISE`.

Then at most 7 findings. Each finding is one of:

- `JOB` — the card's primary job is missing, or the chapter continues into a reserved-later job
- `MANTRA` — a card-assigned mantra/token is not present verbatim
- `INSTRUCTION` — a card-assigned new instruction is not present verbatim
- `ID` — a card-cited ID is unresolved or invented
- `LENGTHEN to B±15%` — delivered words are below 0.85 × B
- `SHORTEN to B±15%` — delivered words are above 1.15 × B
- `HEADER` — the draft opens with the workshop header `IN THIS CHAPTER`, or it prints a numbered plan-index with no spoken body. A numbered ALL-CAPS instruction plus one spoken rationale line is not `HEADER`.
- `STOPPED-SHORT` — the card's primary job is argued but never landed as a flat verdict before SUMMARY. The reader can still hold the entering belief. Quote the missing landing.
- `UNASSIGNED-REFRAIN` — a non-mantra phrase recurs ≥3× verbatim. Name the phrase and the count. Subtract repeats; do not invent a new mantra.
- `RESERVED-REACH` — the draft performs a later chapter's primary job. Name that later card and cut the overreach to at most one sentence.
- `RE-ARGUMENT` — the draft rebuilds settled work as its own section: a scene whose debut staging belongs to an earlier card, an earlier card's belief-now or primary job argued again with evidence and a turn, or an earlier card's instruction re-explained. Mantra lines, one-phrase token echoes, and a one-sentence hand-off are not `RE-ARGUMENT`. Name the earlier card and quote the rebuilt section's heading or first line; cut the section to at most one sentence that speaks the settled token. If that cut leaves the chapter below 0.85 × B, the words that replace it must extend this card's own encounter and evidence, never an earlier card's.
- `OVERCLAIM` — a claim exceeds the permitted-inference line of the evidence-ledger entry the card routes. Quote the overclaim and the bound in the review. Require the writer to delete the unsupported claim or narrow the sentence to what the evidence actually supports, rather than append commentary about the evidence, its scope, or what it can establish. The bound governs the repair; it is not text to transplant into the chapter. Retain any qualification needed for factual accuracy or reader safety, expressed directly in ordinary language; never remove a necessary limitation merely to sound certain, and never print the ledger ID or grade in prose.

No other finding types. No style notes. No "sounds like AI." No comparison
to any other book. No warmth, tone, or voice coaching.

Chapter 1
THE INVITATION

IN THIS CHAPTER
The kitchen cupboard late at night. A clear line around BAD SUGAR. The years I lived on sweet hits. Three small agreements for reading.

*This is not another diet lecture; this is a way to see sweetness clearly and walk out with nothing lost.*

WHY THIS BOOK IS IN YOUR HANDS

Let me ask you something plain.

If sweet food and sweet drink were truly a harmless pleasure that you choose calmly and enjoy fully, why would you be holding a book with this title?

You would not. No one who is calm and content about biscuits and cola picks up a book about quitting them. No one who is truly in charge of the evening packet and the morning pastry looks for help to stop.

I say that with warmth, not as an accusation. I have been where you are. We all tell ourselves the same story: I like it, I choose it, I could take it or leave it. And yet here we are, you with this book open, me writing it because I once needed it.

That fact tells us something important before we argue about anything. It tells us you already sense what this book will show you. A part of you already knows the sweet routine is not the easy pleasure it pretends to be. That part of you brought you here.

So let us be clear from the first page. You are not a pupil. You are not a patient. You are not weak. You are a reader who suspects the whole approach you have been given is wrong, and you are right to suspect it.

“I’ve managed to be good this week.” “I want to get off the roller coaster.” “I just need sugary somethings to get me through.” Do you hear your own voice in those lines? I do. I heard them in my own head for years. They are not the voice of a person enjoying a genuine pleasure. They are the voice of a person caught in a loop.

And here is the good news I want you to hold from the start. The fact that you are holding this book means the decision is already made. You do not need to convince yourself to want out. You already want out. What follows is only good news about how easy that can be.

You may be thinking: “Too good to be true? That was my reaction too.” I welcome that thought. Keep it beside you. I am not asking you to believe me yet. I am only asking you to read on and judge for yourself.

If you live with diabetes or pre-diabetes, take medication that affects blood sugar or appetite, are pregnant, live with an eating disorder past or present, or have any medical condition where changing what you eat could carry risk, talk to your clinician before changing anything and follow their advice first.

THIS IS NOT ANOTHER LECTURE ON FOOD

Is this another diet lecture? No. It is the opposite of one.

A diet lecture tells you what to eat and what not to eat, praises you when you obey and scolds you when you do not, and leaves you to fight your own hunger with rules. You have done that. I have done that. We know how it ends.

This book does none of that. I will not count your calories. I will not weigh your portions. I will not tell you to be miserable for six weeks and then call that misery success. I will not ask you to punish yourself at all.

Ask yourself: has any diet ever set you truly at rest around sweet food? Has any set of moderation rules — one on weekdays, none after six, only at weekends, only the small bar — ever left you calm? Or did each rule make the sweet thing loom larger, sound sweeter, call louder from the cupboard?

Be honest. When you obeyed the rule for three days, did you feel cured? Or did you feel you were holding your breath, waiting for the day the rule would break? And when the rule broke on the fourth evening, did you eat one neat portion like a person who is in charge? Or did the hand seem to move before the mind had decided, and the packet that was meant to last the week was suddenly light?

There is a quiet fraud in that late cupboard reach. Not a fraud by you. A fraud on you. You were told that reach was free choice. It did not feel like free choice. It felt like something pulling.

I am not going to scold you for that pull. There is nothing to scold. I want to show you where the pull comes from and why it has nothing to do with weakness or character.

So put aside all pictures of gloom and deprivation. Cast aside the idea that I am here to take something precious away from you and leave your days grey. That is the Willpower Method talking, and the Willpower Method has lied to you long enough.

My job is different. My job is to help you question what you think you know about sweetness, one clear look at a time, until you see it as it is. When you do, you will not need to fight it.

WHAT I MEAN WHEN I SAY SUGAR

We cannot talk clearly until we mean the same thing by the word sugar. In ordinary talk the word is a blur. It covers the apple in your lunchbox and the cola in the fridge and the white powder in the bakery sack, as if they were one thing. They are not.

When this book says sugar, take it to mean BAD SUGAR. That is my decree to you, and I will keep it on every page that follows.

> WHAT THIS BOOK MEANS BY BAD SUGAR

> BAD SUGAR means refined sugar and added free sugars and junk processed carbs and starchy snack-carbs eaten as sweet hits. Read that box twice. It matters.

“Fruit is sugar too so nothing matters.” Have you heard that line, perhaps in your own head? It is a confusion the blur creates. Once we draw the line, the confusion ends. We are not talking about food. We are talking about a narrow set of engineered doses that were built to hit fast and call you back.

Why draw the line so sharply? Because a trap cannot be seen until its edges are marked. While sugar means everything, you can defend anything. While it means apples and cola at once, every argument dissolves into “but we need energy to live.” Once it means BAD SUGAR, we can look at it straight.

I am not asking you to eat less. I am not asking you to eat differently yet. I am asking you to use words cleanly, so thought can be clean. From now on in these pages, sugar means BAD SUGAR. The apple on the table is food. The cola in the glass is a dose. Keep them apart and much becomes clear.

And notice what this definition already does for you. It takes eating itself off trial. Your hunger is not the enemy. Your appetite is not a flaw. Meals are not the problem. A tight cluster of sweet hits is the problem, and that cluster can be seen, named, and left behind while eating itself stays yours.

I KNOW HOW THIS SOUNDS

You do not know me yet, so let me tell you who is speaking to you.

I was not a moderate sweet eater who dabbled and then wrote a book. I was the person who said “just one” at ten at night and then stood in the kitchen light with crumbs on my shirt, angry and baffled. I was the person who bought the family bar and ate half of it in the car. I was the person who hid wrappers at the bottom of the bin so no one would count.

Morning after morning I promised a fresh start. “I’ll be good today.” By three in the afternoon the promise was thin. By nine it was gone. I blamed myself without mercy. If you have done the same, you know the particular shame of it: not the big dramatic shame, but the small daily shame of a grown adult outwitted by a biscuit tin.

I tried what you tried. I cut down on Mondays. I allowed treats on Fridays. I kept it out of the house and then walked to the shop in the rain to fetch it. I read labels. I swapped brands. I chewed gum. I drank water to fill the gap. Each plan worked for days. None worked for long. Each collapse felt like proof that I was the problem.

Here is what changed everything for me. I met a way of seeing that did not start with rules. It started with a question: what if the thing I called my treat was not treating me at all? What if the lift I thanked it for was part of the very low I blamed on my day? I did not accept that at first. I scoffed. I argued. Then I looked, dose by dose, day by day, until I could no longer defend the old story.

I stopped, not by a feat of character, but by a change of sight. One ordinary evening I had my last ordinary sweet, saw it plainly for what it was, and closed the packet with a strange lightness. I expected nights of torment. What came was quiet. The days that followed were not a test of endurance. They were ordinary days, made plainer and kinder because the tug was gone.

I tell you this not to boast. I tell you because I need you to know I am not speaking from a textbook. I am speaking as one who lived inside the loop and walked out of it without a battle. What happened for me was not exceptional. I have since watched it happen for others who were sure they were the hopeless cases.

“But you don’t know me,” you say. “Mine is worse. I eat when I am tired. I eat when I am kind to myself. I eat when everyone else is eating. I have failed too many times.”

I answer you directly: your case is not different in kind. The details differ — the desk drawer, the glove box, the checkout queue, the sofa at midnight — but the shape is one shape. We fell into the same hole through the same doorway, and the doorway was belief, not character.

Do you want to stop? Of course you do — that is why you are reading this book. And do you fear that stopping means months of misery? Of course you fear it — you were taught to fear it by every failed attempt that used the wrong method.

Let me put the alternative before you. What if stopping has nothing to do with character? What if it has to do with sight? What if a clear look could do what effort never did?

YOU RISK NOTHING BY READING ON

I know this is hard to accept, but hear me out.

You have been promised easy answers before. Pills, powders, thirty-day plans, apps that count your chews. Each promised a shortcut and delivered a new set of chains. Why should you trust one more voice that says this can be easy?

For one reason only: I am not asking you to trust me. I am asking you to test what I say against your own experience as we go. If I am wrong, you will catch me. If I am right, you will feel it happen in your own mind, not because I ordered it but because you saw it.

Consider what I am actually asking. I am not asking for money you cannot afford. I am not asking for weeks of hunger. I am not asking for a public vow or a signed pledge. I am asking for a few evenings of reading with an open mind.

Can you think of a safer bet? On one side, the chance — I say the certainty — of walking out from under a daily tug that costs you money, time, teeth, mood, and self-respect. On the other side, a few hours of reading. Weigh those pans. Which is heavier?

This is why I say to you with a steady voice that you have absolutely nothing to lose and everything to gain. That is not a slogan to me. It is arithmetic. You keep your cupboard as it is. You keep your routine as it is. You lose no pleasure while you read, because I will take no pleasure from you on these pages. You gain a chance to see the one thing that makes all the difference.

“But what if I read the whole book and nothing changes?” Then you are where you are now, minus a few hours. “What if I cannot stop even after I see it?” Then you will at least know what you are dealing with, and that knowledge itself lightens the load. “What if this is just another trick to make me feel guilty?” Then judge me by my tone. Have I shamed you once? I will not. Warmth to you; war on the trap. That is my rule on every page.

Let me ask you three questions, and answer them honestly in your own head.

Did you choose your first sweet hits as an adult with full knowledge of where they lead? Or were they put into your hand as love, as reward, as normal life, before you could weigh anything?

When you eat the third biscuit after promising yourself two, does that feel like a calm decision by a satisfied person? Or does it feel like a pull that arrives before thought?

And if BAD SUGAR truly gave you something real — true comfort, true fuel, true joy — would you need a book to help you stop taking it?

You know the answers. You knew them before you opened this cover. All I am doing is giving those quiet answers a clear voice.

Some readers worry: “If I admit I am trapped, does that make me weak?” It makes you honest, not weak. We were all drawn in by the same sweet talk: parents who soothed with sweets because they were soothed that way, shops that stack bright doses at a child’s eye level, adverts that tie chocolate to love and cola to fun. To be conned as a child and as a busy adult in a loud market is not weakness. It is ordinary life. Seeing the con now is strength.

HOW WE WILL READ TOGETHER

Every method has its way of working. Mine is simple. It works if you work with it in the way it was built to work.

First, read in order. Do not skip to the back to find the ending. A mystery read backwards is just a list of names. This book builds sight step by step. Each chapter lays ground for the next. Trust the order.

Second, read actively, not passively. When I ask a question, answer it. When I quote your inner voice, check whether I heard you right. You are not an audience to be impressed. You are an investigator with me. The conclusion must feel like yours, because it will be yours.

Third, do not judge a chapter by how it makes you feel in the moment. Some pages will please you. Some will unsettle you. That is proper. We are handling a lifetime of sweet talk. A little discomfort while old ideas loosen is not danger. It is the sign that sight is changing.

And now the heart of our agreement.

All you have to do is follow all the instructions. I say that plainly and without apology, because the instructions are small and the prize is large. They are not rules about food. They are rules about reading: how to hold your mind while we look together. Follow them all and the method can do its work. Pick among them and you blunt it.

One of those instructions concerns eating itself, and I want to plant it now so you never fear hunger from me. Eat what you want, when you are hungry, stop when you are satisfied, and never punish yourself again. Read that line again and let it settle. I mean it exactly as written. No starving. No skipping meals to make up for a binge. No good foods and bad foods in the moral sense. Hunger is your signal. Satisfaction is your stop sign. Punishment is over.

“But how can I stop eating too much sweet food if I eat what I want?” I hear the objection, and it is a fair one from inside the old frame. The answer is that want itself changes when sight changes. I am not telling you to control want with rules. I am showing you how want was misled, so it corrects itself. A person who sees clearly does not need to be policed.

You will notice I speak to you directly, and I speak of us together when I speak of the trap. That is deliberate. We fell for the same story, you and I. We stood at the same cupboard. We told the same “tomorrow” story. When I say we, I mean fellow travellers with no shame between us. When I say you, I mean the person I respect enough to give straight orders to. When I say I, I mean the witness who will not ask you to go anywhere I have not been.

Here then is our first order, and it is a small one. It is the doorway to all the rest.
