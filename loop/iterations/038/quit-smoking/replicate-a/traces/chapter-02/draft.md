Chapter 2
ARE YOU REALLY CHOOSING THIS

**IN THIS CHAPTER**
The cold garage and the spray can, the mint tin in the coat pocket, the hand that lights before thought.

*No chooser hides to enjoy himself — you were drawn into a trap that now lights for you.*

### THE MAN WHO INSISTS HE CHOOSES

I know that bristle.

"Nobody holds a cigarette to my head. I buy my own. I light my own. I enjoy my own. This is my choice."

Of course you say it. We all said it. I said it for years while my coat stank and my fingers were stained and my children stepped back from my kiss. We say it because the alternative sounds like an insult. If we did not choose this, then what are we?

Let me ask you as one smoker to another, with no lecture in it.

If you choose so freely, why do you need a book to stop? If you enjoy it so fully, why do you hate being a smoker? If no one forces you, why does your hand move before you have decided?

You will say, "I can stop when I want. I just do not want to yet." Every smoker says it. I said it on the stairs, in the rain, outside hospitals, outside my own house. It is what we say to keep our dignity while we do something we wish we did not have to do.

The fact is a chooser does not behave like this. A man who chooses a hobby does not apologise for it, hide it, pay to do it and then curse himself for paying. He does not stand in the cold to enjoy it. He does not spray his own jacket like a crime scene and chew mints until his tongue burns.

You are not weak. You were conned on false information, and the con removed the choice while leaving the story of choice in place.

That story is the first lock. We will pick it together.

### THE GARAGE AT MIDNIGHT

Picture your own version. Mine was a garage. Yours may be a balcony, a car with the window cracked, a back step in socks.

It is close to midnight. The house is quiet. Your partner is asleep. The kids are asleep. You told them you had cut down. You meant it when you said it.

The want comes, faint and nagging. You tell yourself to ignore it. You last eleven minutes. Then you are up, soft-footed in the hall, lifting your coat without rattling the hook, patting the pocket for pack and lighter, easing the back door so the click does not wake anyone.

The air bites. The bulb is too bright. You light, and for two or three seconds there is that sudden heaven — hot at the throat, sharp in the nose, the shoulders dropping as the first dose hits. You would swear in that second that you love smoking and would never be without that lighting.

Then it passes. It always passes. By halfway the taste is stale paper and ash. By the end you feel shitty, a little sick, a little sad, stubbing it and waving the smoke from your sleeves. You stand there fanning the door, spraying Febreeze the hell out of yourself, rolling a mint around your mouth, checking your fingers for smell. You slip back to bed and lie awake rehearsing that you hate being a smoker while still guarding the lighting you just did.

We live that split and call it normal. Whilst I love smoking I hate being a smoker — that exact ache. I am not mocking it. That heaven second is real as a sensation. It lasts as long as a match flame. It is not a day-long gift and it never was.

Ask yourself what choice needs all that theatre. Does a man who chooses a pleasure Febreeze himself after it? Does a free chooser whisper and hide from the people he loves most? Does enjoyment need an alibi?

I felt that shame too. I lied with a straight face. I aired rooms. I threw ends into bins two streets away. I was warm, honest man in every other room of my life, and a furtive stranger in this one room. The hiding did not mean I was bad. It meant the choosing story was false.

Choice would not need a garage at midnight.

### THE HAND THAT MOVES FIRST

Watch more closely tomorrow, not to change anything, only to see.

Alarm. Feet have not touched the floor before the hand finds the pack. Kettle on. Phone down after a row. Keys in the door after shopping. In each case the order is the same. Hand first. Reason after.

You tell yourself, "I need this to get going. I need this after stress. I deserve this after effort." But the movement came before the sentence. The sentence was written to cover the movement. If anything stressful happens in your life, a cigarette takes care of it — that is what we say, and in the minute we say it we feel a brief taken-care-of quiet and credit the dose. The feeling is genuine. The credit is false. The bill, the row, the deadline sit untouched where you left them.

Do you smoke to taste hot smoke? Then why menthol it, coffee it, mint it away afterwards? Do you smoke because you decided? Then why can you not name the moment of deciding, only the moment of reaching?

This is the nicotine trap in plain sight: a loop that creates a need and then sells you the same loop as the answer, until the hand obeys faster than thought.

I am not asking you to fight the hand. Fighting was the old method and it kept us trapped for years. I am asking you to notice that the hand is not you choosing. It is you obeying. Once you see the order — reach first, excuse second — the chooser story cannot stand.

And when that story falls, something odd happens. You do not feel smaller. You feel larger. A conned man who spots the con is not a fool. He is a witness. There is no doom in that seeing, only the first thin light of an escape you had stopped believing was yours to take.

### THE TUG AND THE STORY

There are two locks on this cage, and you have felt both tonight without having names for them.

The first is physical and absurdly small. A few hours without a dose and something nags low down — not pain, not illness, just an empty, slightly restless, slightly edgy little tug, as if something wants feeding. Feed it and the tug quiets for twenty minutes. Starve it and it complains for a little while, then dies. I call that tiny complainer the Nipper in my own mind, because it nips and begs like a hungry pup, and a pup cannot force a grown man to do anything.

The second lock is everything we were taught to say about that tug. That it means we need a break, a reward, courage, calm, company. That life without the dose would be flat. That we are that kind of person. That haze of taught phrases is the Smokescreen around the pup, and the Smokescreen is what keeps feeding it long after the body would have let go.

You do not need to starve anything today. You do not need to prove anything today. Just separate the two in your seeing. The tug is a small bodily echo. The story is a lifetime of brainwashing about what the echo means. The echo cannot chain a man to a garage at midnight. Only the story can make him call the chain a choice.

So when the next dose calls, watch it as a student, not as a defendant. There goes my hand. There goes the first hot heaven second. There goes the flat stale end. There goes the spray and the mint. There goes the voice saying, "I chose that." You will know, because you are now watching with clear eyes, that you are free to see even while you still smoke, and that seeing is already loosening the lock.

You were never foolish. You were fitted with a story and a tug and left to blame yourself for obeying both.

2. DON'T STOP OR CUT DOWN YET
Smoke as normal until your last cigarette.

**SUMMARY**
- Hiding to smoke proves the choosing story is false; enjoyment would not need a garage and a spray can.
- The heaven second at lighting is real and brief, then turns flat and shitty; it is not proof of a day-long benefit.
- Your hand moves before your reason and writes excuses afterwards; the reach is obedience, not decision.
- A small physical tug and a large taught story hold you together; the story names the tug as need and choice.
