Chapter 10
THE NIGHT THE PATIO PROVED IT

**IN THIS CHAPTER**
- Late patio, low bulbs, cold glasses and loud talk
- The outstretched hand with the lit offer
- The first draw set against night air
- A Friday watched to the last drink
- The same laugh with nothing burning

*The laugh and the drink and the night did all the giving — the stick only hitched a ride and took the credit.*

### THE LIGHT ON THE PATIO

You know this one by heart because we have all stood in it.

Late. Heaters ticking orange over the rail. Inside, the bar hums. Outside, the patio crowd leans in close, coats pulled tight, glasses sweating and clinking. Someone tells the story right. Laughter cracks open and rolls down the table. A lighter flares. Three orange tips bloom at once.

Then the hand turns to you. Two fingers holding out a lit cigarette, smoke curling up past the knuckles.

“Go on. One won’t hurt.”

And the old voice pipes up word for word:

“You don’t want to be the only one not smoking. Everyone’s lit. You’ve had a few drinks, so don’t think about it. One won’t hurt.”

I know that voice. I obeyed it for years on Fridays and Saturdays and birthdays and leaving dos. Not because I weighed anything, but because the night plus the drink plus the mates made the rule I lived by in daylight fold in half.

We call it the social one. The best one. The one that counts as pleasure instead of habit. The daylight ones we admit are need. This one we guard as belonging.

Look at it with clear eyes while we stand there together.

What is actually giving in that picture? Name it piece by piece.

The warmth on the skin from the heater. The cold bite of lager and lime. The tired looseness after a long week. The story told right. The shoulder bumping your shoulder. The hour with no clock in it. The yard air moving cool at the back of the throat when you laugh too hard.

Which of those came out of paper?

Did the laugh get louder because paper burned? Did the drink taste deeper for grey laid over it? Did your mates grow funnier because you stepped six feet away to feed a dose?

You know the honest answers. You have felt them a hundred times and pushed them aside because the hand was already moving.

The cigarette adds nothing to that table. It interrupts the table. Light, draw, cough caught in the throat, eyes watering in the smoke while the punchline lands. Step out to finish it. Step back smelling of ash to a conversation that moved on without you. Another coin burned. Another break cut into an unbroken hour.

The Smokescreen calls that belonging. It is the other way around. Belonging was the lean-in, the listen, the laugh at full lung. The tube pulled you half a step out of the circle and sold the step-out as the circle.

Ask from your own mouth on that patio:

If this one is pleasure, why does it need drink to excuse it? If this one is choice, why does not-taking feel like standing apart? If this one is yours, why is the hand offering it someone else’s?

The night does not need paper to be the night. Drink does not need smoke to be drink. Mates do not need ash to be mates. The stick was sneaking a ride on all three and charging you for the seat.

You do not need to dodge your mates. You do not need to dodge a drink to see this. Let the night stay exactly as it is and watch what the tube does inside it. That is the whole test.

### HEAVEN FOR A SECOND

“But the first draw — that first draw on a night out is heaven.”

I have said that sentence myself, word for word. Let us tell the truth about how long that heaven lasts.

Strike the light. First pull to the back of the throat. Brief flare, brief quiet of the tug, head swimming for two seconds while the shoulders drop. That flare is what the smoker calls heaven. I do not mock it. I felt it too.

Now stay with the same cigarette for sixty minutes and tell me where heaven went.

Second pull: throat drier. Third: tongue coated. By halfway the taste is hot paper and old ash. By the butt the mouth is sour, the fingers stink, the head aches faintly behind the eyes, the chest feels lined with something that should not be there. The drink that tasted bright now tastes dulled under grey. The laugh that rang now rasps.

Heaven for a second, grey for an hour. That is the full arc, every time, without exception in my twenty years of watching it.

Did that one deliver pleasure for the night? Or did it deliver a two-second flare while laying sourness over taste, tightness over chest, and a timetable over talk?

By midnight we were stale, breathless and chained outside while the talk went on inside.

Say that without shame, as a description of a rented state, not of you. Stale in a mouth built to taste salt and lemon and cold. Breath on stairs that should carry talk in full sentences. Chained to pocket-checks and doorway dates while the evening you paid to join happens six feet away.

The trap teaches you to remember the flare and forget the hour. It photographs the lighting and crops out the butt, the spit, the mint, the window opened in the taxi, the shirt left on the line overnight.

Take the whole photograph back.

What did the best one do to the drink? Dulled it. What did it do to the laugh? Broke it in half for the doorway. What did it do to belonging? Priced it at standing apart with fire between fingers.

That is not pleasure kept. That is pleasure taxed.

### THE FRIDAY I WATCHED TO THE END

Give me one room of your time for a plain story. No lesson in it but what happened.

I was thirty-four, seventeen years in, a pack a day on weekdays and more when drink was on the table. I loved the patio version of me. Daylight me coughed and hid packets in the van and sprayed the jacket. Night me felt sharp, sociable, lit and in on it. If you had asked me which cigarette I would keep if I could keep only one, I would have pointed to that one without thinking — cold glass in one hand, lit stick in the other, mates around.

One Friday in October the pub put heaters out late and we stayed. Eight of us round two pushed tables. I counted without meaning to count, the way you do. First with the second pint. Second when Paul told the ferry story. Third because everyone stood and I stood. Fourth with whisky. Fifth because the air bit and someone said last round and I thought a stick would hold the night longer.

At half eleven Sarah — non-smoker, no axe to grind — laughed so hard at Paul that beer came out of her nose and she had to wipe her eyes with her sleeve. The whole table went. Heaters ticking. Breath showing white. Glasses thumping. I watched her face. Open, wet-eyed, all in.

I had missed the middle of the story. I had been cupping a lighter in the wind.

At midnight the landlord called time for outside and we dragged chairs in. My mouth tasted like a tray. My fingertips were yellow-brown and tacky. My chest felt narrow, as if a strap had been pulled one hole tighter since nine. I put a mint in and it stung. I ordered water because lager now tasted of ash no matter what I washed it with. Paul told the end of the ferry bit again for the ones who missed it and I laughed a beat late because I had heard it half-lit, half-listening.

Sarah stayed bright to the end. Talked, listened, remembered names the barman used. She had drunk the same, stayed later than me without once checking a pocket, stepping out, timing anything. Nothing interrupted her. Nothing taxed her.

I walked home with two mates, stopping once in a doorway for what I told myself was one to finish the night. Wind cut down the street. One mate waited, stamping feet, while I burned it to the filter. The other walked on. The stick went down half-tasted because my lips were numb. I dropped it, ground it, and tasted sourness all the way to my gate.

In bed I did the maths I had avoided for years. Five on the patio at maybe four minutes of actual drawing each. Twenty minutes of paper and fire. Three hours of dulled drink, broken talk, sour mouth, tight chest, yellow fingers, one mate walking on without me, one punchline missed in the wind.

I had called that my best. My keeper. My proof that some are worth it.

Lying there I asked what it had added to any piece I named as good. To Paul’s story? Nothing; I missed the middle. To Sarah’s laugh? Nothing; I watched it while lighting. To the lager? It coated it. To belonging? I left the circle five times to stand apart.

The night had been warm and loud and human. The stick had ridden every minute of it and paid for none of it.

I did not decide anything that night. I only saw, for the first time with both eyes open, that the flare I called heaven lived in the first two seconds and the tax lived in the other three hours. I had been photographing the match-strike and calling it the evening.

### DRINK, LAUGH, NIGHT AIR — WITHOUT THE CUT

Come back to that patio with me and run the same hour with nothing burning, because this is where the proof lands in the body, not in words.

Same table. Same low bulbs. Same cold glass sweating under your thumb. Same story told right. Only now both hands stay in play. Glass, sleeve, chair-back. No cupping, no shielding, no half-ear on the punchline while the other ear listens for the wind.

Breathe the yard in full when you laugh. Cold to the bottom, then warm talk straight back out. Taste the lager from first cold sip to last, lemon still bright at the end, no grey laid across it. Hear the whole story, beginning to end, and laugh on time with the table instead of a beat late through smoke.

Watch the offer come and go. The hand extends. “Go on.” You shake your head without a speech, without a gap in listening. The hand withdraws. The circle does not close against you. Nobody tallies. Nobody mourns. Two minutes later the talk has buried the moment and your glass is still cold and your mouth still tastes of what you drank.

It does plenty TO you. It does nothing FOR you.

Read that on the patio itself and it stops being a sentence. The TO is the sour mouth, the missed middle, the doorway in the wind, the yellow on the fingers, the strap one hole tighter. The FOR is an empty chair where the proof was supposed to sit. Sun, leisure, company did the giving. The dose only named the giving as its own.

THE NIGHT GAVE. THE STICK TOOK.

That is the whole ledger of the best one, seen head-on instead of through drink.

Did the night need paper to hold it longer? You held it longer without the five step-outs. Did drink need smoke to taste sharper? It tasted sharper without grey. Did mates need you lit to keep you in? You stayed in without once leaving to feed the Nipper.

You walk back inside with the night still on you and no sour film over it, glad to be free of the doorway dash while the talk goes on uncut.

That is not will against want. That is sight against con. The con said the stick made the night. Sight says the night made itself and the stick taxed it. Once seen, the best one cannot be unseen as best. It stands revealed as the costliest one, because it stole from the hour you loved most and called the theft pleasure.

10. MEET THE BEST CIGARETTE HEAD-ON
Let the favourite prove it gives nothing.

**SUMMARY**
- The late patio pleasure came from heaters, cold drink, loud talk and close shoulders, not from paper.
- The lit offer folds the daylight rule because drink and company are used as cover, not because the dose turns good at night.
- The flare called heaven lives in the first seconds of lighting, then sour mouth, tight chest and broken talk own the next hour.
- A full Friday watched to the end shows the stick interrupting stories, dulling drink and pulling feet to doorways while non-smokers stay inside the evening.
- The same night lived with nothing burning keeps taste bright, laugh on time and belonging uncut from first sip to last chair stacked.
