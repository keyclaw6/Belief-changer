Chapter 5
THE LITTLE PARTIES THAT NEVER WERE

*The party was your finished work, not the puff that followed it — and the mild never made it lighter, only made you pull harder.*

### THE PELLETS AFTER EVERYTHING

We all said it in the same words.

"Cigarettes were my reward for… well, almost everything. Teaching a class. Finishing a story. Finishing a paragraph. Driving 500 miles. Driving to the grocery store."

I know that chain from the inside. I lived pellet to pellet. The bell goes. The door shuts. The chalk still on the fingers. Out to the corridor, tap and light, as if the lesson could not be called done until the stick said so. The paragraph lands, the last sentence clicks, shoulders drop — and before the satisfaction has time to settle, the hand is already paying for it with a light. The car rolls into the drive, bags in the boot, engine off — and the errand is not closed until the puff closes it.

I am not mocking that minute. The pause after a finished thing feels warm and earned. I felt it a thousand times, and I signed the warmth over to the tube exactly as you do.

Watch what actually finished the thing.

Did the class teach itself while you stood outside? Did the paragraph write itself while the ash grew? Did the 500 miles drive themselves while you held the wheel with two fingers and worried the ash out of the window? You taught. You wrote. You drove. The hands shook a little less after because a job done settles any human chest — boy, clerk, mother, driver, long before any packet.

We reversed the order. The work gave the glow. The dose arrived a minute later and took the bow.

Take the teaching afternoon you know best. The room empties. Chairs scrape. One boy stays to say thanks and then he is gone. There is that bright tiredness teachers know — voice hoarse, board wiped, plan done. You step out. Light. First drag. You stare at the yard and tell yourself the smoke is rounding it off.

Now separate the threads, as a witness.

Thread one is finishing. Lungs let go because effort let go. Jaw unclenches because talking stopped. Eyes rest because watching thirty faces stopped. That loosening belongs to done. A non-smoker leans on the same doorframe with folded arms and feels the same bright tiredness without a flare.

Thread two is the dose laid over it. Tap out. Light. Hot scratch. Nose sting. Eyes watering a fraction. One hand busy holding and tapping. Mind leaving the bright tiredness to count draws, watch ash, think about smell before the next class, think about how many left for the evening. Tongue dulling. Mouth drying.

Which part glowed? The done. Which part interrupted the glow to run small errands? The tube.

Ask yourself with kind eyes, as I asked myself after my own finished pages.

If the stick made the paragraph good, why did the good sentence arrive before the match, never during the puffing? If the stick closed the drive, why do you remember the road and forget the puffs except for the burn in the throat? If the stick was the party, why did the party feeling fade the instant the stub went out and leave a stale mouth where the glow had been?

You know the honest answers. The party was work. The puff only rented the room the party was in.

It does plenty TO you. It does nothing FOR you.

Take the paragraph you know. Late morning. The screen white. The kettle cold. You wrestle a sentence for twenty minutes, turn it, cut a word, read it aloud under your breath, and then it clicks. There is a small lift under the ribs. Anyone who makes anything knows that lift. The carpenter knows it when the joint shuts true. The cook knows it when the sauce holds. The driver knows it when the lorry backs square into the bay first time. It does not need smoke. It lived in you when you were ten and finished a drawing and held it to the light.

What did we do with that lift? We would not let it stand alone. Click. Lift. Hand to pocket. Tap. Flare. The lift had perhaps ninety seconds of natural life — look, breathe, re-read, smile — and we cut it at ten seconds to attend to lighting. Then we spent the other eighty seconds holding, drawing, coughing a little, tapping, watching the ash lengthen, thinking about the packet count, thinking about the smell on the jacket. Then stub. Stale. Back to the keys with a duller tongue.

Did the lift get bigger for being smoked over? Or did it get cut short for being smoked over?

You have done both in your life, though you may not have named it. There were days with no chance to step out — an exam hall, a train with no smoking, a house with a new baby — when you finished a thing and could not pay it. What happened? The lift stayed. You re-read. You smiled. You told someone. You carried the bright tiredness home intact. No flare. No ash. No count. The party did not cancel for lack of a tube. It ran fuller because no one barged in to take the bow.

And there were the other days, pellet to pellet, when every small done had its toll. Taught. Lit. Wrote. Lit. Drove. Lit. Shopped. Lit. By three o'clock the tongue was coated and the throat faintly raw and the head a fraction dulled, and the next done felt a little less bright, not because the work got worse but because the taster got worse. Would you praise a guest who dulled your palate before dinner and then asked to be thanked for dinner?

We were conned, not foolish — you and I both — by a timing trick. Done brings a natural settling. The last dose fading brings a natural tug at about the same minute. The brainwashing we were fed pasted one label over both and pointed both to the pocket. Light. Tug quiets for minutes. Settling gets signed over to the light. Next tug arrives on the same clock and asks for the same signing. Pellet to pellet to pellet. Not a chain of parties. A chain of signings.

Watch the 500 miles you know. Early start. Cold wheel. First hundred easy. Second hundred the back aches. You stop for fuel, stamp to wake the legs, light while the pump runs, tell yourself the smoke is the rest. Is it? The rest is legs moving, eyes off the white line, shoulders dropping, cold air on the face. The puff adds throat heat and hand business and ash to watch in wind and a count for the road ahead. Then back in. Same road. Same ache in an hour, plus a drier mouth. The miles were driven by you watching and steering and staying awake. The tube rode along and charged in cough and coin.

Or the grocery run. List in pocket. Aisles bright. Trolley with one bad wheel. You choose, you lift, you queue, you pay, you carry. Bags on the step. Door kicked shut. Kettle on. There is a small, decent pride in stocked shelves — animal, old, human. No packet needed. What did we add? Step out. Light. Tell ourselves this is the taste of a job well done while the tea cools inside and the tongue coats before the first sip. Did the shelves fill fuller for the puff? Did the tea taste sharper? You know. Duller. Later. Staler.

That is the whole pellet con in one picture. A human does a human thing well. Nature pays with a settling. The tube steps in front of the pay desk and takes the coin and leaves ash.

### TASTE IT WITHOUT

"But I like the taste. The pause. The little break."

I hear you. We all said it. Let us test it the way we test anything we claim to like — by looking at what we do with it.

Do you light the first inch for flavour and then lay it down to admire? No. You puff it down to the filter, chase the last third even when it turns hot and sorry, stub it halfway only because the end tastes of paper and ash. Is that how we treat a taste we love? We sip good soup slowly. We keep it. We do not hurry it before it burns down and then cough and then want another identical taste forty minutes later.

Taste tomorrow's finished thing without the overlay. Only notice. Finish the paragraph. Hands off the keys. Sit back. Let the eyes rest on what you made. Breathe through the nose. Drink water. Feel the bright tiredness settle without a flare laid over it. That settled minute is the party you thought the tube gave. It was always yours. The tube only stood in front of it and charged admission.

It never added flavour. It dulled the tongue and then took credit for the hunger returning.

I want you to look close at that taste claim, because it was my last kindness to the habit too. "They were my little parties." "I love the first one with coffee." "I love the one after dinner." We speak of flavour as if we were connoisseurs. Were we?

A connoisseur keeps the thing still to study it. He turns the glass. He holds the sip. Did we ever hold a puff still? Did we ever lay the stick down to admire the second inch? Did we ever finish a dose and say, "No more — that flavour will carry me till evening"? No. We hurried it. We chased it down while it burned. We coughed and kept puffing through the cough. We smoked it in rain under a hand, in wind with eyes watering, in cold with fingers numb, tasting mostly wind and cold and hurry. We smoked it with a blocked nose. We smoked it with a sore throat. Is that tasting? Or is that feeding?

Ask the honest questions and let them answer themselves.

If it was flavour, why did we always want the same flavour again within the hour, while no soup or song or sunset asks to be repeated on that clock? If it was flavour, why did the flavour always fade by the third puff to paper and heat, yet we kept puffing to the filter? If it was flavour, why did food and drink taste blunter after, not sharper — tea of ash, dinner of ash, morning mouth of ash?

You know because your tongue knows. The first hot scratch is not flavour. It is signal — the hit arriving. The brain, taught for years, reads that signal as pleasure the way a bell-trained animal reads a bell as food. The mouth coats. The nose dulls. Then, when the signal fades, the mouth clears a fraction and normal hunger and taste return, and we thank the next signal for the return.

It is back to front. The tube takes taste away and sells it back in sips.

Look at the pause claim the same way. "I love the break." I loved the break too. Who does not love stopping a good ache? The teacher loves the corridor after noise. The writer loves hands off the keys after grip. The driver loves legs out after clutch. The break is real. Was the tube the break?

Separate them again with kind eyes.

A break is stopping, air, still hands, eyes resting, nothing to hold. A dose is holding, lighting, drawing, tapping, watching, counting, smelling, stubbing. One is empty hands. The other is full hands. We called full hands a break from full hands. Would you call picking up a spade a break from digging?

Watch your own hands tomorrow. Finish the paragraph. Push back. Hands empty. Eyes on the page. Breath low. That is a break. Now watch the other version. Finish the paragraph. Tap out. Light. One hand taken. Eyes on ash. Breath around smoke. Mind on count. That is not a break. That is a second job laid over the rest, with its own tools and litter and arithmetic.

And the little pause between puffs — that soft stare out of the window while the stick burns down — whose pause is that? Any tired human stares out of the window. The clerk stares. The child stares. The non-smoker at the next desk stares longer, because no ash hurries him. We stared in snatches between draws, hurried by the burning inch, and called the snatches peace.

I stood in that corridor a thousand times. Chalk on fingers. Yard noise. Stick up. I told myself I was resting my voice. Was I? The voice rests when the throat rests. The throat does not rest around hot paper. My eyes were on ash and count. I returned to the room less rested, with a drier throat for talking, and told myself the corridor had helped.

It had helped the way a stone in the shoe helps a walk — by giving you something else to feel for a minute.

You will hear the old voice: "But the first drag is heaven." I know that heaven sentence. "The moment I light up I'm in heaven, but feel shitty afterwards." That heaven is a second long — heat, signal, tug quieting — and then paper and business. Would you call a whole evening heaven for one warm second at the door, followed by hours of washing up you were not allowed to leave? We did. We named the second and forgot the hour.

A party that leaves the tongue coated, the throat drier, the hands smelling, the packet thinner, the next hour already hungry, is not a party. It is a fee taken at the door of every done thing.

### THE HOLE IN THE MASK

And the milds. The lights. The smooth ones with the pale pack and the promise in the name.

We believed them because we wanted to believe there was a softer way to stay inside. I believed them too. I held that pale stick like a sensible compromise.

Hold one to the light tomorrow. Look close at the filter. See the ring of tiny vent holes punched around the paper, neat as pinholes. On the factory machine, air rushes in through those holes and the machine reads lower numbers. Clean. Neat. Lower.

You are not a machine.

The fact is plain, and I state it as fact. Those ventilated sticks do not mean the smoker took less. Lips close over the holes. Fingers close over the holes without knowing. The chest pulls harder to find the hit it was taught to expect. Not puff-for-puff identical for every smoker — we all hold and pull our own way — but human mouths do what brass mouths never do. They cover and compensate.

Picture what that is. A mask with holes drilled to let clean air in, sold as easier breathing. Only the wearer, without meaning to, presses his lips and fingers over the holes and sucks harder through the cloth to get the same breath. Would you call that easier breathing? Would you thank the maker for the holes?

We did. We paid the same, puffed harder, and called harder lighter.

Did the mild ever leave you wanting fewer, or did the day hold the same count with deeper pulls and more checking? Did one mild ever close the want, or did the next want arrive on the same clock?

You have lived the answers. The want arrived on the same clock because the want was the same need. The pale pack never touched the need. It only taught the hands a new grip and the chest a bigger pull, and then took credit for gentleness.

That is not a kinder dose. It is the same dose with a mask that leaks the moment a human holds it. The industry that punched the holes counts the doses by the day-box, not by the flavour word. If you feel ill or take prescribed medicine, see your clinician for that care — this book settles what the puff gives, nothing else.

Once seen, the mild cannot be believed again in the same way. The hand still covers. The chest still pulls. But you know the pull is covering, not tasting, and the covering proves the need was never about taste at all.

Look closer at that grip, because your fingers know it better than any diagram. Thumb and first finger pinch the filter exactly over the ring. Not below. Not above. Over. You did not decide that. The pull decided it. The chest wants its accustomed hit, the holes thin the smoke, the lips seal to thicken it again, the fingers seal what the lips miss. Grip finds holes the way a tongue finds a sore tooth.

And the pull. With the old dark stick the pull was short and sharp and the throat said enough. With the pale one the pull goes longer, deeper, held a fraction at the top to let the thinned smoke count. Cheeks hollow a little more. Shoulders lift a little higher. Then you tell yourself smoother means less. Would you drink a watered pint in bigger gulps and call yourself more sober?

I did that for months. Pale pack. Neat holes. Sensible man. I counted the day-box at night and the number had not moved. Nineteen. Twenty. Twenty-one on a long teaching day. Same clock. Same evening run to the shop. Same morning cough to clear. The only thing lighter was the word on the pack. The chest worked harder for the same quieting, the fingers learned a tighter seal, the mind learned a new sentence — "at least these are mild" — to defend the same chain.

Ask yourself as a witness, not as a judge.

If mild meant less, why did less never feel like enough? Why did one pale stick never close an afternoon the way one meal closes hunger? Why did the hand reach on the same minute, the packet thin on the same hour? Why did you sometimes smoke the pale one faster, harder, down closer to the filter?

Because the need was not for flavour or smoothness or a word on cardboard. The need was for the hit the chest had been taught to expect. Thin the smoke and the chest pulls deeper. Measure the machine and the machine smiles. Measure the human and the human compensates — not all of us in the identical millimetre, we all hold and pull our own way, but all of us in the same direction: toward the accustomed quieting.

That direction is the proof. A taste would be satisfied by less taste. A need pulls until fed. The pale pack proved, puff by puff, that we were feeding, not tasting. Each deeper pull was the body saying what the mind would not: it was never about mild. It was about the dose.

And who punched the holes? Not your lungs. Not your taste. A factory that counts the day-box. Warm to yourself. Harsh to the drill.

Hold that pale filter to the light once and you cannot unsee it. Neat holes. Human seal. Bigger pull. Same clock. Same count. Same stale. The gentleness was in the word, not in the chest.

### WHAT THE TOLL TOOK ALL DAY

Teaching, sentence, drive, bags on the step — each bright minute had a toll booth put across it. Remove the booth and the road does not vanish. The road runs clear.

Start at half past eight. Classroom door. Bell still ringing in the ears.

You teach. Forty minutes of talking, watching, coaxing. When the chairs scrape and the room empties, there is that teacher's bright tiredness — done, for an hour. The body wants ninety seconds: lean on the frame, arms folded, eyes on the yard, jaw unclenching.

Watch what the booth charges for letting that ninety seconds through.

Hand to pocket. Tap out. Flare. First hot scratch while the jaw still wants to unclench. One hand now taken for holding. Mind leaves the boy who said thanks and attends to small errands: draw, tap over the bin, watch ash in draught, think about smell before next class, think about how many left for the evening. Tongue, clean from tea at eight, coats by half past nine. Throat, hoarse from honest talking, dries a fraction more. Four minutes gone.

Price that booth. Holding: one hand taken for four minutes that wanted to be empty. Tapping: eyes on ash that wanted to be on yard. Counting: number for evening laid over thanks from a boy. Tongue-dulling: tea-clean mouth coated before ten. Same-clock wanting: tug quieted till ten, guaranteed to whine again by eleven.

Now eleven. Paragraph. Screen white.

You wrestle. False start. Delete. Second try holds. Last sentence clicks. That small lift under the ribs arrives. The body wants sixty seconds: sit back, hands off, re-read aloud, smile.

Watch the second booth.

Click. Lift. Hand moves before smile finishes. The lift, which had sixty seconds of natural life, gets ten seconds before lighting barges in. Then fifty seconds of holding, drawing, tapping into the mug, thinking about count — nineteen left, eighteen after this — thinking about next paragraph before this one has been tasted. Tongue coats thicker. Head goes a fraction cottony. You stub halfway because the last third tastes of paper. The sentence you made is still good — you made it before the match — but the tasting of it is gone.

Price that booth. Holding: hands that wanted to rest now holding and tapping. Tapping: eyes on ash that wanted to be on your own sentence. Counting: evening arithmetic laid over maker's lift. Tongue-dulling: clean sip now ash sip. Same-clock wanting: quiet till twelve, whine again by one.

Now one. Drive and shops, the grocery run that closes the morning.

List in pocket. Choose, lift, queue, pay, carry. Bags in boot. Engine off on the step. Kettle thought. There is that decent pride — stocked shelves, carried weight. The body wants two minutes: bags down, hands empty, back straightening.

Watch the third booth.

Bags down. Hand up. Flare on the step. First puff sharp. Second duller. Third paper. While the kettle boils inside, you stand outside with one hand taken, eyes on ash in wind, mind on count — how many for the afternoon, will this last till night? — tongue coating thicker, nose dulling so the tea tastes faintly of ash. Four minutes gone. When you come in, the filler is staler: dry mouth, ash on fingers to wash before touching food, one fewer again.

Add the three booths. Not as slogan. As day detail.

Holding across the day: perhaps fourteen minutes with one hand taken that wanted to be empty — doorframe, keys, wheel, bags. Tapping: eyes on ash, on burn-down, on wind, instead of on yard, on sentence, on home. Counting: morning box full, twenty. After teaching, nineteen and the mind notes. After paragraph, eighteen and the mind sums. After shops, seventeen and the mind forecasts evening. By two the box holds seventeen and the mind holds a second errand — will it last, where to buy — that was never in the teaching or the writing.

Tongue-dulling: tea-clean at eight, coated by nine, thicker by eleven, blunted by one. Same-clock wanting: quiet till ten, whine by eleven. Quiet till twelve, whine by one. Quiet till two, whine by three. Not a chain of parties. A clock.

Now ask the flat-day fear straight, with the day priced.

"You will feel flat without the little parties." Flat compared to what? To a day with three coatings, three counts, three hurries, three stales, fourteen minutes of holding, a blunted tea, a hurried sentence, a drier throat? That flatness was not the loss of parties. That flatness was the tolling. Remove the booths and what is left is teaching tasted, sentence re-read, shelves filled, tongue clean, hands empty, eyes on yard and page and home, no sums for evening.

I know this is hard to accept at first, because the booths stood so long we mistook them for the road. But walk the priced day once as a witness and the fear turns.

Which part of the teaching morning felt flat — the boy saying thanks, or the four minutes of ash-watch and count that cut the thanks short? Which part of the paragraph felt flat — the click, or the fifty seconds of tapping and dulling that cut the click short? Which part of the shops felt flat — the stocked step, or the cold tea while you stood outside to coat the tongue before tasting?

You know. The done never felt flat. The paying felt flat, and then the paying was praised as the party that saved the done from flatness.

It is back to front, pellet by pellet.

Picture the same three dones with no booths, not as a new method but as priced subtraction. Bell. Room empties. Lean. Arms folded. Eyes on yard. Ninety seconds. Boy's thanks stays whole. No ash. No count. Paragraph clicks. Sit back. Hands off. Re-read. Smile. Sixty seconds. No tapping over keys. Sentence tasted. Shops done. Bags down. Stretch. Kettle poured hot. Tea clean.

Is that flat? Or is that the bright tiredness you have always known, only uncut?

Carry that taking into tomorrow. Not as a vow. As eyes. Teach. Notice the hand move and the thanks thin. Write. Notice the smile cut at ten seconds. Shop. Notice the tea blunt. Notice holding, tapping, counting, dulling, clock. Once seen, the next pellet no longer reads as a party to keep. It reads as a booth to pass — and the road beyond it runs as it always did when you were a boy and finished a drawing and held it to the light with clean hands.

### NO OTHER VOICE GETS A VOTE NOW

I know this is hard to accept at first. The little parties were the last kindness we allowed the habit. To see them go feels, for a minute, as if the day will be flat.

It will not be flat. We have priced the flat. The flat was fourteen minutes of holding, eyes on ash, sums for evening, tongue coated by one, want back by three. Remove the booth and the teaching, the sentence, the drive, the bags stay. They were always yours. The tube only rented the rooms they were in.

You will hear other voices as we walk. A mate with a tip. A packet with a new name. A memory that says a certain break needs a certain stick. A headline that says cut down, switch, soften. Hear them and do not borrow them. You are inside an argument that has not finished yet, and a half-heard tip now only rebuilds what we have just pulled down.

Let this argument finish before you borrow another.

**SUMMARY**
- The glow after teaching, writing, driving and errands belonged to finishing, not to lighting.
- The taste you credited was dulled by the puff that claimed it, and the break was crowded by holding, tapping and counting.
- The mild with vent holes did not mean less was taken, because lips and fingers cover the holes and the chest pulls harder.
- A day of pellets paid for every done thing with coated tongue, busier hands, evening sums and want back on the same clock.
- The flat fear belonged to the tolling, not to the loss of parties, and the road runs clear when the booths are passed.
