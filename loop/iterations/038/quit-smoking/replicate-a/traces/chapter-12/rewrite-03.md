Chapter 12
YOUR LAST CIGARETTE

*Smoke this last one with your eyes open and you walk from the table already a non-smoker — nothing kept, nothing missed.*

### ARE YOU READY AT THE GATE

You have cut every string.

The desk hour turned out to be tug-quieting only. The little parties turned out to be work and company with paper laid over them. The best night-out hour turned out to be night and drink and friends all along. The careful one and the kind tomorrow turned out to be the bottle kept built.

There is no careful dose left to defend and no kind date left to wait for.

So I ask you plainly, as I ask every smoker at this gate: do you feel ready? You should be champing at the bit to be done with it. Not braced. Not gloomy. Eager to put the last dose out and get on with the evening without it.

That eagerness matters. A vow made from dread does not hold because dread still believes something is being lost.

If you do not feel it, do not force it. Put this chapter down. Re-read the night hour and the lamp until your answers come clean again. Come back when the lightness is there.

If you do feel it — a clear, solemn lightness, a wish to finish — then stay. This is the moment of parting you have prepared for since the first page.

I have stood at this table myself after twenty failed tries with drawers full of gum and patches. I know the flutter in the chest that says what if I am the one it does not work for. Listen to me: Remember, you have nothing to lose and everything to gain.

We were all conned the same way. Bright packs. Laughing posters. Mates offering. Thousands of doses teaching the tug to call itself pleasure. That is not your fault. It was a con laid for a child who did not know what the first puff was buying.

Ask yourself in your own voice before we sit down:

If the last dose truly helped, why did the thousand before it leave you still reaching? If lighting truly belonged to you, why did your hand move before you decided? If life without paper truly left a hole, what hole did you feel as a child before the first one?

You know the honest answers because your own evenings gave them. Nothing was given. The hand was conned. No hole waits. You leave it behind easily, immediately and permanently.

There is one more question to ask at the gate, and it is the only one that counts now. Do you see that the next dose would not add anything you have not already tested a thousand times? You have smoked when calm and when wild, when bored and when busy, when alone and when crowded, after food and before sleep, in rain and in sun. Which one of those thousand told the truth differently? None did. The article is known. The test is over. What remains is not another experiment but an ending carried out with open eyes and a quiet chest.

I do not ask you to screw yourself up to some pitch of courage. Courage is for facing a loss. There is no loss here to face. I ask you to feel the plain fact that the search is finished. You came to find out what a dose gives. You found out. Stain. Ash. Sourness. A bill paid for nothing. Once that is seen, the gate is not a cliff to jump from. It is a door already open with the noise stopped on the other side.

### THE FAREWELL TABLE

Do not make this last one special. Make it ordinary and make it awake.

Sit where you usually sit. No drink bought to christen it. No last supper lighting. No chain laid out to test yourself. One ordinary dose from the pack, smoked as normal to its end, with full attention on what is actually there.

We have always smoked half-asleep, eyes on talk or work while the hand did the labour. This time keep your eyes on the stick.

Look at your fingers as you hold it. See the faint yellow at the nail edge that soap never quite lifts. Smell the paper warming. Pull, and notice the first heat rasp the throat you have taught yourself not to notice. Taste the grey lay itself over the tongue. Watch the ash lengthen, pale and dead, and tap it off. Watch the smoke curl and thin and leave nothing in the hand but smell.

Halfway down, pause. Let the mouth speak. Sour at the back. Dry on the lips. Chest a fraction tighter. Is there pleasure here, or only the quieting of edginess that the previous dose planted? Is the talk brighter, or only interrupted? Does the body say thank you, or does it cough to clear an insult?

Smoke to the stale end, where we always used to stub or pinch and light another to cover the taste. That stale end is the truth of every dose you ever called heaven at lighting. The lighting flash lasted seconds. The tray taste lasts hours.

Notice the filter going soft and brown at the lip. Notice the paper blistering near the band. Notice how the last two pulls burn hotter and taste fouler, and how the old habit was to call that foulness a signal for another rather than what it is — the article exposing itself.

Now stub it. Grind it until no ember lives. Look at the crushed butt, the grey smear, the smell rising off the tray. This is how you shut the nicotine trap with your eyes open.

Do not hurry the smoking of it to get it over with. Hurrying is the old sleep. We hurried hundreds because we did not want to look. We looked at the street, at the phone, at the door, anywhere but at the paper in our own hand. This time look. Let the dose take its usual four or five minutes and let each minute report. First minute: heat and paper. Second minute: grey on the tongue and tighter draw. Third minute: ash knocking and fingers warming too much. Fourth minute: sourness settling and the mind already rehearsing the next one to cover this one. That rehearsal is the confession. A genuine pleasure does not rehearse its own cover in the same minute.

Hold the pack while you do it. Feel its weight. Cardboard, foil, the rattle of sticks like change in a box. We used to pat that box the way we pat a wallet, to check we were safe for the evening. Ask what safety that box ever gave. It gave doorway dates. It gave pocket-checks in the middle of talk. It gave arithmetic — can I last till the break, till the meal ends, till the taxi comes. Weigh it in the palm and feel it for what it has become in your sight: a day's supply box of doses, each tube loaded to quiet for minutes the edginess the last tube planted.

When you grind the last one, grind the meaning with it. You are not grinding a friend. You are grinding a dispenser that billed you for its own noise.

### THE MINUTE AFTER

Do not stand up yet. Stay seated for the minute after. This minute is the whole book in practice.

Look at the tray. A minute ago there was lit paper in it and you were a smoker. Now there is cold paper in it and you are not. Nothing else in the room has changed. The chair holds you the same. The light falls the same. The sounds in the next room go on the same. Only the timetable in the pocket has stopped.

Put your hand where the pack used to pull. Feel the pocket flat. No box. No bulge. No counting how many left for the evening. No planning where to stand to steal the next three minutes. All those years the pocket decided when you left talk, when you cut work, when you stepped into rain. Feel how quiet the pocket is now.

Leave it there a moment longer. Let the hand learn idleness. We spent years cupping and shielding, one hand hollowed around flame, the other fanning wind. Now both hands lie open on the table. Pick up the cup with either hand. No transfer needed. No ash to mind on the sleeve. The hands are full of nothing and ready for anything.

Breathe through the nose. Not a deep test breath. Just the ordinary breath you will take thousands of times today. Notice the air arriving cooler than the smoke did, without heat or rasp. The chest does not lift higher. It lifts cleaner. You do not need to be taught how to do this. Your body knows how to breathe and taste without instruction.

Drink water. Let it run over the sour back of the tongue. Taste how the water tells the truth — clean blunted by grey for a little while, then clean again. That blunting is not loss. It is the last grey washing off. In an hour food will taste brighter because no new grey was laid over it. In the morning the mouth will be yours, not coated from fresh paper.

Listen to the room. No clock ticking toward the next doorway. No edgy arithmetic. The day has stopped being sliced. It lies whole in front of you again, the way it lay before the first dose taught you to cut it.

I want you to feel the solemn part of this joy. This is not giddiness. It is the quiet after a long noise stops. You lit that last one awake and it gave you stain on the fingers, ash on the tray, sourness on the tongue, tightness in the chest. It did plenty to mouth and throat and pocket and left nothing for talk or work or calm. Once seen awake, it cannot be unseen.

Leave the Nipper unfed and it dies within days. Not a mighty enemy you must wrestle. A small, starving creature whose voice is already thinning because no new dose replanted it. What you feel across the next days is that thinning — an empty tug that typically sharpens for two or three days and then eases within weeks, though not on the same clock for every smoker — not proof paper was your friend, but proof the little tenant is closing.

You do not wait to become a non-smoker after some trial of days. You are one now because there is no belief left that a dose gives. That is what instant identity means. Not that the tongue clears in a minute. But that the question is settled in a minute. The smoker who lit is not being punished. He is being left at the table with warmth, because he was conned. The con that fed him is being left without mercy.

Sit one more minute and let that settle. No sadness rising. No pang of farewell for a friend. If a soft thought comes — surely something is over — answer it awake: what is over is doorway dates and pocket-checks and tasting food through coating. What is beginning is evenings uncut and mornings uncoated. Sorrow would only make sense if something had given. Nothing gave.

Stand and walk to the sink with the tray. Tip the cold butt and the grey into the bin. Rinse the tray under the tap and set it upside down to dry. That small clink is larger than it sounds. For years that tray held the timetable. Now it holds water drops. Wash your hands with soap, slow, between the fingers, under the nails. Smell them after. Soap, skin, cup-handle, cloth. That is the smell of your hands when no new grey is laid over them. Put the empty pack in the bin on top of the butt. Not as a gesture of strength. As tidying after sight. The kitchen is as it was. The hands are as they were before the first con. Only the tenant has no food.

### THE VOW

Stand up from the table and make the vow out loud, even if only in a whisper.

“I am not smoking any more. Not one puff. Not ever.”

Say it as fact, not as wish. Not as hope. As done.

You say it now because the sight is complete, not because you have promised to grit your teeth. Teeth never freed anyone from this. Sight does. If you live with illness or take prescribed medicine, speak to your clinician for your own care. This book changes what you believe a dose gives. It does not prescribe.

When the thought floats up next week — a doorway, a laugh, a hand reaching where the pack used to be — you do not argue with it and you do not mourn it. You answer it from sight with the thought you will keep for life, said with joy in the chest because an enemy is dead and no friend is lost:

YIPPEE! I'M FREE!

Say it now once, to feel how it sits in the mouth compared with ash. Sour was the stick. Clear is you.

All you have to do is follow all the instructions.

12. SMOKE YOUR FINAL CIGARETTE AND KNOW YOU ARE FREE
Close the trap with joy, not sadness.

Congratulations. You came as a smoker braced for lecture and you leave this table as a non-smoker with cold paper in the tray. I freed myself this way after years inside, and I have watched thousands walk the same minute after the same tray. Welcome to clear mornings and talk that runs uncut.

### THE TWO DOORS THAT STAY SHUT

Two knocks will come. Both sound like you. Both are the old script arriving pre-labelled.

Hear each one now while the tray is still damp in the rack, so that when it comes in the flesh you meet it as a non-smoker handling a memory, not as a smoker fighting a want.

One comes on a hard hour when the old line says a dose steadies — desk calm was tug-quieting only, the papers sitting untouched all along.

Stay at the table and live that hard hour through with the pocket flat, because it will not wait a week. It comes tonight.

You have stubbed at eight. At twenty past the phone lights. A bill. A sharp message. A voice on the other end that takes a tone. You put the phone down and your shoulders climb. The hand moves by itself toward the pocket where the box used to sit. Flat cloth. Nothing to pat. In the old days that flatness would have been panic. Tonight let it be information.

Sit back in the chair. Do not stand to chase anything. Feel what is actually in the body: an empty, slightly restless tug under the ribs, a thin edginess at the back of the neck. Name it for what it is while it is happening. That is not a need for paper. That is the Nipper thinning because it was not fed at eight. It typically sharpens for two or three days and then eases within weeks, though not on the same clock for every smoker, and this evening is the sharp edge doing its brief work.

Say what you do, out loud in the room, so the body hears the owner and not the tenant: “Pocket flat. Tray cold. Nothing to light.” Then answer the tug with the thought that replaces it for life, not as a chant to drown feeling but as a report of fact felt in the chest:

YIPPEE! I'M FREE!

Feel how different that sentence sits from the old doorway shuffle. The shuffle said: step out, burn three minutes, come back to the same papers with a drier mouth. This says: stay in, keep the evening whole, go back to the papers with a clear mouth.

Now do the next physical minute. Both hands open on the table. Pick up the water glass with either hand. Drink. Let the water run over the tongue. Put the glass down without shielding anything from wind. Turn the phone face down. Look at the papers. They sit untouched, exactly as they sat after every old dose. No line on them was ever moved by ash. Move one line now with a pen. One answer written. One message returned in plain words. That one moved line is more calming than a hundred step-outs because it touches the thing that made the hour hard.

What do you think when the old line tries again ten minutes later? It will try. “That water and one line prove nothing. You are just white-knuckling through. Have a proper steadier.” Hear it arrive already labelled, not as your reason but as the trap’s script talking through tight shoulders. You answer it from sight, not from effort: “Steadier of what? The bill is still the bill. The message is still sent. Paper never moved a figure.” You do not count minutes. You do not watch the clock for when you may. You stretch the neck once, roll the shoulders once, breathe ordinary air through the nose, and go back to the second line.

What do you feel at nine, when the hour would once have cost three doses? The tug is still there, thinner than at twenty past. The shoulders are lower. The papers have two lines moved. The mouth is still yours. The pocket is still flat. No doorway in the rain. No mint stinging on coating. No splitting yourself into the one who knows and the one who hides. The evening lies uncut from eight to nine, and the hard thing was met by hands and pen, not by fire.

That is what staying shut feels like in the body as a non-smoker now. Not a triumph posed for a mirror. A quieter ledger: time kept, taste kept, hands kept, temper kept. The Nipper asked and was not fed, and its asking proved it is small and closing, not that paper ever steadied.

One comes with a smile when drink and mates make an offer glow — patio pleasure was the night and company all along, and I know I can never have just one because one lit match reopens what one stubbed end closed.

Live that smile through as well, because it comes as early as tomorrow and it wears a friendly face.

Next evening. Glasses clinking. Heaters ticking. Laughter running over. A mate you like holds out the pack, taps one forward. “Go on. Have one with me. One won’t hurt.” The hand extends. The foil glints. For a second the old film runs: first light, head swim, belonging.

Keep your hands where they are — one around the glass, one on the chair-back — and let the film run to its true end while the offer hangs there. You know that end because you smoked it awake yesterday. Yellow at the nail. Heat rasp. Grey on the tongue. Pale ash. Stale end. Sour back. Tight chest. Tray taste for hours. That is what the shining stick becomes in four minutes. There is no version where it stays shining.

Say it plainly with warmth, without preaching and without stepping away: “No thanks — I don’t use them any more.” No story. No apology. No lecture on their dose. Turn back to the talk you were in. Taste the drink bright because no new grey was laid over it. Laugh at the punchline the first time because you did not miss the middle of it outside.

What do you think when the old line queues up behind the laugh? It will queue up. “You handled the hard hour. Surely one with friends is different. Prove your control by having just one and leaving it.” Hear it arrive already labelled. That is the lamp-rub dressed as sociability. You answer it from sight: “Control was the tray yesterday. One lit match reopens what one stubbed end closed. I closed it as done.” You hold finality not as a rule to obey but as an identity already settled. You are not a smoker refusing a treat. You are a non-smoker noticing that no treat was offered.

Feel the next ten minutes as they run. The mate lights and steps half aside into the cold edge. You stay in the warm circle. No pocket-check. No counting how many left for the night. No planning where to stand. The talk runs on without a slice cut out of it. Your glass empties and you fetch another with either hand, no transfer needed, no ash to mind. Someone tells the long story and you hear the middle that you used to hear through smoke. At the taxi you smell coats, night air, rain on pavement — not tray on shirt. In the morning the mouth is yours, the tongue uncoated, tea tasting of tea.

That is the second staying-shut in the flesh. Not deprivation endured among friends. Belonging kept whole while one stepped out to feed a tug.

Now hold both knocks side by side in the same evening of your mind, because they are the only two doors and both stay shut the same way.

Hard hour: pocket flat, tug named as thinning tenant, replacement thought said with joy, hands and pen moving the true problem, evening kept whole.

Smiling offer: hands kept on glass and chair, true end run to stale butt, warm no without story, replacement thought said with joy, night kept whole.

In both, what you feel across the next days is the same thinning — an empty tug with a short typical peak then easing, varying by person — not proof paper helped, but proof the little tenant is unfed. In both, one offered light after months clear does not prove control, it rebuilds the chain, so no puff is allowed, not as a vow of iron but as plain sight: the stubbed end closed; the lit match would reopen.

If you ever slip and take a puff in unguarded seconds, do not turn a warning into a sentence. A slip is a loud rumble strip, sour in the mouth at once, there to steer straight back to sight. Grind any half out at once. Rinse the mouth with cold water. Go back inside to talk uncut by doorways. Do not feed a second. Guard the belief and the chain does not rebuild. Let the belief back in and repeat the dosing and the chain rebuilds. Learn, do not mourn.

ONE STUBBED END CLOSES IT. ONE LIT MATCH REOPENS IT.

- The vow marked the crossing to non-smoker as done now, not after days counted.
- The replacement thought answers any future doorway or laugh with joy that paper is no longer used.
