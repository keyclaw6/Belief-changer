Chapter 13
MORNINGS, SHOPS AND ORDINARY DAYS

IN THIS CHAPTER
The kettle and the cold kitchen floor. The shop queue and the shutter wall. The eleven o'clock step outside. A passing thought at the doorway. Talk that runs to the taxi.

*There is nothing to handle in an ordinary day — you live it as it is and find it was whole all along.*

### The Cold Floor And The Kettle

You wake to the same alarm, in the same bed, with the same light coming through the same crack in the curtains.

We used to wake into arithmetic. Eyes half open, hand already moving toward the pack on the chair before feet found anything solid. First heat on empty lungs, throat taught not to notice, mouth coated before tea. The day began cut in two — the dose first, then everything else through grey.

This morning there is no first errand. Feet find the cold floor before the hand finds anything, and that small order tells the whole story. You stand. You walk to the kitchen. You put the kettle on.

I stood in that same kitchen myself the morning after I stubbed the last one. Same lino cold underfoot. Same kettle taking its minute. I remember expecting a gap, a missing prop, a hand with nothing to do. What came instead was plain: two hands for the cup, nose over steam, tea tasting of tea from the first sip because no new grey had been laid over the tongue in the night.

Drink it slowly. Notice what is actually there. The cup warm in both palms. No transfer from hand to hand to keep ash off the sleeve. No eye on the clock counting when you can step out. No planning the doorway before the day has started. The mouth is yours, a little sour from yesterday perhaps, clearing with each sip of water and tea. The chest lifts without rasp. Ordinary breath through the nose, cooler than smoke ever was, arriving without heat.

Shower after. We used to carry the smell in with us — hair, fingers, shirt from the night before — and Febreeze the hell out of ourselves to hide the divided self from partner and kids. Now soap meets skin and stays. Smell your hands after. Skin, soap, cloth, kettle steam. That is the smell of hands that lit nothing since evening.

Dress as normal. Change nothing else in life. Same coat. Same bag. Same route. Pat the pocket if the old habit pats. Flat cloth. Nothing to count. In the old days that flatness would have meant panic halfway to the bus. Now it means information: no box, no bulge, no timetable deciding when you leave talk or cut work or step into rain.

Ask yourself, not as a test but as a plain noticing: what is missing? Not pleasure. Not calm. Not company. What is missing is doorway dates and pocket-checks and tasting food through coating. What is present is a morning uncut from alarm to door.

That is the first owned day. Not endured. Lived.

### The Shop Queue

You will go to the shop today, as you always do. Milk. Bread. A paper. The queue moves its slow shuffle toward the till.

We all know that wall behind the counter — the shutter, the rows, the bright packs lined like tins. We used to read it the way we read a menu, eye flicking to our brand, throat giving a little pull, hand already rehearsing coins. The smoker in the queue ahead buys twenty and the foil glints and the old film runs: first light, head swim, belonging.

Stand in that queue as you are now and watch what actually happens.

You see the shutter. You hear the beep of the till. You feel your feet on the lino and the basket light in your hand. No hand moves by itself. No arithmetic starts — can I last till the break, till the meal ends, till the taxi comes. The pull, if it comes at all, is thin and brief, an empty tug under the ribs that passes while the queue moves one shuffle forward. You do not argue with it. You do not lecture it. You let the queue move and it moves past.

I know that moment at the till. I stood there the second day, basket in hand, eyes level with the wall I had paid thousands to. For years I had told myself those packs held my reward, my pause, my little parties after teaching a class or finishing a paragraph or driving to the store. Seen awake, they held paper tubes packed to quiet for minutes the edginess the last tube had planted. The party had been the work done, the drive done, the paragraph done. The tube had only laid itself over the top and billed me for the covering.

Buy what you came for. Put the coins down. Take the change. Walk out with the bag in either hand — no transfer needed, no shielding from wind, no cupping a flame in a doorway. The bell over the door rings the same behind you. The street smells of rain on pavement and bus fumes and bakery, not tray on shirt. Notice how much arrives when nothing is laid over it.

Someone lights ahead of you at the bus stop and steps half aside into the cold edge with the old hollowed hand around flame. Watch with clear eyes and keep your own judgment. Does that really look like enjoyment? Shoulders up. Draw tight. Eyes on when the next one can come. You stay in the warm circle of your own morning, bag in hand, mouth clear, no slice cut out of the wait.

That is the second owned hour. Not a trial of strength. A shop done as shops are done.

### The Eleven O'Clock Step

Mid-morning comes with its old cue. Work pauses. Chairs push back. Someone says air.

We used to hear that word as a bell. Break meant doorway. Doorway meant dose. Three minutes outside, talk interrupted, coffee cooling inside while we burned paper to quiet the tug and called the quiet focus. Then back in with drier mouth and tighter chest, already rehearsing the next one.

Take the break as it is now.

Carry your cup out if you like. Stand on the step. Feel the air on the face without heat or rasp laid over it. Both hands open — one for the cup, one for the rail — no ash to mind on the sleeve, no butt to grind. Drink. Let the drink tell the truth, bright because no new grey was laid over it. Look at the street. Buses. Pigeons. A delivery van double-parked. Ordinary, complete, asking nothing to be added.

Talk if talk comes. We used to miss the middle of every story, stepping out to feed the tug while the punchline landed inside. Now you hear the long story whole. You laugh at the punchline the first time because you did not miss the middle of it outside. Your glass empties and you fetch another with either hand. No counting how many left for the afternoon. No planning where to stand.

If the tug whispers — "just step out with us, keep us company" — hear it for what it is. Not your reason. The old script arriving pre-labelled. You answer it not by effort but by sight: the step is company already. The cup is warm already. The pause is yours already. The dose never moved a line of work; the papers sat untouched after every old step-out exactly as they sit now. Move one line now with a pen when you go back in. One answer written. One message returned in plain words. That one moved line touches the thing that made the morning busy.

By twelve the morning lies whole from kettle to lunch, uncut by doorways. No mint stinging on coating. No splitting yourself into the one who knows and the one who hides. Time kept. Taste kept. Hands kept. Temper kept.

That is what a break is when paper is not laid over it. Not empty. Full.

### When The Thought Drops By

It will drop by. Not as a storm. As a passing thought.

Afternoon. A doorway. A laugh from the smoking corner. That extended hand on the patio with its shining offer, remembered for a second in daylight. The foil glints in memory. The old film starts: first light, head swim, belonging.

Let it come. You cannot not think about something by forbidding it, and you do not need to. You meet it as a non-smoker handling a memory, not as a smoker fighting a want.

Keep your hands where they are — one around the cup, one on the chair-back — and let the film run to its true end while the memory hangs there. You know that end because you smoked it awake at the farewell table. Yellow at the nail. Heat rasp. Grey on the tongue. Pale ash. Stale end. Sour back. Tight chest. Tray taste for hours. That is what the shining stick becomes in four minutes. There is no version where it stays shining.

Say what you do, out loud in the room if you like, so the body hears the owner: "Pocket flat. Tray cold. Nothing to light." Then answer the thought with the sentence you will keep for life, said with joy in the chest because an enemy is dead and no friend is lost.

YIPPEE! I'M FREE!

Feel how different that sentence sits from the old doorway shuffle. The shuffle said: step out, burn three minutes, come back to the same papers with a drier mouth. This says: stay in, keep the afternoon whole, go back to the papers with a clear mouth.

What do you think when the old line tries again ten minutes later? It will try. "That was just words. Prove it by having one and leaving it." Hear it arrive already labelled, not as your reason but as the trap's script talking through tight shoulders. You answer it from sight, not from effort: "Control was the tray. One lit match reopens what one stubbed end closed. I closed it as done." You hold finality not as a rule to obey but as an identity already settled. You are not refusing a treat. You are noticing that no treat was offered.

Drink water. Let it run over the tongue. Put the glass down without shielding anything from wind. Stretch the neck once, roll the shoulders once, breathe ordinary air through the nose, and go back to the second line of work. By three the tug is thinner than at two. By four the afternoon has run uncut and the thought has passed the way weather passes — noticed, named, gone.

That is the whole handling. Not suppression. Replacement.

### Talk That Runs To The End

Evening comes earliest to test the old story. Glasses. Heaters ticking. Laughter running over. Mates you like. Someone steps half aside and you stay in the warm circle.

We used to live evenings sliced — talk, doorway, talk, doorway — missing the middle of every long story and calling the slices belonging. We patted the box the way we pat a wallet, to check we were safe for the night. What safety did that box ever give? It gave arithmetic and rain and mints on coating and coats smelling of tray in the taxi.

Stay for the whole talk now.

Sit where you usually sit. Taste the drink bright because no new grey was laid over it. Listen to the long story and hear the middle. Laugh the first time. When glasses empty, fetch another with either hand, no transfer needed. No pocket-check. No counting. No planning where to stand to steal the next three minutes. The talk runs on without a slice cut out of it.

I sat that first evening myself after years of stepping out, certain the night would feel flat without paper. What felt flat had been the stepping out — the cold edge, the burn, the return to talk already moved on. What felt full was staying — coats, night air, rain on pavement on the way home, tea tasting of tea before bed, morning mouth yours and uncoated.

At the taxi you smell coats, night air, rain on pavement — not tray on shirt. In the morning the mouth is yours, the tongue uncoated. No tray to rinse. No butt to tip. No arithmetic for the day ahead. The day lies whole in front of you again, the way it lay before the first con taught you to cut it.

Ask the only question worth asking at the end of such a day: did any hour need paper added to be complete? Morning kettle. Shop queue. Step outside at eleven. Doorway thought. Evening talk. Each ran to its end without it. Each was brighter, not duller, for no new grey laid over it.

Once seen awake in ordinary hours, it cannot be unseen.

**SUMMARY**
- The morning kettle proved the day starts whole, with tea tasting of itself and both hands open for the cup.
- The shop queue proved the shutter wall holds paper tubes only, with nothing in them the basket needs.
- The eleven o'clock step proved a pause is air and talk and drink already, with work moved by pen not by fire.
- A passing doorway thought proved a memory can be met and answered with joy, then left to pass like weather.
- Evening talk proved belonging stays in the warm circle, hearing the middle of the story to the taxi home.
