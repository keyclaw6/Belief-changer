Chapter 3
WHAT DOES IT ACTUALLY DO FOR YOU

**IN THIS CHAPTER** — The kitchen-table ledger, the hug that won't let go, the cooling dinner and the finished job

*Forget the price for a minute — tell me what you ever bought.*

### SAY IT OUT LOUD

You carry a ledger in your head. We all did.

On one side, in red, the price. Money. Cough in the morning. Stairs that leave you short. The coat that smells. The midnight garage. The mint and the spray. On the other side, in blue, what you tell yourself you buy. Calm. Company. Taste. A pause. A reward after work done. Company on a night out.

And all day you weigh red against blue and tell yourself, “Well, it harms me, but it does something for me too.”

I want you to lay that ledger on the table and look at the blue side with a witness’s eyes. Not to argue about the red. We are not talking about harm today. Harm is real, but harm never freed anyone. A man can know a thing harms him and still clutch it, because he believes it gives him something he cannot get elsewhere.

So say it out loud, plainly, in your own dialect, while you smoke as normal. No editing.

“It steadies me when I am stressed. It tastes good. It is my reward. It keeps me company. It helps me concentrate. It punctuates my day. Those little parties between jobs.”

Good. That is honest. I said every one of those. Every smoker says them. Now keep the packet where it is and do the plain test I did at my own kitchen table.

Did you say any of those things before you learned to smoke?

Your first dose did not steady you. It burned your throat and made you dizzy and made you cough until your eyes watered. Your first dose did not taste good. You had to learn to перекрыt — no, you had to learn to cover the taste with coffee and mints and menthol. Your first dose was not a reward for work. You had done no work. It was a dare, a pose, a way to look grown while the older boys watched.

We did not pick these reasons and then smoke. We smoked, and then the Smokescreen pasted reasons on afterwards to explain why the hand kept moving.

Ask yourself, with kind eyes:

If the dose truly added taste, why do you taste food best before you light, and taste ash after? If the pause truly came from the dose, why does a non-smoker pause just as fully with his arms folded and his eyes on the street? If company truly came from the stick, why does the talk go on laughing after you have stubbed it?

You know the honest answers because you have lived them. The food fed you. The pause rested you. The company warmed you. The dose only rode along and asked for the credit.

That is the switch we turn in this chapter. Not “does harm outweigh benefit?” The fact is there is no benefit to outweigh. There is only a cost that learned to sign its name where your life signed.

### THE HUG THAT WON'T LET GO

Let me show you what that signing looks like from the inside.

You are upset. Phone down hard. Jaw tight. Papers exactly where you left them. The hand is already in the pocket. Tap, up, cup against the wind. First hot scratch at the throat. Shoulders drop a fraction for a minute. You say, “Ah. It takes care of it. It calms me down.”

I am not mocking that minute. I felt it a thousand times. That first heaven second at lighting is real as sensation — sharp in the nose, hot at the throat — and then, as you know, it turns flat and stale by halfway and a little sorry by the end. I am not denying the minute. I am asking who made the need for the minute.

Picture this. You are held tight from behind by strong arms. You struggle. You cannot get loose. Then the arms loosen just enough for you to draw breath, and you gasp with gratitude toward the arms. “Thank you. You saved me. I could not breathe without you.”

You never ask who was holding you.

That is what the dose does. It wraps itself around your chest in the morning, squeezes a little through the day, then eases for twenty minutes when you feed the Nipper, and you thank the easing as if it were kindness. It is not kindness. It is the same grip, loosened briefly.

The cigarette is both the arms and the loosening. It never touched the phone call. It never touched the bill. It never touched the row with its chairs still facing each other. The problem sits untouched where you left it while you stand outside. You return to the same papers, the same tight jaw, plus a stale mouth — and you call that help.

It is the other way around.

Once you see it, you cannot un-see it. A rescuer who created the danger is not a rescuer. A friend who will not let go is not a friend. We were grateful to the very thing that kept us breathless, and we called the gratitude pleasure.

Tell me — if a man held your head under water and let you up for air every forty minutes, would you call him your air? Would you list him in your ledger in blue ink as “helps me breathe”? Would you fear life without him? You would call him what he is. The nicotine trap did the same in miniature, puff by puff, until gratitude looked like knowledge.

### THE PARTY WAS YOURS

“But my reward,” you say. “Surely you will leave me that. After a class taught, after a paragraph finished, after the long drive, after the shop run — that little party is mine.”

I hear you. I lived by those little parties. Teaching a class — light. Finishing a story — light. Finishing a paragraph — light. Driving 500 miles — lights all the way. Driving to the grocery store — light for that too. Cigarettes were my payment for almost everything.

Listen to that list. Almost everything. What does that tell a witness?

It tells you the party was never in the work. The work was hard or dull or done, and the dose jumped in at the end and claimed the applause. The class went well because you knew your subject and the room listened. The paragraph landed because you found the true sentence. The miles passed because you drove them. The dose arrived after, laid its paper taste over a mouth that had just worked, and said, “That was me. Celebrate me.”

Did the dose ever write a sentence for you? Did it ever steer the car? Did it ever teach the child? No. You did. It sneaked a ride on your own competence and then charged you for the ride.

Watch tomorrow while you smoke as normal. Finish something small. Do not change the lighting. Just notice the order. Effort, finish, small lift in the chest that belongs to finishing — then light, then flat taste, then stub. The lift came before the match. The match did not make the lift. It borrowed it.

And food. Sit with a meal you like. Eat it slowly. Notice taste vivid while you chew. Notice the pause after, when flavour lingers and the body settles. Then, as usual, the hum comes and you light. What happens to taste? Ash covers it. The tongue dulls. The lingering is cut short. By the last third you are smoking because it is lit, not because it adds. Did the dose deepen dinner, or did dinner deepen in spite of it and then get wiped?

Company is the same. The laugh was the friend. The warmth was the drink in your hand and the night around you. The story was funny before you reached. Take the dose out in your mind and replay the scene — sun, leisure, faces, talk — and point to one laugh that needed hot paper to exist. You cannot. The scene was complete. It was only ever the dose telling you the scene would be empty without it, while it emptied your pocket to stay in the scene.

“I love smoking but I hate being a smoker.” You have said that split in bed at night. Love at the lighting. Sorry after. That is not a man describing a genuine gift. That is a man describing a bright second that turns stale, and a haze that tells him the second proves a day-long pleasure. The second never proved it. It proved only that a tug quiets briefly when fed.

Whose joy was it, then — the finished job, the meal, the pause, the talk? Yours. All yours.

### PLENTY TO YOU, NOTHING FOR YOU

So let us close the ledger and speak plain.

We smoked to still a nag we called stress, and the papers stayed where they were. We smoked to crown work, and the work had already crowned itself. We smoked to taste, and taste faded. We smoked to belong, and belonging was already holding our chair.

What, minute by minute, did the dose add to food, to pause, to company, to thought? Nothing you can point to that was not already there before the match. What did it take, minute by minute? Breath, money, taste, time outside in the cold, truth on the landing, mornings started with self-contempt.

That is not a balance. That is a one-sided bill with a forged signature on the other side.

It does plenty TO you. It does nothing FOR you.

Read that again with your own exhibits in front of you. Plenty TO your throat, your pocket, your stairs, your sleep, your hiding. Nothing FOR your problem, your work, your dinner, your friend, your pause. The TO is thick and daily. The FOR is smoke that clears when you look straight at it.

If there is no FOR, what is there to weigh? The whole red-versus-blue arithmetic collapses, not because red is heavy — though it is — but because blue is empty. You are not choosing between health and pleasure. You are choosing between health and a trick that taught you to call loosening its own grip a pleasure.

Do you see what that does to dread? Dread needs a loss to feed on. “How will I cope? How will I enjoy?” Those questions assumed something real would be taken. Once the blue side is empty, the questions have no mouth. There is no coping to replace, because the papers were never moved. There is no joy to replace, because the joy was the meal and the work and the friend all along.

Bring that empty blue side with you. Bring the names you learned — the nicotine trap as the place, the Nipper as the small whine, the Smokescreen as the haze that translates whine into need. With those in your pocket, the next rooms are simple looking, not hard fighting. We will look at calm and at parties up close, each in its own light, until each credit is handed back to its true owner.

You do not need to act today. You need only to stop calling a grip a gift.

3. BEGIN BY FEELING GREAT TO BE ESCAPING
There is no doom here, only freedom ahead.

**SUMMARY**
- The ledger was false because one side was empty; no weighing can make an empty side heavy.
- The minute of quiet after lighting never moved the problem; it only loosened for a moment a grip the doses had built.
- The bright second at lighting turns flat and sorry; it never added up to a day-long gift.
- Work, food, pause and company pleased on their own; the dose arrived after and claimed the credit.
- With no gift left to miss, the question changes from what harm costs to what good was ever there.
