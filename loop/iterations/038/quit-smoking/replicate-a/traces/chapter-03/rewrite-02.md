Chapter 3
WHAT DOES IT ACTUALLY DO FOR YOU

**IN THIS CHAPTER** — the kitchen table and the blue ledger, the hug from behind, the classroom and the finished paragraph, the hot dinner plate, the desk with the stuck letter

*Forget the price for a minute — tell me what you ever bought.*

### SAY IT OUT LOUD

You carry a ledger in your head. We all did.

On one side, in red, the price. Money out of the pocket every day. Cough in the morning. Stairs that leave you short. The coat that smells. The midnight step in socks. The mint and the spray. On the other side, in blue, what you tell yourself you buy. Calm. Company. Taste. A pause. A reward after work done. A break that makes the day hang together.

And all day you weigh red against blue and tell yourself, “Well, it harms me, but it does something for me too.”

I want you to lay that ledger on the kitchen table and look at the blue side with a witness’s eyes. Not to argue about the red. We are not talking about harm today. Harm is real, but harm never opened a locked mind. A man can know a thing harms him and still clutch it, because he believes it hands him something he cannot get elsewhere.

So say it out loud, plainly, in your own dialect. No editing.

“It steadies me when I am shaken. It tastes good. It is my reward. It keeps me company. It helps me concentrate. It punctuates my day. Those little parties between jobs.”

Good. That is honest. I said every one of those. Every smoker says them. Now keep your packet where it is and do the plain test I did at my own table.

Take the one you guard most closely if you think straight. “It helps me concentrate.”

I guarded that one like a toolbox. I told myself a ciggie cleared my head. Stuck on a letter, stuck on a bill, stuck on what to say to a difficult man — light up and think. Every smoker knows that lean-back with the first drag, eyes narrowed, as if thought had been switched on.

Do the witness test on that very desk, tomorrow, without smoking less or more. Only watch the order.

You sit with a stuck page. The sentence will not come. The hum starts low down — that faint nagging you now know by name. You shift. You re-read the last line. You tap the pen. Then the hand is in the pocket before you have decided. Tap, up, cup against the air. Light.

Tell me honestly — did the idea arrive with the match?

Watch what actually happens in the next three minutes. First hot scratch at the throat. Shoulders drop a fraction for twenty seconds. Then eyes water a little. Then a small cough you cover with your hand. Then you re-read the same line again because the drag interrupted the reading. Then you reach for the ashtray, miss it, look at the ash, think about the price of ciggies, think about the wife smelling it, think about whether you have enough left for the evening. Then you stub it halfway because it tastes stale, and only then, with the paper gone and the mouth tasting of ash, the sentence comes.

Did the dose bring the sentence? Or did it bring five minutes of fidget and interruption, and the sentence came when you returned to the page in spite of it?

We confuse the pause after interruption with help. The hand stopped the work, walked us to the window, filled the head with smoke and small errands — tap, ash, stub, spray — and then when we sat back down the mind settled for a second and we credited the walk. That is like crediting a man who trips you for helping you up.

Ask yourself with kind eyes, as I asked myself at my own littered desk:

Did you lose concentration before you smoked, or after you started to smoke? Before you ever lit, as a boy at school, could you sit and finish a page without hot paper in your hand? Of course you could. You did it for years. Did the great pages you have written since arrive while the smoke was in your eyes, or in the quiet seconds between doses when your head was clear? You know. They arrived between.

And look at the non-smoker at the next desk. He gets stuck too. He frowns. He stares out of the window. He turns the pen over. He does not leave his chair for cold air. He does not lay ash over his tongue. His sentence comes — or it waits a while, as sentences do — without a dose to crown it. Is his thinking broken for lack of a stick? Is yours sharper with yellow fingers?

Here is the plain order a witness writes down. Stuck mind, hum, light, interruption, flat middle, stub, return, idea. The dose did not carry thought in. It carried thought away for a few minutes and then handed back what was left and asked for thanks.

“But it punctuates my day,” you say. “Surely those stops help me think.”

The stop helps you think. The smoke does not. A man can stand, stretch, look at the street, breathe to the bottom of his lungs, let the back of his mind work while his eyes rest — and call that a pause. We stood, we lit, we coughed, we watched the clock, we hurried because the dose was burning down, and we called that thinking. Which pause rested you? The one with clean air in it, or the one with hot paper?

Try this small seeing without changing anything. Next time you are stuck, notice the moment before the hand moves. The thought is still there, circling, close. Then the hand moves first, as it always does. Notice how the circling stops — not because the idea landed, but because the ritual barged in. Lighter, drag, ash. You lose the thread you were holding. You spend the dose looking for the thread again. When you find it after, you say, “There — the ciggie did it.” Would you thank a dog for finding a shoe it had hidden itself?

It punctuates, yes. It punctuates the way a smudge punctuates a clean page. It breaks the sentence and then takes credit when you re-read it.

And concentration that needs feeding every forty minutes is not concentration. It is attendance on the Nipper. A mind that must stop to quiet a tug, quiet the tug for twenty minutes, then stop again to quiet it again, is not a focused mind. It is an interrupted mind calling interruption focus. The Smokescreen pasted a grand word — concentration — over a small routine — feeding.

Did the dose ever give you an idea you did not already have in you? Did it ever spell a word for you? Did it ever add one fact to your head? No. You did the thinking. It did the interrupting. It sneaked a ride on your own mind and charged you in breath and money for the ride.

Keep that desk in front of you when you check the rest. The same order will appear everywhere. The good was yours before the match. The match arrived after and borrowed it.

Did you say any of those things before you learned to smoke?

Your first dose did not steady you. It burned your throat and made you dizzy and made you cough until your eyes watered. Your first dose did not taste good. You had to learn to cover the taste with coffee and mints and menthol. Your first dose was not a reward for work. You had done no work. It was a dare, a pose, a way to look grown while the older boys watched. Your first dose did not keep you company. You were already with people. You left the talk to cough round the corner.

We did not pick these reasons and then smoke. We smoked, and then the Smokescreen pasted reasons on afterwards to explain why the hand kept moving.

Ask yourself, with kind eyes:

If the dose truly added taste, why do you taste food best before you light, and taste ash after? If the pause truly came from the dose, why does a non-smoker pause just as fully with his arms folded and his eyes on the street? If company truly came from the stick, why does the talk go on laughing after you have stubbed it? If thought truly came from the dose, why does the thread return clearest after you have stubbed it and the air clears?

You know the honest answers because you have lived them. The food fed you. The pause rested you. The company warmed you. The mind thought when you left it alone. The dose only rode along and asked for the credit.

That is the switch we turn in this chapter. Not “does harm outweigh benefit?” The fact is there is no benefit to outweigh. There is only a cost that learned to sign its name where your life signed.

### THE HUG THAT WON’T LET GO

Let me show you what that signing looks like from the inside.

Picture this. You are held tight from behind by strong arms. You struggle. You cannot get loose. Then the arms loosen just enough for you to draw breath, and you gasp with gratitude toward the arms. “Thank you. You saved me. I could not breathe without you.”

You never ask who was holding you.

That is what the dose does. It wraps itself around your chest in the morning, squeezes a little through the day, then eases for twenty minutes when you feed the Nipper, and you thank the easing as if it were kindness. It is not kindness. It is the same grip, loosened briefly.

I am not mocking that minute of loosening. I felt it a thousand times. That first hot second at lighting is real as sensation — sharp in the nose, hot at the throat — and then, as you know, it turns flat and stale by halfway and a little sorry by the end. I am not denying the sensation. I am asking who made the need for the sensation.

A rescuer who created the danger is not a rescuer. A friend who will not let go is not a friend. We were grateful to the very thing that kept us breathless, and we called the gratitude pleasure.

Tell me — if a man held your head under water and let you up for air every forty minutes, would you call him your air? Would you list him in your ledger in blue ink as “helps me breathe”? Would you fear a day without him? You would call him what he is. The nicotine trap did the same in miniature, puff by puff, until gratitude looked like knowledge.

The row you left in the kitchen sits untouched where you left it.

It is the other way around.

Once you see it, you cannot un-see it.

### THE PARTY WAS YOURS

“But my reward,” you say. “Surely you will leave me that. After a class taught, after a paragraph finished, after the long drive, after the shop run — that little party is mine.”

I hear you. I lived by those little parties. Teaching a class — light. Finishing a story — light. Finishing a paragraph — light. Driving 500 miles — lights all the way. Driving to the grocery store — light for that too. Cigarettes were my payment for almost everything.

Listen to that list. Almost everything. What does that tell a witness?

It tells you the party was never in the work. The work was hard or dull or done, and the dose jumped in at the end and claimed the applause. The class went well because you knew your subject and the room listened. The paragraph landed because you found the true sentence. The miles passed because you drove them. The dose arrived after, laid its paper taste over a mouth that had just worked, and said, “That was me. Celebrate me.”

Did the dose ever write a sentence for you? Did it ever steer the car? Did it ever teach the child? No. You did. It sneaked a ride on your own competence and then charged you for the ride.

Watch tomorrow. Finish something small. Do not change the lighting. Just notice the order. Effort, finish, small lift in the chest that belongs to finishing — then light, then flat taste, then stub. The lift came before the match. The match did not make the lift. It borrowed it.

And food. Sit with a meal you like. Eat it slowly. Notice taste vivid while you chew. Notice the pause after, when flavour lingers and the body settles. Then, as usual, the hum comes and you light. What happens to taste? Ash covers it. The tongue dulls. The lingering is cut short. By the last third you are smoking it because it is lit, not because it adds. Did the dose deepen dinner, or did dinner deepen in spite of it and then get wiped?

Company is the same. The laugh was the friend. The warmth was the drink in your hand and the night around you. The story was funny before you reached. Take the dose out in your mind and replay the scene — sun, leisure, faces, talk — and point to one laugh that needed hot paper to exist. You cannot. The scene was complete. It was only ever the dose telling you the scene would be empty without it, while it emptied your pocket to stay in the scene.

“I love smoking but I hate being a smoker.” You have said that split in bed at night. Love at the lighting. Sorry after. That is not a man describing a genuine gift. That is a man describing a bright second that turns stale, and a haze that tells him the second proves a day-long pleasure. The moment I light up I am in heaven, but I feel shitty afterwards — that exact turn from heaven to stale never added up to a day of pleasure. It proved only that a tug quiets briefly when fed.

Whose joy was it, then — the finished job, the meal, the pause, the talk, the thought that came when you left it alone? Yours. All yours.

### WHEN THERE IS NOTHING LEFT TO WEIGH

So let us close the ledger and speak plain.

We smoked to crown work, and the work had already crowned itself. We smoked to taste, and taste faded. We smoked to belong, and belonging was already holding our chair. We smoked to think, and thinking returned clearest when the smoke cleared.

What, minute by minute, did the dose add to food, to pause, to company, to thought? Nothing you can point to that was not already there before the match. What did it take, minute by minute? Breath, money, taste, time outside in the cold, truth on the landing, mornings started with self-contempt.

That is not a balance. That is a one-sided bill with a forged signature on the other side.

It does plenty TO you. It does nothing FOR you.

Read that again with your own exhibits in front of you. Plenty TO your throat, your pocket, your stairs, your sleep, your hiding. Nothing FOR your problem, your work, your dinner, your friend, your pause, your page. The TO is thick and daily. The FOR is smoke that clears when you look straight at it.

Do you see what that does to dread? Dread needs a loss to feed on.

I want you to stay inside that sentence until you feel what it does to an ordinary weighing day, because the ledger-keeper in you will not stop weighing by being told to stop. He will stop only when he catches himself starting and finds nothing to put on the scales.

Weighing is a habit, not a thought. You know its hours. Price up at the till — weigh. Cough on the stairs — weigh. Child wrinkling her nose at your sleeve — weigh. Doctor frowning — weigh. Midnight promise broken again — weigh. Each time the same tired sum: red heavy, blue light but present, sigh, light another dose to dull the sum. “I know it harms me. But I love my little parties. But I need my pause. But how would I cope? But how will I think?” The weighing never settled anything. It only rehearsed loss on both sides. Lose health if you carry on. Lose pleasure if you stop. Tug this way. Tug that way. And the hand moved while the mind added.

Now watch what happens when blue is empty.

Morning kettle. Pack out. Lighter up. The old calculator wakes on cue. Red — money, breath, smell. Blue — and the hand hovers, because the blue column has no entry that survives daylight. Taste? You tasted dinner best before the match. Pause? You watched the neighbour pause with folded arms and need no paper. Company? You heard the laugh carry on after the stub. Reward? You felt the lift arrive before the match and get borrowed after. Thought? You watched the thread return after the stub, not with it. Calm? The papers sat where they were. Each blue phrase you try to write dissolves as you write it. Not argued away. Simply absent when you reach for it.

At first that absence feels strange. The mind keeps reaching like a tongue for a missing tooth. Surely something must go here. Surely the heaven second counts. Surely the minute of quiet counts. Then you remember what you watched with your own eyes: heaven at lighting, flat by halfway, sorry by the end. A minute of loosened grip, not a gift. A borrowed lift, not a made lift. An interrupted page, not a helped page. And the tongue stops probing because there is no tooth to find.

That is the moment weighing stops, not balances.

Balancing assumes two weights. Stopping assumes one column went missing. You do not decide health matters more than pleasure. You look for the pleasure to set against health and find printed smoke. No heavier red needed. No louder warning needed. No new resolve needed. The scales do not tip. They are put away because one pan holds air.

Notice how quiet the head goes when the scales are put away.

For years every health notice started the same inner noise. Poster at the surgery — noise. Price ticket — noise. Short breath on the second flight — noise. That noise was not thought. It was the ledger opening of its own accord and demanding its daily rehearsal. Red versus blue. Fear versus want. Should versus cannot. You lived inside that noise so long you called it thinking. Now the same poster appears and the ledger opens and finds one column gone, and there is nothing for fear to grip on the other side. The poster is still there. The price is still there. The stair is still there. But the inner courtroom does not convene, because a courtroom needs two parties.

This is what catches smokers off guard. They expect stopping the arithmetic to feel like effort. It feels like a room going silent after a fan is switched off. You keep listening for the hum out of habit, and its absence is the news.

You will catch yourself starting the old sum out of pure reflex and then smiling mid-sum at its absurdity. Till queue. You see the figure on the packet. Old voice: “Dear, but worth it for...” For what? The sentence does not finish because the witness in you is already holding up the exhibits: dinner tasted, work finished, talk laughed, pause rested, page written — all without addition. The sentence trails off, not suppressed, simply unfinished for lack of material. You pay, you pocket, you walk out, and the unfinished sentence stays behind at the till like a receipt you did not need.

Bus stop. Cold air. Someone lights beside you. Old voice: “Ah, that pause looks...” Looks what? You watch the man cup and drag and finish because it is lit, and you know that heaven-to-stale turn better than you know his face. The phrase “looks good” cannot complete itself. Not because you forbid it, but because you have watched too many middles go flat to lend it weight.

Evening chair. Meal done. Plate warm. Old voice: “Now the...” Now the what? The lingering flavour is still on the tongue. The body is settling in its own way. The hum suggests a full stop, and you see the full stop is already there in the empty plate and the eased shoulders. The dose would not punctuate the sentence. It would wipe it.

Desk again. Stuck line. Old voice at its most clever: “Just one to get the mind...” Get the mind what? Going? Clear? You have watched that film. Hand first, thread lost, ash, fidget, stub, thread found again where you left it. The verb has no object that survives daylight. “To get the mind” hangs in the air with nowhere to land, and you turn back to the page with the sentence still hanging, unneeded.

Night landing. Teeth brushed. Old voice at its most pious: “One to...” To what? To sleep? You know sleep comes dulled, not deepened, with stale on the breath. To settle the day? The day is settled in the blanket and the quiet house. The verb has no object. “One to” hangs in the air with nowhere to land, and you switch off the light with the sentence still hanging, unneeded.

Each unfinished sentence is dread losing its mouth.

Dread always spoke in full sentences. “How will I face mornings?” “How will I get through breaks?” “How will I think my way through hard work?” Those questions sounded like practical problems. They were ledger sentences in disguise. Each assumed a blue weight that would be taken and must be replaced. Mornings assumed the dose started the engine. Breaks assumed the dose made the rest. Work assumed the dose made the thought. Nights out assumed the dose made the warmth. Once you have watched engine, rest, thought and warmth arrive without addition, the questions cannot form their subject. How will I face mornings without what? The “what” has no referent. The mouth opens and finds no food.

That is why this is not bravery. Bravery still feels the loss and walks through it. This feels the loss dissolve under inspection until there is no through to walk. You are not telling yourself pleasure does not matter. You are seeing the named pleasures were your own dinners and pauses and friendships and finished pages all along, with a paper tax laid over them. No man needs courage to stop paying a tax he never owed.

And that changes what tomorrow’s triggers do.

A trigger used to be an order. Coffee meant light. Meal-end meant light. Phone down meant reach. Car door meant tap and up. Stuck page meant lean-back and drag. Now a trigger is only a clock chime reminding you to look. Coffee lifts, and you taste the cup doing the comforting. Meal-end settles, and you feel flavour lingering without need of ash. Stuck page tightens the brow for a minute, and you feel the idea circling close, with no stamp of helper laid over the hum. Phone down with a sharp word still tightens the jaw for a minute, and you feel shoulders and papers and breath exactly as they are. The chime rings. You look. You see. The hand may still move while you are learning to look — we are studying, not fighting — but the commentary no longer signs confessions. There goes the tug. There goes the haze with its old translation. There goes the flat middle I know so well.

No trial follows. No vow follows. No “tomorrow I will be different” follows. The energy that went into trial and vow and self-sentencing stays in the day where it belongs. You will be startled how much of your tiredness was not smoke but sentencing. To wake and smoke and call yourself weak before your feet touched the floor took work. To rehearse failure between doses took work. To hide the tin, to air the coat, to count and promise and break took work. With the judge quiet, the hours thin out. The same day holds more day.

Bring that thinner day with you. Bring the names you learned when the trap first showed itself — the nicotine trap as the place, the Nipper as the small whine, the Smokescreen as the haze that translates whine into need. With those in your pocket, the blue column cannot refill behind your back. Each time the haze offers a fresh blue phrase — a new taste, a new pause, a new belonging, a new bright idea — you hold it up to a present you just watched. Did taste need ash five minutes ago? Did rest need paper? Did talk need hot smoke to laugh? Did the sentence need interruption to arrive? A frightened future cannot survive many quiet presents.

You do not need to act today. You need only to stop calling a grip a gift, and to let the calculator spin and find nothing and fall silent. That silence is not emptiness. It is the first clean sound of a mind no longer paying to argue with itself.

3. BEGIN BY FEELING GREAT TO BE ESCAPING
There is no doom here, only freedom ahead.

**SUMMARY**
- The ledger was false because one side was empty; no weighing can make an empty side heavy.
- The minute of quiet at lighting never moved anything; it only loosened briefly a grip the doses had built.
- The bright second at lighting turns flat and sorry; it never added up to a day-long gift.
- Work, food, pause, company and thought pleased on their own; the dose arrived after and claimed the credit.
- With no gift left to miss, weighing stops rather than balances, and dread has no mouth to speak with.
