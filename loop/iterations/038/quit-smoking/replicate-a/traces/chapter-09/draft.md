Chapter 9
WHO BUILT THIS WANT

IN THIS CHAPTER
The pack weighed in the palm, the filter and the mild promise, the mouth and chest in the morning mirror, the two fears pulling opposite ways

*You never invented this hunger — it was built for you, and fear is holding both ends of the rope.*

### THE BOX IN YOUR POCKET

Take the pack out now and hold it in the palm while we look at it together.

Not to smoke it. Just to see it. Feel the weight. Twenty tubes in a paper box, cellophane crackle, flip-top click. The crest, the colour, the gold band, the clean white filter tip. It looks like a personal thing. Like your thing. Like choice in a box.

Turn it over. That is not a box of pleasures. That is a day's supply box.

We bought it in the morning and counted the day in its numbers. One for the bus stop. One for the break. One for after food. One for the phone call. By night the box was light and the day was gone, and we bought another box to count tomorrow. No bottle of perfume is drunk in a day. No favourite food is eaten twenty times before dark. Only a dispenser is emptied on a timetable.

Look closer at the tube itself. Paper, filter, a pinch of leaf laced with a fast drug. The paper burns to carry. The filter paints a clean tip to hold between lips. The leaf is the carrier. Each draw is a measured squirt through that tube, up into lungs built for air, into blood, into brain in seconds, then sinking fast so the next squirt is called for within the hour. The factory does not count pleasures. It counts doses per pack, packs per day, days per life.

In their own planning papers, written for each other and never meant for your eyes, their own scientists put it flat: the cigarette was not the product at all. The wrapper was the packet. The drug inside was the product.

Let that sit in the palm. The taste you chose, the brand you were loyal to, the mild you moved to when your throat burned — none of that was the product. The product was the powder that pulls you back every forty minutes. The rest was theatre to let you hold the tube without seeing the squirt.

"But I chose it. Nobody holds my hand to my mouth."

I know. I said the same for years. So answer from your own hand.

Did you choose the dose size? Did you choose how fast it fades? Did you choose to need another one an hour later, and another after food, and another with drink? Did you choose the first one knowing it would teach your throat to want the twentieth thousand?

You chose the first paper on phoney information — that it looked grown-up, calm, sociable, sharp. We all did. We were young, or stressed, or out with friends, and the tutoring said try. After that, the timetable chose. That box is the nicotine trap in cardboard.

Be angry at the right address. Not at yourself. You are not stupid. You were conned by men in rooms who measured your breath and your puff and your day, and built a box to fit inside it. Warm to you, always. Contempt belongs on the bench that built the want and called it yours.

### THE THEATRE OF MILD

When the throat burned and the morning cough thickened, many of us did what seemed sensible. We moved to milds. Lights. Low-tar. The white tip with the tiny holes. The promise of less.

Hold one of those tips to the light. Around the filter you will see a ring of pinprick vents, too neat to be accident. On the machine that tests them, air slips in through those holes and the dial reads low. Low tar. Low nicotine. Mild.

Now put human lips and fingers on the same tip.

Lips close over the holes. Fingers cover the ring without knowing it. The chest pulls harder for the missing pull. The puff goes deeper, held a second longer. The body seeks the level it was taught to seek, and the hand and mouth do the seeking without asking you.

Machine smoke is not human smoke. The dial does not have lips. It does not have a morning throat that needs its level. It does not learn to pull harder when the squirt is thinned. You do.

Not to the same millimetre in every smoker — no two pairs of lips sit the same, no two chests pull the same — but the pattern was known on the bench before the pack ever reached your pocket. Thin the squirt and the smoker will work the tube harder to find it. That is not failure of will. That is the designed answer of a taught hunger to a smaller squirt.

Ask the mild years plainly.

If milds were milder, why did the day still count twenty? If the filter protected you, why did the cough stay, the throat stay raw, the morning throat still call for the first tube before feet touched floor? If low-tar meant low want, why did want never go low?

Because the tube was still a dispenser, only dressed in white to look innocent. The holes were not for your lungs. They were for the dial.

That tutoring is the Smokescreen at work.

It told you taste mattered, brand mattered, strength mattered. It told you to argue about flavour while the level rose and sank underneath. It told you a smoother con was a kinder con. While we debated menthol or Virginia, mild or full, the bench counted only one thing: did the level fade fast enough to bring the hand back to the pocket?

You never smoked flavour. You fed a level. The flavour talk was the curtain. Behind it, the squirt went in, faded in minutes, and called again.

### WHAT IT LEAVES ON YOU

Stand with me in front of the bathroom mirror tomorrow morning before the first tube, and look at what the box has rented on you.

Mouth first. Run the tongue over teeth. Sour, coated, grey at the back. Last night's final tube still camped on the tongue. The kiss you turned away from. The mint you chewed in the car with the window down, spraying, hiding, Febreezing the jacket and calling it fresh. Warm to you — I did the same dance, garage door up, mints, lies by omission to people I loved. Harsh on the need that made a grown man hide like a schoolboy. The mouth was not made to wake coated. The tube coated it.

Chest next. Climb the stairs you climbed yesterday. Halfway up, the pull shortens. The legs are willing. The chest is tight. A friend who never smoked walks beside you talking in full sentences while you ration breath to reach the landing. No lecture in this. Just your own stairs telling you what twenty squirts a day lay in the pipes. The body was built to carry you up without thought. The tube taught it to notice every step.

And the chain. This is the one we feel least and pay most. Watch the day you lived last week. Leave the house — check the pack. Long meeting — plan the break. Meal with non-smokers — watch the clock for the step outside. Train, plane, cinema, hospital corridor — first thought not where to sit but where to stand to burn. Rain, wind, cold — shivering in doorways while the party goes on inside without you. Money counted in tens a week, hundreds a month, thousands a year, for the privilege of standing apart.

Three marks, same owner. Sour mouth in the mirror. Short breath on the stairs. A timetable around the wrist tighter than any watch.

Live in that picture for a second without flinching. I am not painting it to frighten you into stopping. I do not want you to stop from fright, and fright never kept any smoker stopped past Friday. I want you to see whose initials are on the marks. They are not yours. They are the rent slip for a dispenser you carry for someone else's counting house.

You are stale, breathless and chained.

Say it without shame, as a man names a room he is walking out of. Stale in the mouth that was meant to taste. Breathless in the chest that was meant to climb without counting. Chained in the day that was meant to be yours hour by hour. That triple is not you. That is what twenty small squirts a day do TO a body, while doing nothing FOR it.

### PULLED BOTH WAYS

If the box is a dispenser and the mild is theatre and the rent is that triple, why does the hand still hover? Because fear holds it.

Not one fear. Two. Pulling opposite ways on the same arm.

First rope: "I cannot do it. I have tried twenty times. Patches, gum, promises on Mondays. I always crawl back. What if I try and fail again?"

I hear that voice because it was mine. Listen to what it proves. Every past try used the same wrong method — white knuckles and waiting, counting days, mourning a friend — and every past try taught you that you are weak. You are not weak. It takes a stubborn, wilful human to keep paying, coughing, hiding and still turning up for work and family. The method failed. You did not. The rope that says you will fail is braided from those old method-scars, and the scar-maker profits when you believe the scar is you.

Second rope, quieter, pulling the other way: "What if I do it — and life goes flat? Who am I on the break without my prop? At the table? With drink? With stress? Will I be a bore? Will I never enjoy a pause again? Will I lose me?"

That sounds like identity. It is inventory. Morning, meal, break — you have already stood in each without paper and felt feet, taste, air do their own work. The favourite was never the tube in the scene. The scene did the giving while the tube took the name. The fear of a flat life without is the same tutoring that told you mild meant safe. It paints the non-smoker day as grey so you will not look at your own stairs-breath and sour mouth and see the grey you already pay.

Two fears. One says you will fail to leave. One says you will succeed and be sorry. Both cannot be true. Both feel true in the same chest on the same afternoon. That is the clue.

Who benefits when you are pulled both ways? Not you. A man pulled both ways stands still. Standing still buys another pack.

Did you invent those ropes? Did you as a child fear a life without paper tubes? Did you as a teenager lie awake afraid you could not stop something you had never started? The ropes were put in your hands after the first thousand squirts, by the same bench that built the box and the same tutoring that sold the mild. One rope says try and you will fail. One rope says succeed and you will miss. Both ropes are held by the same hands that held the dispenser to your mouth.

BOTH ROPES BELONG TO THE TRAP.

See it as one picture and the pull collapses. There is not a fear of failure and a separate fear of success to solve one by one for the rest of your life. There is the tug-of-war of fear, and you are the rope only while you believe the want is yours.

Drop the maker's end. Feel what stays. The palm empty of cardboard. The mouth ready to taste. The stairs waiting to be climbed without counting. What is left when both ropes drop is ordinary daylight with your own hands in it, and lungs that remember what freedom feels like from the inside.

**SUMMARY**
- The pack is a day's supply box, and the tube is a squirt of drug through paper, not a personal pleasure.
- The maker's own planning words named the drug as the product and the cigarette as its packet.
- Vents and mild promises change the dial reading, not the human timetable, and no smoker owes thanks for a smoother curtain.
- The daily rent shows in mouth, chest and day-plan as sour coating, short breath and a timetable around the wrist.
- Failure fear and flat-life fear pull opposite ways so the hand stands still, and both ropes are held by the same bench that built the want.
