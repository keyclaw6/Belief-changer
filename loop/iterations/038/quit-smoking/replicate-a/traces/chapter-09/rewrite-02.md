Chapter 9
WHO BUILT THIS WANT

**IN THIS CHAPTER** — The pack weighed in the palm, the filter vents held to the light, the bathroom mirror, the stairs at midday, both ends of the rope, tomorrow with the same hours.

*You never invented this hunger — it was built for you, and fear is holding both ends of the rope.*

### THE BOX IN YOUR POCKET

You have felt feet, taste and air do their own work. Hold that baseline while we look at what was laid across it.

Take the pack out now and hold it in the palm while we look at it together.

Not to light it. Just to weigh it. Feel the cellophane crackle under the thumb. The flip-top click. The crest and the colour chosen to look like yours. The gold band. The clean white tips in a row. It sits in the hand like a personal thing. Like choice in a box.

Turn it over and count what it really is.

We bought it in the morning and counted the day by its numbers. One for the bus queue. One for the doorway. One for after food. One for the phone call that ran long. By night the box was light and the day was gone, and we bought another box to count tomorrow. No perfume is drunk to the last drop in a day. No favourite meal is eaten twenty times before dark. Only a dispenser is emptied on a timetable.

Look at the tube itself. Paper, filter, a pinch of leaf laced with a fast drug. The paper burns to carry. The filter paints a clean tip to hold between lips. The leaf is the carrier. Each draw pulls a measured squirt through that tube into lungs built for air, into blood, into brain in seconds, then the level sinks fast so the next squirt is called for within the hour. The bench that drew it does not count pleasures. It counts squirts per pack, packs per day, days per life.

In their own planning papers, written for each other and never meant for your eyes, their own scientists put it flat. The cigarette was not the product at all. The wrapper was the packet. The drug inside was the product.

Let that sit in the palm. The taste you chose, the brand you stayed loyal to, the mild you moved to when your throat burned — none of that was the product. The product was the powder that pulls the hand back every forty minutes. The rest was theatre to let you hold the tube without seeing the squirt.

“But I chose it. Nobody lifts my hand to my mouth.”

I know. I said the same for years. So answer from your own hand.

Did you choose the size of the squirt? Did you choose how fast it fades? Did you choose to want another one an hour later, and another after food, and another with drink? Did you choose the first one knowing it would teach your throat to want the twenty-thousandth?

You chose the first paper on phoney information — that it looked grown-up, calm, sociable, sharp. We all did. We were young, or pressed, or out with friends, and the tutoring said try. After that, the timetable chose. That box is the nicotine trap in cardboard.

Be angry at the right address. Not at yourself. You are not stupid. You were conned by men in rooms who measured breath and puff and day, and built a box to fit inside your day. Warm to you, always. Contempt belongs on the bench that built the want and called it yours.

Hold the pack a second longer and see the day inside it.

Twenty tubes stand for twenty returns to the pocket. Pocket to mouth, mouth to bin, bin to pocket again. Morning tube before feet touch floor. Mid-morning tube to break the sitting. Noon tube to close the plate. Afternoon tube to break the sitting again. Evening tube with drink. Night tube to close the day. The box does not ask what the day held. It only asks that the hand come back. Joy, argument, boredom, work done, work avoided — all are ground into the same motion. That is not a portrait of taste. That is a portrait of a level topped and sunk, topped and sunk.

Ask whose handwriting is on that timetable.

Did you as a boy write, I shall pause my life twenty times a day to stand apart and burn? Did you as a girl write, I shall carry paper and fire everywhere and check the pocket before I check the keys? We wrote no such page. The page was written for us, printed in gold and cellophane, and slipped into the pocket until the pocket felt naked without it.

The pack is not a companion. It is a day’s supply box. The tube is not a pleasure. It is a squirt of drug through paper.

### THE THEATRE OF MILD

When the throat burned and the morning cough thickened, many of us did what seemed sensible. We moved to milds. Lights. Low-tar. The white tip with the tiny holes. The promise of less.

Hold one of those tips to the light. Around the filter you will see a ring of pinprick vents, too neat to be accident. On the machine that tests them, air slips in through those holes and the dial reads low. Low tar. Low nicotine. Mild.

Now put human lips and fingers on the same tip.

Lips close over the holes. Fingers cover the ring without knowing it. The chest pulls harder for the missing pull. The puff goes deeper, held a second longer. The body seeks the level it was taught to seek, and the hand and mouth do the seeking without asking you.

Machine smoke is not human smoke. The dial does not have lips. It does not have a morning throat that needs its level. It does not learn to pull harder when the squirt is thinned. You do.

Not to the same millimetre in every smoker — no two pairs of lips sit the same, no two chests pull the same — but the pattern was known on the bench before the pack ever reached your pocket. Thin the squirt and the smoker will work the tube harder to find it. That is not failure of will. That is the taught answer of a taught hunger to a smaller squirt.

Ask the mild years plainly.

If milds were milder, why did the day still count twenty? If the filter watched over you, why did the cough stay, the throat stay raw, the morning throat still call for the first tube before feet touched floor? If low-tar meant low want, why did want never go low?

Because the tube was still a dispenser, only dressed in white to look innocent. The holes were not for your lungs. They were for the dial.

That tutoring is the Smokescreen at work.

It told you taste mattered, brand mattered, strength mattered. It told you to argue about flavour while the level rose and sank underneath. It told you a smoother con was a kinder con. While we debated menthol or Virginia, mild or full, the bench counted only one thing. Did the level fade fast enough to bring the hand back to the pocket?

You never smoked flavour. You fed a level. The flavour talk was the curtain. Behind it, the squirt went in, faded in minutes, and called again.

See how complete the curtain was.

We learned the language of connoisseurs for a thing we never tasted. We said smooth, harsh, light, strong. We held the box out like a card. We tapped the tube, we rolled it, we admired the ash. We did all the small rituals that make a thing feel chosen. None of those rituals changed the powder. They only kept the eyes on the fingers while the level did its work underneath.

“I can tell the difference. I like mine.”

Can you? From your own mouth, blind, with the crest covered, could you pick yours from three others while the level clamoured? And if you could, what would it prove? That one curtain is blue and another red does not change what stands behind both. The bench knew you would defend the curtain because to defend the curtain is to stop looking behind it.

The throat kept score while the curtain stayed drawn.

### WHAT IT LEAVES ON YOU

Stand with me in front of the bathroom mirror tomorrow morning before the first tube, and look at what the box has rented on you.

Mouth first. Run the tongue over teeth. Sour, coated, grey at the back. Last night’s final tube still camped on the tongue. The kiss you turned away from. The mint chewed in the car with the window down, spraying, hiding, scrubbing the jacket and calling it fresh. Warm to you — I did the same dance, garage door up, mints, lies by omission to people I loved. Harsh on the need that made a grown man hide like a schoolboy. The mouth was not made to wake coated. The tube coated it.

Chest next. Climb the stairs you climbed yesterday. Halfway up, the pull shortens. The legs are willing. The chest is tight. A neighbour who never smoked walks beside you talking in full sentences while you ration breath to reach the landing. No lecture in this. Just your own stairs telling you what twenty squirts a day lay in the pipes. The body was built to carry you up without thought. The tube taught it to notice every step.

And the chain. This is the one we feel least and pay most. Watch the day you lived last week. Leave the house — check the pack. Long meeting — plan the doorway. Meal with non-smokers — watch the clock for the step outside. Train, plane, cinema, hospital corridor — first thought not where to sit but where to stand to burn. Rain, wind, cold — shivering in doorways while the talk goes on inside without you. Money counted in tens a week, hundreds a month, thousands a year, for the privilege of standing apart.

Three marks, same owner. Sour mouth in the mirror. Short breath on the stairs. A timetable around the wrist tighter than any watch.

Live in that picture for a second without flinching. I am not painting it to frighten you into changing. I do not want you to change from fright, and fright never kept any smoker quit past Friday. Fright makes the hand shake and the hand then reaches for the very thing that frightened it. I want you to see whose initials are on the marks. They are not yours. They are the rent slip for a dispenser you carry for someone else’s counting house.

You are stale, breathless and chained.

Say it without shame, as a man names a room he is walking out of. Stale in the mouth that was meant to taste. Breathless in the chest that was meant to climb without counting. Chained in the day that was meant to be yours hour by hour. That triple is not you. That is what twenty small squirts a day lay on a body while giving nothing back.

Look again at the mirror and the stairs and the wrist together.

The mouth did not sour because you are careless. It soured because grey was laid on it twenty times a day. The chest did not tighten because you are unfit by nature. It tightened because pipes built for air were used as a chimney. The day did not shrink because you are poor at planning. It shrank because a box in the pocket cut it into forty-minute slices. Each mark has the maker’s fingerprints on it, not yours.

Does that picture accuse you? It accuses the bench. You carried the box in good faith. They built the box in full knowledge. You hid in the garage because you loved people and did not want the smell on them. They punched vents in a filter knowing lips would cover them. Keep the blame where it belongs and the shame drops away by itself.

### PULLED BOTH WAYS

If the box is a dispenser and the mild is theatre and the rent is that triple, why does the hand still hover? Because fear holds it.

Not one fear. Two. Pulling opposite ways on the same arm.

First rope. “I cannot do it. I have tried twenty times. Patches, gum, promises on Mondays. I always crawl back. What if I attempt again and fail again?”

I hear that voice because it was mine. Listen to what it proves. Every past attempt used the same wrong method — white knuckles and waiting, counting days, mourning a friend — and every past attempt taught you that you are weak. You are not weak. It takes a stubborn, wilful human to keep paying, coughing, hiding and still turning up for work and family. The method failed. You did not. The rope that says you will fail is braided from those old method-scars, and the scar-maker gains when you believe the scar is you.

Second rope, quieter, pulling the other way. “What if I do it — and life goes flat? Who am I on the doorway without my prop? At the table? With drink? With pressure? Will I be dull? Will I never take pleasure in a pause again? Will I lose me?”

That sounds like identity. It is inventory. Morning, meal and doorway — you have already stood in each without paper and felt feet, taste, air do their own work. The floor held you. The plate closed. The pause rested because work stopped and legs moved and eyes looked far. The favourite was never the tube in the scene. The scene did the giving while the tube took the name. The fear of a flat life without is the same tutoring that told you mild meant safe. It paints the non-smoker day as grey so you will not look at your own sour mouth and short stairs and see the grey you already pay.

Two fears. One says you will fail to leave. One says you will succeed and be sorry. Both cannot be true. Both feel true in the same chest on the same afternoon. That is the clue.

Who gains when you are pulled both ways? Not you. A man pulled both ways stands still. Standing still buys another pack.

Did you invent those ropes? Did you as a child fear a life without paper tubes? Did you as a lad lie awake afraid you could not stop something you had never started? The ropes were put in your hands after the first thousand squirts, by the same bench that built the box and the same tutoring that sold the mild. One rope says attempt and you will fail. One rope says succeed and you will miss. Both ropes are held by the same hands that held the dispenser to your mouth.

BOTH ROPES BELONG TO THE TRAP.

See it as one picture and the pull collapses. There is not a fear of failure and a separate fear of success to solve one by one for the rest of your life. There is the tug-of-war of fear, and you are the rope only while you believe the want is yours.

Drop the maker’s end. Feel what stays. The palm without cardboard. The mouth ready to taste. The stairs waiting to be climbed without counting. What is left when both ropes drop is ordinary daylight with your own hands in it, and lungs that remember from the inside how morning air tastes at the back door.

### STANDING STILL BUYS ANOTHER PACK

Stay with that stillness one minute longer, because stillness is not neutral. Stillness has a timetable of its own. Let us walk it forward into tomorrow with the seller named at each turn, so you see what standing still does while the ropes ache.

Morning. The alarm cuts. The hand finds the pack on the table before the eyes are open. One tube gone before feet touch floor. The mouth wakes sour and is made more sour. The chest takes grey before water. The mirror shows last night still camped on the tongue. You wash, you dress, you tell yourself today you will think about it. Thinking about it means carrying it.

Bus stop. The wind is sharp. Three minutes to wait. The hand goes to the pocket without asking. Click, light, draw. Two minutes of standing apart from the queue, smoke in the eyes, fingers cold around fire. The bus comes. The tube is dropped half-burned. Half a coin burned for nothing, because the timetable called, not the need.

Mid-morning. The screen blurs. The shoulders climb. The doorway calls. Out to the wall with the others. Light, draw, stare. Ten minutes of talk about nothing while the level tops and starts to sink. Back in. The throat dry. The fingers smelling on the keys. The mind already dating the next doorway. The work waited. The box did not.

Noon. The plate emptied. The taste still in the mouth, salt and butter and heat. Before the taste fades the hand is out. The taste is burned under grey. The meal that was closed now needs a mint, a drink, a wash. The afternoon starts coated.

Afternoon. The phone call runs long. The voice presses. The hand reaches while the receiver is still warm. First puff credited with calm while the problem papers sit untouched on the desk. Nothing on the papers moved. Only the level topped. An hour later the papers still sit and the hand reaches again.

Evening. The shop on the corner. The bell over the door. The hand puts coins down. The seller — not a friend, not a host, a till — slides another day’s supply box across. The factory counts another day. The bench that drew the vents and wrote the packet-not-product memo counts another day. You carry the box home like bread, only bread feeds and this only rents.

That is what standing still buys. Not a pause in the argument. A purchase. Another box, another twenty squirts, another day cut into slices, another set of fingerprints on mouth and chest and wrist. Neutrality is not standing outside the shop. Neutrality is walking in at six o’clock and putting coins down.

Ask the stillness plainly.

If waiting protected you, why did waiting never lower the count? If putting off the look protected the purse, why did the purse thin every week you waited? If holding both ropes kept you safe, why did safe feel like checking the pocket before the keys, like planning doorways before meetings, like hiding from people you love?

Because both ropes are held by the same seller. One rope says, do not attempt, you will fail like before. The other says, do not succeed, you will lose yourself. While you honour both, the feet do not move and the hand keeps buying. The bench does not need you to love the box. It only needs you to stand still long enough to need another box.

Now drop both ends at once and watch tomorrow rewrite itself with the same hours and a different palm.

Morning. The alarm cuts. The hand finds nothing because nothing was bought to be found. Feet touch floor. Cold lino wakes the soles. Water wakes the face. Kettle hums. The back door opens two inches. The chest takes the yard air all the way down. The mouth is yesterday’s mouth, still a little coated, but no fresh grey is laid on it. The tongue comes back by noon because nothing burns the taste off. That is not waiting. That is daylight doing its work while the pocket stays light.

Bus stop. The wind is sharp. Three minutes to wait. The hands stay in pockets, warming each other. The eyes watch the street, the roofs, the number plate. No stepping apart. No half-burned coin. The bus comes. You board with the queue, not after it.

Mid-morning. The screen blurs. The shoulders climb. The chair pushes back. The corridor, the stairs, the handle. The wall. Cold on the cheeks. Breath showing. Neck rolling left, right. Jaw unclenching. Eyes looking far instead of near. Ten minutes where nobody asks for anything. Back in with legs looser and eyes holding far seeing into near work. Nothing tapped, nothing flicked, nothing timed.

Noon. The plate emptied. Last bite chewed slow. Fork down. The clink on the plate. Taste staying where it belongs. Water lifting and carrying. Talk without watching the clock for the step outside. The mouth clean enough to taste the second wave when you swallow. The afternoon starts without coating.

Afternoon. The phone call runs long. The voice presses. The receiver goes down. The papers sit. The hand does not move to the pocket because the pocket holds no box. The shoulders drop because the call ended, not because a tube burned. The problem sits untouched — as it always did after a puff — only now you see it sits untouched, and you pick up the pen. That is not courage. That is seeing.

Evening. The shop on the corner. The bell over the door. You walk past with coins still in the pocket for bread, for tea, for a paper. The till does not slide. The factory does not count. The bench that measured your puff does not get another day of measurement. The palm stays without cardboard and the fingers find the keys, the mug, the sleeve of a child, without anything held aside for fire.

Which tomorrow was yours? Both used the same alarm, the same bus, the same screen, the same plate, the same shop. One counted the day in squirts for the seller’s book. The other kept the day in hands, mouth, chest and hours for its owner. The difference was never will or nerve. The difference was seeing who built the want.

I stood that shop evening myself with the old tug tapping thin at the pocket. There and then thinning while I stood. I bought tea. I carried it home in the same hand that carried boxes for years. The street looked the same. The coins felt different. No drum roll. No white knuckles. Just a man walking past a till that had counted him long enough.

9. SEE THE SELLER BEHIND THE SMOKE
Remember who built the trap and why.

**SUMMARY**
- The pack is a day’s supply box, and the tube is a squirt of drug through paper, not a personal pleasure.
- The maker’s own planning words named the drug as the product and the cigarette as its packet.
- Vents and mild promises change the dial reading, not the human timetable, and no smoker owes thanks for a smoother curtain.
- The daily rent shows in mouth, chest and day-plan as sour coating, short breath and a timetable around the wrist.
- Failure fear and flat-life fear pull opposite ways so the hand stands still, and both ropes are held by the same bench that built the want.
- Standing still is not neutral, it buys another box tomorrow, and a palm without cardboard keeps the same day for its owner.
