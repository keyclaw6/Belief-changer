Chapter 4
THE CALM THAT CREATES THE STORM

IN THIS CHAPTER
The phone put down too hard, the unpaid papers, the hand already moving, three minutes watched puff by puff, the hour after, the man at the next desk

*It never took care of the strain — it only quieted for a minute the tug it had lit, while the trouble sat exactly where you left it.*

### IT TAKES CARE OF IT

Say it in your own words, as we all said it.

"If anything stressful happens in my life, a cigarette takes care of it."

I know that sentence from the inside. I lived inside it for years. The phone slams. The voice on the other end leaves a sting in your ear. Bills spread across the table. A school letter. A sharp word with the woman you love. The shoulders climb toward your ears. The stomach knots. And the hand is already moving to the pocket before you have chosen.

I am not mocking that reach. That minute after lighting feels real as sensation. Heat at the throat. Shoulders dropping a fraction. A small pause in the noise in your head. I felt it a thousand times, and I credited it exactly as you credit it.

I am asking you to watch with a witness's eyes what that minute does and what it leaves untouched.

We all learned to call that minute calm. We all learned to call the dose a friend that steps in when life steps hard. Did we ever check the order, or did we only feel the loosening and sign the credit over?

### WATCH THE THREE MINUTES

Take the desk you know best. Yesterday, or this morning.

The row ends. You put the phone down too hard. Your jaw is tight. On the desk the papers sit exactly as they sat: the unpaid bill, the form half-filled, the note with the name you must call back. The tightness is in your chest and in the room.

Watch the hand. It does not wait for thought. Tap. Up. Cup against the air. Light. First hot drag. Shoulders drop for twenty seconds. Eyes narrow. You stare at nothing.

Now watch the next three minutes without looking away.

You drag. You cough a little and cover it. You reach for the ashtray. You miss it. You look at the ash on the bill. You think about the smell on your coat. You think about whether you have enough left for the evening. You stub it halfway because the last third tastes stale and sorry. You sit back.

Where are the papers? Exactly where they were. Where is the row? Word for word where it was. Where is the bill? Unpaid. Where is the call you must make? Unmade.

Tell me honestly, as a witness and not as a judge.

Did the row shrink while you puffed? Did one paper sort itself? Did the tightness stay loosened after the stub, or did it return with the same weight and a stale mouth laid over it?

You know the honest answers because you have lived them a thousand times. The dose did not touch the papers. It touched the tug low down for a minute and left the papers to wait.

That mistiming is everything. The strain came from the row. The tug came from the last dose wearing off. The two met in the same chest at the same minute, and the Smokescreen pasted one label over both: stress. You lit to answer the row and you quieted the tug, and you thanked the dose for handling the row.

The order is back to front.

### IT HITS IN SECONDS AND FADES IN MINUTES

The fact is plain, and I state it as fact.

When smoke enters the lungs the nicotine hits the brain within seconds. That is why the first drag registers so fast. And the acute effect fades within minutes. That is why the hand goes back forty minutes later, and why strain an hour later seems to call for another.

Not to the same second for every smoker, but quick to come and quick to go for the way we smoke. That short rise and short fade is not a gift that sorts life. It is replenishment. The Nipper, that tiny physical whine we all feed, quiets briefly when fed and then whines again. The quiet feels like the row easing because the two discomforts arrived together.

Picture it as it is. You bump your head and someone hands you a cold coin to press on it. The cold distracts for a minute. The bump remains. Would you say the coin healed the bump? Would you carry coins everywhere and fear a day without coins in your pocket?

We did exactly that with paper tubes. A brief cooling of our own withdrawal laid over an untouched row, and we called the cooling a cure.

I stood at that same desk. Argument done. Hand shaking a little. I lit and stared at the wall and told myself, "There. I can think now." Then I looked down. I had not thought. I had puffed and fidgeted and ashed. The sentence I needed came later, when the air cleared and I returned to the page in spite of the interruption, not because of it.

Did the dose ever speak to the man who insulted you? Did it ever pay a penny of the bill? Did it ever add one fact to your head about what to do next? No. You did whatever facing you did. It did the puffing.

It never took care of it. It took from you while it sat there.

### THE PAUSE STEADIES, THE PUFF INTERRUPTS

I know this is hard to accept at first. A lifetime of credit does not move in one minute. So let us stay at that desk a little longer and separate what has been glued together.

We confuse the pause after interruption with help. The non-smoker pauses. We puff. The pause steadies. The puff interrupts. Then we credit the puff for the pause.

Look closer at that same stress-desk minute, puff by puff, breath by breath, while the dose is still in your hand. I want you to see the two threads running side by side in the same three minutes, because you have felt both a thousand times without naming which was which.

Thread one is the pause any human body uses when shaken. The phone goes down. The jaw is tight. The chest is high and short. If you leave the body alone for sixty seconds, this is what it does on its own. The shoulders climb, hold, then drop a fraction without instruction. The breath comes short at the top, then finds the bottom again on its own. The eyes lose focus, stare at the window frame, at the grain in the desk, at nothing. The hand rubs the jaw. The pen turns over twice. The mind goes blank for a few seconds and then comes back a little slower, a little wider. That is not a technique. That is what shoulders and lungs do when you do not crowd them. You did it as a boy before you ever lit. You do it in sleep. You do it when no packet is near.

Thread two is the dose laid over that pause. The hand leaves the desk. Tap out the stick. Tap down the tobacco. Up to the mouth. Cup against the air. Lighter flare. First hot scratch at the back of the throat. Nose sting. Eyes water a fraction. Chest pulls in smoke that was never meant to be there. Shoulders drop for twenty seconds — and here is the con — they would have dropped anyway, but now the drop gets signed over to the flare.

Then watch what the dose charges for that signed-over drop.

Second drag. Small cough you swallow. Throat heat. You shift in the chair to get comfortable and cannot get comfortable because one hand is now busy holding and tapping. You reach for the ashtray without looking and your eyes leave the papers to follow the ash. A flake lands on the bill. You brush it. Grey smear. You think about the smell settling into the sleeve. You think about the window and whether to open it and whether the draught will bring the row back into the room. You look at the stick burning down. You hurry your thinking because the stick is burning down. You take a bigger puff to make it count before it burns. Hotter scratch. Duller head.

Third minute. The taste turns. The first second had heat and sharpness. Now it is paper and ash. The tongue dulls. The mouth dries. You think about the packet. How many left for the evening. Whether the shop on the corner stays open late. Whether she will comment when you come out. You stub it halfway because smoking it to the end would be pure chore, and the chore leaves a stale end on the tongue. You sit back. Lost minutes. Four minutes gone in which no paper moved. The breath that wanted to go to the bottom has spent four minutes pulling in and pushing out smoke. The shoulders that wanted to drop on their own have spent four minutes lifting to hold, tap, ash, stub.

Now separate the threads with kind eyes.

Which part steadied? The sixty seconds of staring, jaw rubbing, breath finding the bottom, eyes resting — the pause any worried clerk uses at the next desk with his arms folded. Which part interrupted? The tapping, lighting, coughing, ashing, smearing, counting, hurrying, tasting stale, stubbing — the errands the dose brought with it.

You felt both at once and called both calm. That is the whole Smokescreen in miniature. It takes a human pause that steadies on its own and a paper tube that interrupts, glues them together in the same three minutes, and teaches you to praise the tube for the pause.

Try this seeing tomorrow without changing your smoking. Only notice the order inside the minute.

Row ends. Phone down. Tight chest. Hand moves first, as it always does. Notice the thought you were holding about what to say back — how it scatters the instant the lighter flares. Not because the row got bigger, but because the ritual barged in. Lighter, drag, ash. You lose the thread you were holding. You spend the dose looking for the thread again. When you find a corner of it after the stub, you say, "There — the ciggie cleared my head." Would you thank the wind for bringing back the letter it blew off your desk?

Notice the breath. Before the light, the breath is short and high. Anyone's breath is short and high after a sharp voice. After the first drag, the breath is still short and high, only now it is short and high around smoke. After the stub, the breath coughs once, clears, then goes low again on its own because lungs go low when left alone. The low breath after was not made by the tube. It arrived when the tube stopped getting in the way. The dose did not bring the low breath. It rented it out for three minutes and handed back what was left and asked for thanks.

Notice the shoulders. Before the light, they climb toward the ears. That is what shoulders do under a sting. During the puffing, they lift and turn with holding and tapping. They cannot drop fully while one arm is up and the eyes are on the ash. After the stub, both hands are on the desk again, the eyes are back on the papers, and the shoulders drop a fraction because there is nothing left to hold. The drop belongs to putting down, not to lighting up. The non-smoker puts nothing up and puts nothing down. He frowns, he breathes, his shoulders drop on the same clock without ash to watch.

Notice the mind. Before the light, the mind is tight but present. It holds the sting and the next step together: what he said, what you must do. During the puffing, the mind leaves both and attends to small errands: draw, cough, tap, smell, count. It is busy but not present. After the stub, the mind returns to sting and step, a little dulled, a little later, with less time. We call that return clarity. It is only return. Clarity was there before the match, left during the match, and came back when the match went out. The tube did not add a thought. It borrowed attention for three minutes and returned it thinner.

Ask yourself with kind eyes, as I asked myself at my own littered desk:

If the tube steadied nerves, why do the heaviest smokers shake most when the packet runs low? If the tube cleared thought, why does thought return clearest after the stub, when the head clears? If the tube handled strain, why does the same strain return an hour later with an extra tug laid over it?

You already know. The strain was yours to meet. The tug was the nicotine trap calling for its next feed. The dose answered the second and claimed it answered the first.

Here is the separation to carry into the next urge. When the next sharp minute comes — phone hard down, letter opened, voice raised in the next room — two hungers will arrive together again. One is the sting in the chest that belongs to the news. It asks for pause, breath, frown, a minute of staring, then the next paper. The other is the low whine that belongs to the last dose fading. It asks for tap and flare. The brainwashing you and I were fed calls both by one name and points both to the pocket.

Watch them as two. Feel the sting and say, "There is the row." Feel the whine and say, "There is the feed-call." The sting eases when you pause and breathe and let shoulders drop, as shoulders do. The whine quiets for minutes when fed and then returns, as whines do. One belongs to life and passes with looking. The other belongs to the con and passes only to return until starved. Once seen as two, the next urge no longer reads as proof you need a tube to cope. It reads as what it is: a feed-call arriving at the same minute as trouble, hoping to be mistaken for trouble.

That is why the minute felt like help and was not. Help would have left the papers lighter. Help would have left the breath deeper. Help would have left the mind fuller. This left papers untouched, breath thinner, mind later, mouth stale, packet lighter, evening count shorter. A friend who leaves you thinner each visit is not a friend. We were conned, not foolish — you and I both — by a grip that loosened briefly and asked to be called kindness.

It causes the aggravation. It does not relieve it.

### THE HOUR AFTER THE STUB

Now do not stop the film at the stub. That is where we always stopped it. We lit, we stared, we stubbed, we told ourselves the worst had passed. Keep watching the same desk into the next hour, because this is where the calm shows what it creates.

You sit back. Stale on the tongue. The papers still there. Four minutes gone. The bill still unpaid. The name still to be rung back. Only now you have less time to do it in, and a thinner head to do it with.

Notice the clock. The row took five minutes of your morning. The dose took four more on top. The next appointment has moved four minutes nearer while nothing moved on the desk. That nearer clock tightens the chest a fraction by itself. Is that tightness from the row? No. It is interest charged by the interruption. You borrowed four minutes from a pressed morning to feed a whine, and the morning is now more pressed.

Notice the mouth. Before the light, your mouth was clean. After the stub, it is dry and faintly sour. Water tastes of ash. Breath feels short at the top. You cough once to clear it and the cough reminds you of the stairs last night. That small reminder brings its own prick of worry to lay over the row. Before, you had one sting to meet. Now you have sting plus stale plus prick. Which of those did the dose remove?

Notice the hands. Before the light, both hands were free for papers. During the puffing, one hand was taken for holding and tapping. After the stub, the fingers smell. You rub them. You sniff the sleeve. You wonder if the room smells. You half-open the window. Cold air comes in. The papers lift and you put a mug on them. More small errands. Each errand takes ten seconds of attention away from the call you must make. Ten seconds here. Ten seconds there. Soon the call feels bigger for having been put off, and you tell yourself you are putting it off because it is difficult. Are you? Or are you putting it off because the desk is now littered with ash-watch and smell-watch and count-watch that were not there before the match?

"I need one to calm down before I make that call." You have said that sentence. We all have. Play it as a witness.

What does before mean? Ten minutes of puffing and stale and counting, during which the call grows in your head from a two-minute job into a confrontation. The voice on the other end grows sharper while you wait. Your opening line, which was clear before the light, grows blurred. You rehearse it twice and lose it once because the head is a little dulled from smoke. You take a bigger drag to sharpen up before you dial, and the bigger drag dulls a fraction more and dries the throat you need for speaking. Then you stub and dial with less time, duller head, drier throat, and a flutter that you read as proof you are not ready. You put the receiver down again and say you will ring after the next one.

Do you see what happened? The dose did not prepare you for the call. It pushed the call away and made the pushing feel like preparation.

Then the next sting arrives on top of the delayed first one. The letter you had not opened. The second sharp voice in the next room. Life does that on pressed days — trouble comes in pairs. Before, you would have met the second sting with the first sting still met or at least faced. Now you meet it with the first sting unmet, four minutes shorter, mouth stale, packet lighter, and a fresh low whine starting again low down because the last feed has already faded.

Two hungers again, only louder. The new row shouts. The new feed-call whines underneath it. Because you answered the last pair with a light, the brain points to the pocket faster this time. The hand moves quicker. Less hesitation. More urgency. You tell yourself, "I am really stressed now. I really need it." Of course the need feels bigger. You have two rows now where you had one, and two fades where you had one. The second dose will do what the first did — quiet the whine for minutes while both rows wait — and charge a second four minutes, a second stale, a second count.

Watch that count grow across a stressful day. Morning packet full. After the first row, one gone and nineteen left. After the second, two gone and the mind doing sums. By midday, after three rows and three habit smokes between rows, half the box gone and the afternoon still to face. Now a new worry joins the old worries: will this last till night? Shall I slip out at lunch to buy? What will she say if I buy another? That evening-count worry was never in the original row. The original row was about a bill and a voice. The count worry was built puff by puff by using the off-switch as the answer. Each use laid its own small errand of ash and smell and number over the papers, until the desk holds two piles — the life pile that was there at nine, and the dose pile you built over it by twelve.

And watch the temper shorten. Before the light, you were sharp but present. You could hear the other voice. After two or three cycles of interruption and hurry, you are late and stale and behind. The next voice finds you less patient. You snap where you would have answered. You snap, then feel sorry, then tell yourself the day is getting on top of you and you need a minute with a dose to settle. The snap, which came from hurry and dullness the doses helped build, is now used as proof you need another. The storm feeds itself.

Ask yourself, as a witness:

Did the morning get calmer with each use, or tighter? Did the papers get fewer, or more backed up? Did the head get clearer, or more crowded with small counts? Did the hand go to the pocket less urgently, or more urgently as the box thinned?

You know. Tighter. More backed up. More crowded. More urgently. That tightening across the hour is not life being cruel. It is the off-switch charging for its brief quiet. One puff leaves the row untouched. Repeated puffs leave the row untouched and the hour poorer — less time, less breath, less patience, more errands, more whine to answer at the next sting.

This is what I mean when I say the calm creates the storm. Not as a slogan. As desk detail you can check tomorrow. First use: row untouched. Second use: two rows untouched plus count worry. Third use: three rows untouched plus hurry plus snap plus smell-watch. Each quiet minute is real as sensation and false as help, and each false help makes the next true strain harder to meet with a clear head and full hands.

I am not saying the row was small. Some rows are large. Bills are real. Sharp voices sting. I am saying the dose never shrank one of them by a hair, and then added its own weather on top, and then pointed to the worsened weather as the reason to light again. That is not a friend stepping in when life steps hard. That is a guest who eats while you worry and then tells you the house is bare because you worried.

Once you see the hour, you cannot credit the minute again in the same way. The minute still tingles. The heat still comes. The shoulders still drop for twenty seconds. But you know what the twenty seconds cost across sixty minutes — papers unmoved, time shortened, head dulled, temper thinned, box thinned, next hunger already forming. A cure that leaves the illness exactly where it was and leaves the patient poorer for the visit is not a cure. It is a fee taken at the door of trouble.

### WHAT THE MAN AT THE NEXT DESK DOES

Look sideways, as a witness.

The man at the next desk gets the same call. Same sharp voice. Same heat in the face. He puts the phone down. He frowns. He rubs his jaw. He stares out of the window for a minute. He turns the pen over. He does not leave his chair for cold air. He does not lay ash over his tongue. His shoulders climb and then drop on their own because shoulders do that when you leave them alone.

Is his row less real for lack of a stick? Does his bill stay unpaid forever because he did not light? Does he crack in two by evening? No. He frowns, he breathes, he picks up the next paper. Or he waits a while, as worried minds do, and then picks it up.

And you as a boy did the same. Before you ever lit, you took scoldings and scrapes and exams and sour days without hot paper in your hand. You shook. You cried. You sulked. You got through. Did your nerves fail for lack of a dose then? Did you need feeding every forty minutes to face a hard hour?

We were taught to read that boy as untested and this man as tested, as if the tube had become part of the nerves. The nerves never changed. Only the whine was added, and then the whine was taught to speak in the voice of the nerves. Remove the whine and the nerves are the boy's nerves still — shaken, steadying, going on.

I want you to keep that next desk in front of you when the old voice comes. "You need it to cope." Watch him cope without it. Not bravely. Not with method. With frown and stare and pen turned over. That is coping. Frown, pause, breath, next paper. No flare needed. No ash watched. No count for the evening. The pause did the steadying. The man did the facing. The tube, where it was present, did the interrupting and took the bow.

Who did the steadying on your hardest day before you smoked? You did. Who does the steadying at the next desk today? He does. Who will do the steadying at your desk when the threads are seen as two? You will. You always did. It was only ever sneaking a ride on your own pause and charging you in breath and money for the ride.

I have led hundreds out of this same corner, and the ones who walk out easiest are not the strongest-willed or the sternest with themselves. They are the ones who keep an open mind and do what the book asks as we go, page by page, without adding private tests.

All you have to do is follow all the instructions.

**SUMMARY**
- The taken-care-of minute never touched the row, the bill, or the papers — they waited exactly where they were.
- The fast hit and fast fade only quieted a need the last dose had left — the quiet was replenishment, not help.
- The steadiness belonged to the pause any body uses — breath low, shoulders dropped, eyes rested.
- The interruption belonged to the dose — ash, cough, counting, stale taste, lost minutes.
- The next urge reads as two hungers at once — trouble to face and a feed-call to see through.
- Hour by hour the off-switch use tightens the day — less time, duller head, thinner patience, more counting, and the next sting harder to meet.
