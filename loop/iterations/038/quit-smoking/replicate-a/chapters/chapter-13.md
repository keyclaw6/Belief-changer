Chapter 13
MORNINGS, SHOPS AND ORDINARY DAYS

*You do not get through an ordinary day now — you live it whole and it carries you forward by itself.*

Feet find the cold kitchen floor before the hand finds anything now, and the kettle sings for tea that tastes of itself.

You will go to the shop today, as you always do, and that is where the day shows you what has changed without asking you to prove anything.

### THE SHOP QUEUE

Milk. Bread. A paper. The queue moves its slow shuffle toward the till.

We all know that wall behind the counter — the shutter, the rows, the bright packs lined like tins. We used to read it the way we read a menu, eye flicking to our brand, throat giving a little pull, hand already rehearsing coins. The smoker ahead buys twenty and the foil glints and for half a second the old film runs.

Stand in that queue as you are now and watch what actually happens.

You see the shutter. You hear the beep of the till. You feel your feet on the lino and the basket light in your hand. No hand moves by itself. No arithmetic starts. If a thin pull comes at all, it is brief, an empty tug under the ribs that passes while the queue moves one shuffle forward. You do not argue with it. You do not lecture it. You let the queue move and it moves past.

I know that moment at the till. I stood there the second day, basket in hand, eyes level with the wall I had paid thousands to. For years I had told myself those packs held my reward, my pause, my little parties after work done and errands done. Seen awake, they held paper tubes packed to quiet for minutes the edginess the last tube had planted. The party had been the work done, the drive done, the paragraph done. That understanding is settled now. You do not need to rehearse it in the queue. You buy what you came for.

Put the coins down. Take the change. The girl at the till does not look up twice. The man behind you reaches past for his lottery ticket. The shutter clatters when someone buys. It is all utterly ordinary, and that is the point. Nothing in you leans forward. Nothing in you steps back. You are neither resisting nor missing. You are shopping.

Walk out with the bag in either hand — no transfer needed, no cupping a flame in a doorway. The bell over the door rings the same behind you. The street smells of rain on pavement and bus fumes and bakery. Notice how much arrives when nothing is laid over it. A bus sighs at the stop. A child drags a scooter. A woman laughs into her phone. You hear it all because you did not step aside from any of it.

Someone lights ahead of you at the bus stop and steps half aside into the cold edge with the old hollowed hand around flame. Watch with clear eyes and keep your own judgment. Shoulders up. Draw tight. Eyes on when the next one can come. You stay where you are, bag in hand, mouth clear, no slice cut out of the wait. You do not feel superior. You do not feel deprived. You feel what you feel when you watch someone queue for something you no longer queue for — a small warmth that you are already outside it.

That bus ride is yours in a way it was not before. We used to ride it doing sums — how long till the stop, where can I stand, will there be shelter, can I last. Now you sit by the window and look out. Shops pass. Rain freckles the glass. The bag sits on your knees. Your hands lie open. One stop. Two stops. No timetable in the pocket. No bulge to pat. Flat cloth means nothing to count.

Get off at your stop with everyone else. Walk the last street home with the bag bumping your leg. The house is as you left it. The key turns the same. Put the milk away. Fold the bag. Put the paper on the table for later. The morning errand is done as errands are done, and it leaves no aftertaste.

### THE ELEVEN O'CLOCK STEP

Mid-morning comes with its old cue. Work pauses. Chairs push back. Someone says air.

We used to hear that word as a bell. Break meant doorway. Doorway meant dose. Three minutes outside, talk interrupted, coffee cooling inside while we burned paper and called the quiet focus. Then back in with drier mouth and tighter chest, already rehearsing the next one.

Take the break as it is now.

Carry your cup out if you like. Stand on the step. Feel the air on the face without heat laid over it. Both hands open — one for the cup, one for the rail — no ash to mind on the sleeve, no butt to grind. Drink. Let the drink tell the truth, bright because the tongue was clear from the night before. Look at the street. Buses. Pigeons. A delivery van double-parked. Ordinary, complete, asking nothing to be added.

Talk if talk comes. We used to miss the middle of every story, stepping out while the punchline landed inside. Now you hear the long story whole. You laugh at the punchline the first time because you did not miss the middle of it outside. You fetch another cup with either hand. No counting how many left for the afternoon. No planning where to stand.

If the tug whispers — just step out with us, keep us company — hear it for what it is. Not your reason. The old script arriving pre-labelled. You answer it not by effort but by sight: the step is company already. The cup is warm already. The pause is yours already. You do not need to stand apart to belong to the pause. You are in it.

Go back in when you go back in. Move one line of work with a pen. One answer written. One message returned in plain words. That one moved line touches the thing that made the morning busy. The papers sat untouched after every old step-out exactly as they sit now. The difference is you stayed to touch them with a clear mouth and both hands free.

By twelve the morning lies whole from kettle to lunch, uncut by doorways. No stinging mint on coating. No splitting yourself into the one who knows and the one who hides. Time kept. Taste kept. Hands kept. Temper kept.

Lunch is lunch. Sit down. Eat slowly. Taste food following food without grey between bites. We used to hurry the meal to get to the doorway after it, cutting the end short for the paper that would coat what we had just tasted. Now the meal closes itself. Plate empty. Cup finished. Conversation running on because no one has left the table. You wipe your mouth, push the chair back, and the afternoon begins without a toll paid at the door.

### WHEN THE THOUGHT DROPS BY

It will drop by. Not as a storm. As a passing thought.

Afternoon. A doorway. A laugh from the smoking corner. That extended hand on the patio with its shining offer, remembered for a second in daylight. The foil glints in memory.

Let it come. You cannot not think about something by forbidding it, and you do not need to. You meet it as a non-smoker handling a memory, not as a smoker fighting a want.

Keep your hands where they are and let the film run to its true end while the memory hangs there. You know that end because you smoked it awake at the farewell table. Yellow at the nail. Heat rasp. Grey on the tongue. Pale ash. Stale end. Sour back. Tight chest. Tray taste for hours. There is no version where it stays shining.

Then answer the thought with the sentence you will keep for life, said with joy in the chest because an enemy is dead and no friend is lost.

YIPPEE! I'M FREE!

Feel how different that sentence sits from the old doorway shuffle. The shuffle said: step out, burn three minutes, come back to the same papers with a drier mouth. This says: stay in, keep the afternoon whole, go back to the papers with a clear mouth.

When the old line tries again ten minutes later — that was just words, prove it by having one and leaving it — hear it arrive already labelled, not as your reason but as the trap's script talking through tight shoulders. You answer it from sight, not from effort: control was the tray. One lit match reopens what one stubbed end closed. I closed it as done. You hold finality not as a rule to obey but as an identity already settled. You are not refusing a treat. You are noticing that no treat was offered.

Drink water. Put the glass down without shielding anything from wind. Stretch the neck once, roll the shoulders once, breathe ordinary air through the nose, and go back to the second line of work. By three the tug is thinner than at two. By four the afternoon has run uncut and the thought has passed the way weather passes — noticed, named, gone.

That is the whole handling. Not suppression. Replacement.

### TALK THAT RUNS TO THE END

Evening comes earliest to test the old story. Glasses. Heaters ticking. Laughter running over. Mates you like. Someone steps half aside and you stay in the warm circle.

We used to live evenings sliced — talk, doorway, talk, doorway — missing the middle of every long story and calling the slices belonging. We patted the box the way we pat a wallet, to check we were safe for the night. What safety did that box ever give? It gave arithmetic and rain and mints on coating.

Stay for the whole talk now.

Sit where you usually sit. Taste the drink bright. Listen to the long story and hear the middle. Laugh the first time. When glasses empty, fetch another with either hand, no transfer needed. No pocket-check. No counting. No planning where to stand to steal the next three minutes. The talk runs on without a slice cut out of it.

I sat that first evening myself after years of stepping out, certain the night would feel flat without paper. What felt flat had been the stepping out — the cold edge, the burn, the return to talk already moved on. What felt full was staying — coats, night air, rain on pavement on the way home, tea tasting of tea before bed, morning mouth yours and uncoated.

You settle the bill without doing the old sums. No setting aside for the pack tomorrow. No working out whether you have enough left for the taxi and the twenty. The money lies where it lies. The night has cost what a night costs, and no extra tax was paid to a paper tube for permission to enjoy it.

Outside, the air is cold and clean. Someone tells the last joke by the kerb. You hear the end of it because you are there for the end of it. Cars pass with lights smearing on wet road. A bus sighs past. The group loosens, hugs, promises, splits toward taxis and buses and walks home.

At the taxi you smell coats, night air, rain on pavement — not tray on shirt.

### THE FRONT DOOR

Ride that taxi home as a passenger, not as a planner.

We used to ride home doing the last arithmetic of the day. How many left in the pack. Is there enough for the morning. Should I stop at the garage. Will the shop still be open. Can I borrow one off the flatmate without looking desperate. The night air came through the cracked window mixed with our own smoke blown out, and we watched the streets pass while rehearsing the morning's first light.

Tonight there is no rehearsal. Give the driver the street. Lean back. Watch the meter tick. Watch the town go past — lit takeaways, shuttered shops, a fox crossing under a lamp, rain freckling the windscreen and cleared by the wipers in two clean sweeps. Your coat smells of night and heaters and food from the table, not of paper. Your fingers smell of glass and cutlery and soap from the washroom, not of ash. You do not check the pocket. You do not pat for bulge. Flat cloth sits quiet against your hip and asks nothing.

Pay. Say goodnight. The door slams softly behind you. The taxi pulls away with its red lights melting into wet tarmac, and the street is suddenly very quiet.

Stand a moment on your own step. This is a small hour we never inhabited. We used to hurry through it — fumble the key while holding the last butt, grind it on the wall, fan the hand, suck a mint before turning the lock so the house would not know. Now the key turns cleanly. No butt to hide. No mint to sting. No fan of the hand. Just the Yale clicking, the hallway light warm behind frosted glass, your own feet loud on the path.

Push the door open. The house smells as you left it this morning — cooking from lunch faint on the air, soap in the bathroom, laundry drying on the rack, paper and dust and kettle. Not Febreezed. Not aired in panic. Just lived-in. Drop the keys in the dish. They clink. Hang the coat. It swings heavy and damp from rain. Take off the shoes and line them up. The carpet is soft under socks. The whole place is still and uncut, waiting for you to close it for the night rather than to serve a timetable.

Walk to the kitchen. Put the kettle on, not because you need a prop for your hands but because a cup before bed is a pleasant thing when the mouth is clear. While it heats, empty your pockets onto the counter the way we all do — coins, receipt from the shop, phone, keys. No box among them. No lighter. No cellophane to crackle. Look at that small pile and feel what is not there without making a ceremony of it. It is not an absence to endure. It is tidying that stayed tidy.

The kettle clicks. Pour. The steam rises. Carry the cup with either hand to the front room. Sit in the chair you always sit in. Not the farewell-table chair with its tray and stare. Just your evening chair, springs sighing, lamp warm. Pick up the paper you bought this morning and left folded for later. Read the piece you missed. Drink. The tea runs clean over the tongue. The second sip is as bright as the first because nothing was laid between them to blunt it.

Listen to the house. The fridge hums. The heating ticks as it cools. Rain patters once against the window and stops. Someone upstairs moves a chair. A car passes far off. No clock ticking toward a doorway. No edgy arithmetic about whether one more before bed is allowed or wise. Bed is allowed whenever you are sleepy. That is the only permission the night needs.

I remember that first night in my own front room, cup in hand, half expecting restlessness to climb the walls. What climbed instead was quiet. I kept waiting for the hand to move by itself toward the old chair-arm where the pack lived. It did not move, because there was no belief left to move it. I drank the tea to its dregs. I folded the paper. I turned the lamp off and the room went dark around the street-light edges at the curtains, and I felt — not triumph posed for a mirror, but something plainer and larger. The day had stayed whole from morning feet to front door, and I had not held it together. It had held itself.

When the cup is empty, carry it to the sink. Rinse it. Set it upside down on the rack beside the morning cup. That small clink is the day closing itself. Turn the kitchen light off. Check the front door — locked, chain on. Check the back — bolt across. We used to do that check with a butt hanging from the lip, one eye on the lock and one eye on the ember. Now both eyes are on the lock, both hands free to turn it.

### BEDTIME WITH A CLEAR MOUTH

Go to the bathroom as normal. Change nothing else in life.

Brush your teeth slowly. We used to brush to cover — hard scrub, much paste, mouthwash burning, floss snapping, trying to scour the tray taste off before sleep and knowing the morning would recoat it before the kettle boiled. Now brushing is just brushing. Paste foaming, brush moving, water cold from the tap, spit, rinse. Look in the mirror while you do it. Eyes a little tired from talk and late air. Face yours. No grey film to smile around.

Wash the face. Warm flannel, soap, cold splash. Smell the skin after. Skin, soap, cloth. That is the smell of a face that spent the evening inside talk rather than outside in rain feeding a tug.

Go to the bedroom. The bed is turned down as you left it. The window is cracked as you like it. Night air comes in thin and cool. Undress. Put the day clothes over the chair. Put on what you sleep in. Get in. The sheets are cold at first, then warm around legs and shoulders. Pull the duvet up. Lie on your back a minute and listen.

The body at the end of a free day feels different, not because it has been taught a technique but because it has not been interrupted. Legs heavy from walking and standing. Shoulders lower than at eight when the phone lit. Chest moving without rasp. Mouth clean, a little dry from heaters and talk, eased by the last sip of water on the bedside table. Drink it. Set the glass down. Turn on your side.

We used to lie here rehearsing — counting how many smoked, counting money burned, promising tomorrow will be different, fearing tomorrow will be the same, planning the morning doorway before sleep had come. That rehearsal was the Smokescreen talking through tired shoulders, keeping both ropes of the tug-of-war in its hands even in bed.

Tonight there is no rehearsal because there is nothing to rehearse. The figures of the day are plain: morning kettle drunk whole, shop done whole, break taken whole, work touched by pen, talk heard to its end, taxi ridden without sums, door locked with both hands free. No doorway in the rain. No tray to rinse. No butt to tip. Time was not sliced, so there is nothing to add up.

If a soft thought comes — surely something is over — answer it awake as you drift: what is over is doorway dates and pocket-checks. What is beginning is evenings uncut and mornings uncoated. Then let sleep come. It comes as it comes to a non-smoker, not as a reward for strength but as the body doing what it knows how to do when no new dose replanted edginess at midnight.

I slept that first night without waking for the old two-o-clock cough and throat-clear. I woke once to turn over and heard the rain and went straight back under. No hand groping the bedside table. No throat rasping for water to wet ash. Just sleep, uncut, until the alarm.

### NEXT MORNING, ALREADY FORWARD

The alarm goes at the same time. The light comes through the same crack. The house is cold in the same way.

And here is the part no lecture can give you and no willpower method can promise: the second morning is easier than the first, not because you tried harder in the night but because you did nothing in the night to replant the noise.

Get up. The floor is cold again. The kettle takes its minute again. The cup warms both palms again. Tea tastes of tea from the first sip because no new grey was laid over the tongue in hours of talk and sleep. The chest lifts without rasp. Ordinary breath through the nose, cooler than smoke ever was.

Shower. Soap meets skin and stays. Dress. Same coat, still faintly damp at the hem, now drying. Same bag. Same route. Pat the pocket if the old habit pats. Flat cloth. Nothing to count. In the old days that flatness halfway to the bus would have meant panic. Now it means information carried forward itself while you slept.

Walk to the bus in morning air that smells of bins and bakery and wet leaves. Nod to the same driver. Sit by the same window. The town passes again — shutters lifting, vans double-parked, pigeons startled from the kerb. Your hands lie open on the bag. No timetable. No bulge. The day lies whole in front of you again, the way it lay before the first con taught you to cut it, and you did not have to hold it whole in the night. It held itself while you slept because nothing was fed to cut it.

At work the chairs push back at eleven again. Someone says air again. You carry your cup out again. The step is there again. The talk runs. The drink is bright. The pen moves a line when you go back in. None of this needs deciding. None of this needs defending. That is what freedom carries forward means: not that you remember the arguments every hour, but that you do not need to. The arguments did their work at the table. Life does the rest by being lived.

Lunch will close itself again. The shop queue will shuffle again. The shutter will clatter again. The bell will ring again behind you. Evening talk will run to the taxi again. The front door will click again. The kettle will sing again. The bed will warm again. Each hour proves in the living what was once only seen in the arguing — that nothing was given and nothing is missed — and each proving makes the next hour quieter, not by effort but by ownership.

Ask the only question worth asking at the end of such days: did any hour need paper added to be complete? Morning kettle. Shop queue. Step outside at eleven. Doorway thought met and left like weather. Evening talk heard to its middle and its end. Front door locked with both hands. Bed slept in without rehearsal. Next morning begun without panic. Each ran to its end without it. Each was brighter, not duller, for being run whole.

Once seen awake in ordinary hours, it cannot be unseen. And once lived awake for two days running, it does not need to be re-seen. It is simply how days go now — whole, uncut, yours, carrying forward themselves while you get on with living them.

**SUMMARY**
- The shop queue proved the shutter holds nothing the basket needs, and the street smells brighter walked whole.
- The eleven o'clock step proved a pause is air and talk and drink already, with work moved by pen.
- A passing thought proved a memory can be met and answered with joy, then left like weather.
- Evening talk proved belonging stays in the warm circle to the taxi home.
- The front door and kettle proved the night closes itself whole, with keys and cup and lock handled by free hands.
- Bedtime proved sleep comes uncut with a clear mouth, without rehearsal or sums.
- The next morning proved freedom carries forward by itself, with tea tasting of itself and the day lying whole again.
