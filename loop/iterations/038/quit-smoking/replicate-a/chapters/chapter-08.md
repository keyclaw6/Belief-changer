Chapter 8
MORNINGS, MEALS AND BREAKS WITHOUT IT

*Your morning, your meal and your break were never lit by a cigarette — they were complete before the match was struck.*

### THE HAND BEFORE THE FEET

We know that morning.

The alarm cuts. The eyes are not open. The feet have not touched the floor and the hand is already moving across the bedside table, finding the pack by feel, finding the lighter by feel. We sit on the edge of the bed and put smoke on empty lungs before we have swallowed, before we have stood, before we have thought a single thought of our own. Then we cough and call the cough waking up.

"I need it to start me. Without it I cannot face the day."

I hear you. I lived that half-asleep fumble for years. I am not mocking the feeling. I am asking you to look at what it was.

If that first dose woke you, how were you awake enough to find it in the dark? If it gave you energy, why did you still move like a man underwater until the shower, the water, the kettle did their work? A non-smoker wakes with the same thick head, the same dry mouth, the same slow limbs. He stands, he washes, he drinks something warm, and the thickness clears. You did the same — stand, wash, drink — only you put a dose in the middle and gave the dose the credit.

Watch it without the dose for a morning in your mind.

Feet on cold floor. Feel the lino, the rug edge, the chill up the ankles. Stand. The weight goes through the legs. The knees click. The back straightens after the night bent. Walk to the bathroom. The door cold on the palm. Light on. Tiles white and loud to the eyes. Water on. Hands under, then face under. Cold stings, then clears. The mouth is sour — that is the stale end of last night, not a lack. Brush, rinse, spit. The tongue comes back. The nose opens a fraction.

Kettle on. The click, the hum building, the steam starting to breathe on the window. While it boils, stand at the back door. Open it two inches. First breath at the gap. The chest moves. The air is sharp. Cough, yes, a little. Then a second breath, deeper, taken all the way down. Shoulders lift and fall. Hands around nothing, hanging, rubbing each other for warmth. The yard, the roofs, the bins, the pale light. Nothing asking for anything.

Where in that chain did smoke do anything but interrupt?

Ask what woke you on the days the shop was shut and the pack was empty. You still woke. You still stood. You still cursed and washed and found shoes and got out. Grumpy, yes. Slow, yes. But upright, moving, talking, locking the door. The legs carried. The lungs pulled. The kettle boiled whether the hand held paper or not.

What is left over in those first minutes is an empty, slightly restless, slightly edgy little tug.

Not need. Not self. That is the usual shape, not a sentence on you. A collar-tight hum from the last dose fading overnight. We read it for years as proof we could not start. It was proof we had finished the night low, as every smoker does between evening and morning, and covered the low instantly rather than letting the kettle and the water and the moving legs do the starting.

You start. The dose never started you.

Follow the morning further, because the fumble hid more than the waking.

We sat on the bed and lit and told ourselves we were gathering ourselves. What were we gathering? The eyes still gummed. The head still thick. The first draw burned, the chest tightened, the mouth filled with grey, and we sat there coughing and blinking, doing nothing a man would call preparation. Then we stood anyway, washed anyway, drank anyway, and the day began anyway. The ten minutes on the bed added phlegm and hurry. They added no thought, no plan, no strength.

A non-smoker gathers himself by moving. He puts feet down and blood follows. He puts water on skin and nerves report back. He drinks heat and the stomach wakes. Every step is a signal to the body that day has started. We had the same steps waiting for us every morning. We laid smoke across them and could not see them.

Try the order true tomorrow in your mind. Feet, floor, stand, bathroom, water, face, mouth clean, kettle, door, breath, drink. Let each do its own work in its own second. Feel the cold floor wake the soles. Feel the wash wake the face. Feel the first hot sip go down and spread. Notice how much starting happens before any thought of a pack. Notice the hands. They open the door. They hold the mug. They button the shirt. They have work from the first minute. They were never empty for lack of paper. They were full of morning.

Did the dose ever put your shoes on? Did it ever find your keys? Did it ever wash the sour from your tongue, or did it lay another sour on top and call that waking?

You know. The morning was complete before the match was struck.

### THE PLATE THAT DOES NOT NEED A FULL STOP

Then the meal.

We finish the last bite. The plate is empty. The taste is still in the mouth — salt, butter, coffee edge — full and warm. And before the taste has faded the hand is already out. Click. Light. Draw. The taste is gone under grey. We lean back and say that finished it. That rounded it. That made it perfect.

"Food is nothing without that cigarette after."

Is that true? Answer from your own mouth, not from the pack talk.

If the after-meal dose made food taste better, why did it always come after the tasting was done? If it completed the meal, why did it wipe the meal's taste off the tongue and replace it with ash? Did you ever light before the first bite to make the food better? Of course not. You waited until pleasure was already there, then stood a dose in front of it and told the dose it brought it.

Think of your own evenings. The Sunday roast eaten slow, gravy cooling at the edge, fork heavy, belly warm. The curry with friends, laughing, second helping, bread torn, mouth alive with heat. The sandwich at the desk eaten in four bites because work pressed, still bread and filling and hunger met. In each one the pleasure was heat, hunger met, company, rest from work. The dose added no flavour. What smoker ever said, this needs no salt, it has smoke? We put good things in our mouths and then burned the aftertaste on purpose.

Try the end of a meal in your mind without it.

Last bite chewed slow. Do not bolt it while reaching. Chew. Let the jaw work. Fork down. The clink on the plate. Sit. Do not get up. Let the taste stay where it belongs. Breathe through the nose. The mouth is alive for minutes after eating — that is the body tasting, as it is built to do. Sip water. The water lifts and carries, and the taste comes back in a second wave, milder, cleaner. Sip coffee. Bitter meets rich. Talk. The full belly settles. The shoulders drop because work is paused and hunger is gone, not because a tube burned.

Stay there. Five minutes. The plate in front of you, knife marks, crumbs. The room sounds coming back. The chair holding weight. The stomach sending its quiet full signal. This is the close of eating. Animals know it. Children know it. We knew it before the first dose taught us to stand up at exactly this second and go out to the step.

You will feel the old half-move. Hand to pocket. That old pellet after a finished paragraph, that little payment after teaching, driving, finishing — the same hand, the same second. That tug visits, thin, and passes if you leave the taste alone. Ten minutes later the taste has faded on its own, clean, and you are still satisfied. With a dose, ten minutes later the taste was ash and the tug was already rebuilding for the next hour.

Which honoured the food — the leaving of the taste to linger, or the burning of it?

Ask the tongue plainly.

If the dose rounded the meal, why did the mouth after it feel coated, dry, in need of a mint, a drink, a wash? If the dose was the crown of the cook's work, why did no cook ever ask for it? Why did the best meals of your life — holiday tables, birthday tables, tables where you laughed till you ached — live in memory as food and faces, never as ash? You remember who sat where. You remember the second helping. You do not remember the brand that followed. It left no memory because it made no pleasure.

It was you that tasted. Not the stick. The work of eating, the hunger, the cooking, the company — yours. The dose only quieted its own claim while you did it, then stole the meal's name.

Let the meal run longer in your mind and watch satisfaction hold.

Fifteen minutes after the last bite without a dose, the belly is calm. The mouth is clean. The head is clear for talk. You can still taste a faint ghost of what you ate when you swallow. That ghost is the body's own keeping. With a dose, fifteen minutes after, the ghost is gone. The mouth is stale. The fingers smell. The throat is dry. You drink more, chew gum, spray, hide. The meal that was closed now needs managing.

Who closed it better — the lingering or the lighting?

### THE BREAK THAT WAS NEVER A BREAK

And the break. This is the one we guard hardest.

The screen blurs. The back aches. Voices press. We push the chair back, fish the pack, step out to the doorway. Cold hits the face. We light, draw, stare at nothing. Ten minutes. Then back in. We say we rested. We say we can think again. We say non-smokers do not understand what a break needs.

We lived that doorway, you and I. Let us look at what rested us.

Was it the paper and the powder? Or was it pushing the chair back, standing, walking, cold air on hot cheeks, eyes looking far instead of near, hands empty for ten minutes, nobody asking for anything? Strip the dose out and every one of those stays. A man who steps out with nothing in his hands still stands, still feels the air, still looks at the sky, still lets the shoulders fall. The dose was only ever sneaking a ride on the pause.

Do it now in your mind, as you will do it tomorrow.

Chair back. The scrape on the floor. Stand. Legs straighten. The knees stiff, then loosening. The spine lengthens after the hunch. Walk. The corridor, the stairs, the handle. Door opens. The outside hits — traffic hum, rain smell, that stairs-breath pull halfway up when you climb for air. Let the chest take it. Stop at the wall. Roll the neck. Left, right. Unclench the jaw. You did not know it was clenched until it opens. Watch people pass. Think nothing on purpose. Hands in pockets, nothing to hold, nothing to tap, nothing to time.

The tug comes, of course. Empty, nagging, a little edgy at the edges. Let it sit beside you while you stand there. It does not stop you standing. It does not stop you seeing. It does not stop the back easing because you stood. Two minutes in, the mind clears — because you stepped away, because blood moved, because noise dropped. That clearing was always the break. We credited smoke for what distance did.

Stay out the full ten. Feel how the break has its own arc.

First minute, the noise comes with you. The last email line still on the eyes. The last voice still in the ear. Second minute, the cold takes the front. Cheeks cool. Forehead cool. Breath shows. Third and fourth minute, the shoulders give. They drop half an inch. The hands in pockets warm. The eyes stop hunting and start seeing — brick, cloud, pigeon on the wire, number plate, tree moving. Fifth minute, thought loosens. Not forced. The knot you brought out sits beside you and unties a little because you are not pulling it. Sixth, seventh minute, the body reports. Back less tight. Legs awake. Mouth clean. Nose running a little in the cold and you sniff and smile because it is alive.

Ask the doorway plainly.

If the break dose rested you, why did you always need another one an hour later? If it cleared the head, why did the head always cloud again before the next doorway? If non-smokers cannot rest without it, why do they take the same ten minutes, drink the same tea, stare at the same car park, and go back in steady without once thinking of a pack?

You know. The rest was stopping work. The clearness was air and distance. The dose only fed the Nipper for minutes while you rested, then made the next hour's itch certain.

The Nipper is small. The Smokescreen around the break is large. It says pause means puff, pause has always meant puff, pause will be empty without puff. That tutoring was installed, not born. Before the first cigarette you paused a hundred times a day — kicking a ball, looking out a classroom window, sitting on a wall — and every pause worked without a light. The body knows how to pause. It never forgot.

### THE WALK BACK IN

Now do not stop the picture at the wall. The break is only half lived if you do not walk back in. Stay in the same rehearsal and go back.

Stub nothing. Carry nothing. Turn from the wall. Door handle, warm from inside after the cold outside. Pull. The heat of the building on the face. The hum of lights and machines. Stairs down or corridor along. Feel the legs now. They are looser than when you went out. The back is taller. The jaw is still unclenched. The eyes have far seeing still in them.

Sit. Chair under weight. Desk in front. Mug where you left it. Pick it up. It is still warm. Drink. Let the drink go all the way. Tea to the bottom, coffee to the dregs. Taste it to the end. Without grey in the mouth the drink keeps its own flavour from first sip to last — milk, tannin, sugar, bitter, heat. Notice the tongue working. Notice the nose over the rim. The steam up the nostrils. The throat swallowing clean. This is drinking, whole, not rinsing ash.

Hands now. They have nothing to tap, nothing to flick, nothing to shield from wind. Put them on the keys, on the pen, on the paper. They are steady. They were shaking a little on the way out — that shake was hours of sitting and the old tug talking at once. Ten minutes standing has steadied them. Watch them move. Fingers find home row. Thumb turns page. Palm holds mug. Every tool answers at once because nothing is held aside for paper and fire.

First five minutes back. The screen is the same screen. The pile is the same pile. But the eyes are different. Far seeing carried in. You look at near work with eyes that have looked far. Lines hold. You read a paragraph through without re-reading the first line. You hear the room without being pulled by every voice. The tug taps once, thin, and goes behind the work because the work is in front. You do not argue with it. You leave it while you type the next line.

Second five minutes. Breath through the nose. In, out. The chest moves low and slow from the cold air still in it. The shoulders stay down. The neck stays loose. Thought comes in order. One thing, then the next. Not racing. Not blank. The knot you brought out is smaller. You see the next step without forcing it. You write it, you do it, you cross it. The hand moves to the mug, not to the pocket, because the mouth wants taste, not grey.

Ten to twenty minutes in. This is where the old break used to fail. With a dose, ten minutes back in meant the taste turning, the throat drying, the fingers smelling on the keys, the mind already counting to the next doorway. The break gave minutes and took the hour. Without the dose, ten minutes back in means the break still giving. The air is still in the chest. The far seeing is still in the eyes. The drink is still warm to the end. Attention stays because nothing in the blood is sinking fast and asking.

Feel the difference in the hands across the half hour. At minute five they typed. At minute fifteen they still type. No pause to fish, to light, to shield, to tap. No leaving the desk to stand in the rain. No queue, no shelter hunt, no cold fingers coming back numb. The work runs uncut. The thread holds. When you look up, twenty-five minutes have gone and you have not watched the clock once. On a smoking day you watched it every ten.

Drink again. The last third of the mug. It is cooler now and sweeter. The tongue finds it. The nose finds it. Eating and drinking after a meal without a dose taught you this — taste lingers when it is not burned. The same holds at the desk. Tea tastes of tea all the way down. Coffee tastes of coffee. Water tastes of water, cold and clean on a mouth that has not been coated. The body reports pleasure in small sips because the channel is open.

Breathe again through the nose while you work. Not a lesson. Not a drill. Simply the way the body breathes when the mouth is shut and the chest is not full. In over the lip, down to the belly, out slow. The air from outside still cool in the nose. The brain likes rhythm. Typing has rhythm. Breathing has rhythm. They fall together without trying. Far-eyes attention means you see the whole desk, the whole page, the whole next hour, not a single burning point.

Ask the half hour what it proves, and let your own hands answer.

If the doorway without paper were empty, why is the desk fuller at minute twenty than it ever was at minute twenty with paper? If the hands needed holding, why do they work better empty than full? If the head needed smoke to think, why does it think in longer lines when only air and distance went in?

You know, because you have just lived it in your mind. Morning, meal and break without a dose are not only complete in the moment. They carry forward. Clean mouth carries into taste. Open chest carries into attention. Loose shoulders carry into work. Nothing stands between the body and its next hour.

Stay a little longer. Twenty to thirty minutes. The mug empty. Turn it. The ring on the desk. The small satisfaction of a thing finished to the end. Stand if you need to stretch. Roll the neck again. Look out the window from inside. The wall where you stood is out there. You can see it. You were there ten minutes ago with empty hands and cold cheeks. You are here now with warm hands and steady eyes. Same morning. Same meal behind it. Same break between. Only the thread is uncut.

This is what non-smokers stand in all day without naming it. Not a high. Not a lift. The normal keeping of taste and breath and attention, hour to hour, because no level is poured and no sink is dug. We called that flat. It was never flat. It was continuous.

### YOUR BODY ALREADY KNOWS

So bring the three together — floor, plate, doorway — and feel the shape that is left.

Morning without covering the low: feet, water, kettle, breath. Meal without burning the taste: last bite, linger, sip, talk. Break without feeding in the doorway: stand, walk, air, far eyes, empty hands. None of them asks for anything but what is already there. None of them leaves you less.

"I will miss the favourite. The morning one. The after-food one. The one with tea on the break."

That sentence sounds like three favourites. It is one sentence with three hats. The favourite was never morning, meal or break. The favourite was the brief quiet of the tug while morning, meal or break did the real giving. Take the same morning, the same meal, the same break, and let the tug quiet on its own — which it does, minute by minute, once no fresh level is poured — and the giving stands out cleaner than before. Food tastes more because ash does not take it. Air feels more because the chest is not full of grey. Mornings start more because you feel the starting happen in the legs and lungs, not in the lighter.

It never fixed the itch. It caused it.

That is not a slogan to learn. That is what your own floor and plate and doorway just told you. Each dose put the itch back where the last dose left it. Each pause without a dose lets the skin close a little more. A smoker never rises above the normal a non-smoker keeps for nothing. He only climbs briefly back toward it, then sinks. Living those hours without topping up is not going without. It is staying where non-smokers stand all day.

I stood in that doorway myself the first week without, tea going cold, hands doing that stupid half-search. The tug tapped. Then the air came cold over the yard and the back of the neck loosened anyway. The tea tasted of tea all the way down. I laughed out loud because the break had worked without the thing I swore made it work. No courage in it. No white knuckles. Just eyes open.

8. TRUST YOUR BODY TO BREATHE AND TASTE
Your body knows freedom without being taught.

**SUMMARY**
- The morning start comes from standing, washing, moving and warm drink, not from smoke on empty lungs.
- The meal is finished by hunger met and taste lingering, not by ash laid over the tongue.
- The break rests because work stops, legs move and air and distance clear the head, not because a dose burns in the middle of it.
- The small tug that visits in each moment is a fading level, not a need and not a self, and left alone it passes while the moment still gives.
- Morning, meal and break lived without a dose are fuller going forward into work, with taste kept to the end, breath kept in the chest, and attention carried to the desk.
