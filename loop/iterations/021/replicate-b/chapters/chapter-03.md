Chapter 3
WHAT DOES IT DO FOR YOU

**IN THIS CHAPTER**
- A kitchen table the morning after, yesterday's wrappers counted
- An ordinary day logged hour by hour, mood against dose
- A bathroom mirror, a toothbrush, and what frequent sweetness does to teeth
- A quiet question about a man who helps you out of a ditch

*Forget what it costs you for a moment — tell me one true thing it gives you.*

You were caught, not weak.

So let us stop arguing about harm and ask the question that ends the argument.

THE WRONG ARGUMENT

You already know BAD SUGAR harms you. We all do.

You know the clothes tighten. You know the afternoon fog rolls in. You know the dentist sighs. You know the money goes. You have read the headlines and heard the warnings and given yourself the little lecture in the shop queue.

And still the hand reaches. Why?

Because harm never freed anyone where a supposed benefit survives. Tell a man a thing harms him and he will weigh it. He will put harm on one side of the scales and pleasure on the other and ask which weighs more. As long as he believes the pleasure is real, he will pay the price and call himself weak for paying it.

I do not want your harm ledger. I want your benefit ledger.

I am not asking whether BAD SUGAR does more harm than good. I am asking what good it does at all. Name it. Point to it. Show me one solid benefit that belongs to the dose itself and to nothing else.

“I know it is bad for me, but it is my little reward. It gets me through.”

I hear you. I said the same for years. Warmly, without blame, I ask you to keep that sentence in front of you while we look at an ordinary day together. Not a worst day. Not a binge day. Your normal day. The moderate day you defend.

We will put every dose on the table and ask of each one what it did FOR you. Not what it cost. What it gave.

A NORMAL DAY, HONESTLY LOGGED

Do this with me now, as an investigator, not as a defendant. No changing anything. Only noticing.

Morning. You start with good intentions. Perhaps a sweetened cereal, or toast with spread, or a sugary tea or coffee to get going. Perhaps you are virtuous and skip it. Write it down either way.

Mid-morning. The first dip. Mouth dry, attention thin. A biscuit with a hot drink. A bar from the drawer. Quick, unthinking. The lift, such as it is, lasts minutes. By eleven you are restless again, a little flat, a little snappy, already glancing at the clock.

Lunch. Perhaps savoury, perhaps proper. Then something sweet to finish, because a meal without it feels unfinished. Cake in the canteen. Chocolate from the machine. You tell yourself, “I've managed to be good this week, so one is fair.”

Three o'clock. The famous slump. Eyes heavy. Work thick. You reach for sugary somethings to get me through. A fizzy drink. Sweets from a bag. Ten minutes of buzz, then the fog comes back lower than before. Second reach at four.

Evening. Tired, deserving. “A treat when you feel you deserve one.” A dessert after supper. A handful while cooking. A biscuit with television. Then the late reach, standing up in the quiet kitchen, the one you do not quite log.

Now add the columns.

Money: small coins every day that make a large sum across a year. Not a fortune in one hit. A steady drip you never agreed to audit.

Time: minutes spent queuing, buying, unwrapping, chewing without tasting, hiding wrappers, promising tomorrow will be different.

Teeth: brushed or not, rinsed or not, the frequent acid wash after every sweet dose. The dentist bill you pay later for what you swallowed quickly today.

Mood: the roller coaster written in your own hand — brief lift, fast drop, edginess, craving, brief lift, fast drop. You went to bed neither satisfied nor proud, already planning to be stricter tomorrow.

That is what it does TO you on a moderate day. Read the list again. Where is the column for what it did FOR you?

Show me the hour it made better in a way nothing else could have made better. Show me the joy it added that was not there before the packet opened. You ate. You drank. You paid. You faded. You craved. Which part would you pay to keep if you could keep the day without it?

You cannot answer with harm. Harm is off the table. I give you harm freely. I am asking for benefit. One clear, irreplaceable benefit that belongs to BAD SUGAR itself.

Take your time. There is no hurry, and there is no judgment here. Only the empty column waiting to be filled.

TEETH AND THE CEILING

Let me put two plain facts on the table, flatly, as they are, because feeling fine misleads us.

First, teeth. Free sugars feed plaque acid and that acid rots teeth. That is the mechanism, simple and bodily. Frequent sweet doses mean frequent acid attacks. Brushing and fluoride and saliva and luck all mediate it — I am not pretending they count for nothing, and I am not giving dental advice — but the stake is real: mouths that meet sweet doses all day carry the decay.

Look in your own mirror. The sensitivity to cold. The filling that needs redoing. The child who needs work before his adult teeth are settled. None of that arrives with a drum roll. It arrives slowly, dose by dose, while we tell ourselves one cannot hurt.

Second, the ceiling. The sober guidance for free sugars is to keep them below a tenth of daily calories, with benefit in going below half that. Most of us in sweet-eating cultures live far above both lines without knowing it, because BAD SUGAR hides in drinks and cereals and breads and sauces as well as in bars and cakes. Feeling normal does not mean intake is low. It means high has become normal.

I speak here of the general picture across populations, not as a sentence on you — and if you live with diabetes, take anything that affects blood sugar or appetite, are pregnant, or carry any medical risk around food, follow your clinician first and use this book for the belief only.

Now hear the relief in the same breath: I do not want you to stop from fear of teeth or charts. Fear never freed anyone for long. It only makes the dose look precious and forbidden, and then more wanted. I give you these facts so you stop trusting “I feel fine” as proof, not so you start obeying fear. We are not running from harm. We are looking for benefit and finding the column empty.

Do you want to keep paying the drip of money and mood and teeth for the rest of your life for something that gives you nothing back?

Or would you rather walk easy around food and shops and evenings for the rest of your life, clear and unhurried, with the drip stopped?

Both futures last the same length. Only one has you free inside them.

A GENUINE TREAT OR FUEL?

“But it must do something for me. I would not keep going back otherwise.”

That is the honest objection. Let us meet it with honest eyes.

What do you believe it does for you? When you strip away the slogans, it always comes down to the same two claims. That it treats you. That it fuels you. That it is, in one short phrase, a genuine treat or fuel.

Treat means it gives pleasure beyond the ordinary. Fuel means it gives energy beyond your own.

Ask of your normal day whether either claim survived contact with reality.

Was the mid-morning biscuit a treat? A treat is noticed, savoured, remembered. That biscuit was eaten while reading email, tasted barely at all, followed by a second without decision. Would you serve it at a feast and boast of it? Would you remember it tomorrow? A true treat does not need hiding, hurrying, or regret. That dose needed all three.

Was the three o'clock hit fuel? Fuel carries you further. That hit carried you ten minutes up and then dropped you lower, calling for another hit to lift the drop. Real fuel does not create the need for more fuel an hour later. Real dinner does not do that. Real sleep does not do that. Real hunger met with real food does not do that.

Then why does it feel like something was given?

Here is the quiet question I promised you, and I ask it only as a seed for later. If a man tripped you into a ditch every morning and then offered you his hand out, would you call him your rescuer?

You see the shape, even before we pull it fully apart. The low that the dose relieves is the low the last dose left behind. The edginess, the flatness, the need-something-now — they arrive on schedule because the schedule put them there. Each fix quiets for minutes the unease the previous fix guaranteed would return. We call the quiet pleasure. We call the hand rescue.

It is not pleasure. It is pause.

It is not fuel. It is debt, briefly rescheduled.

I speak as one who paid that debt for years and watched thousands pay it. We were not enjoying a genuine treat or fuel. We were borrowing relief from our next hour to patch this one, with interest. The day audit proves it without any science: lift, drop, reach, repeat. No hour richer than it would have been without the packet. Only hours poorer, then briefly patched.

So I state the settled fact, flatly, without hedge and without cruelty:

It does plenty TO you. It does nothing FOR you.

Read that again slowly. Let every dose you logged today pass under it. Money TO you. Acid TO you. Fog TO you. Craving TO you. Nothing FOR you that was not the brief quieting of its own noise.

If there is no benefit to doing it often, what exactly would be the point of doing it occasionally? Keep that question alive. It will do more work than any lecture on harm.

JUDGE BY BENEFIT ALONE

You came to this chapter conceding harm but guarding benefit. You leave it with a different instrument in your hand.

Do not weigh harm against pleasure any longer. That scale is rigged, because the pleasure side is empty and the harm side only frightens you into wanting. Weigh benefit alone. Demand one real thing done FOR you that belongs to the dose and to nothing else — not to the break, not to the company, not to the hunger that any meal would have met, not to the relief of a low the last dose built.

You will look for that thing honestly, because you are an honest investigator now, not a defendant. You will look in the morning cup and the drawer bar and the evening box. You will look for the rest of this book as you read, dose by dose, with curious eyes.

And you will find the column stays empty.

That emptiness is wonderful news. It means there is nothing to lose and no sacrifice ahead. It means the only thing standing between you and freedom is a phrase learned long ago, not a need built into your body.

So here is our third numbered agreement, to stand beside the first two:

3. JUDGE ONLY BY WHAT IT DOES FOR YOU
Forget harm versus benefit and ask for the benefit.

**SUMMARY**
- Harm is conceded and set aside. Benefit is the only question that matters.
- A normal logged day shows cost in money, time, teeth and mood, with no hour made richer by the dose.
- Frequent free sugars drive plaque acid and decay, with brushing and other factors shaping the outcome.
- Ordinary intake sits far above sober guidance. Feeling fine does not mean intake is low.
- The lift is a brief quieting of a low the last dose left behind, not new pleasure or new energy.
- No dose yet has filled the benefit column with anything that belongs to BAD SUGAR itself.
