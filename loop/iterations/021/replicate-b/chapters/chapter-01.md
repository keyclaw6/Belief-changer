Chapter 1
THE INVITATION

**IN THIS CHAPTER**
- An armchair, an open packet, a full kitchen shelf
- A thick ring drawn around two small words
- A former sweet-tooth talking plainly about his own escape
- A simple agreement about how this book is to be read

*This is not another lecture about food, and you are not about to be told to want less from life.*

IF YOU THINK YOU HAVE HEARD ALL THIS BEFORE

I know exactly the look you are wearing as you open these pages.

Half of you is hoping. Half of you is scoffing. A third voice underneath, the one you hardly admit, is tired. Tired of the promises, tired of the Monday restarts, tired of being told what a grown adult may put in his own mouth.

You have heard it all. Eat less. Count it. Earn it. Be good this week and allow a little on Friday. Keep it out of the house and be safe. Keep a food diary and be honest. You have tried to be honest. You have tried to be good. And here you are, still reaching, still thinking about sweetness far more than any free person ever would, still wondering why something so small seems to order you about.

Let me put your mind at rest about one thing at once.

This is not that book.

I am not here to lecture you about nutrition. I am not here to weigh you, measure you, scold you, or hand you another set of rules to clutch until they break. I am not here to tell you that you are weak, or greedy, or broken, or that you need more backbone. We have had enough of that talk, you and I, and it has never freed anybody.

I am here as a fellow escapee. I lived for years inside the sweet loop myself. The morning promise. The sensible lunch. The bright, reasonable voice at three o'clock that said just one little something to get through. The evening packet that opened with one and closed empty. The strange fog the next morning, and the firm promise to be stricter tomorrow. I know that roller coaster from the inside. I rode it, I believed in it, I defended it, and I hated what it did to my days.

And I got out. Not by fighting myself harder. Not by becoming a saint about food. I got out by seeing something so plain that I laughed when I saw it. Since then I have had the privilege of watching thousands of other confirmed sweet-lovers see the same plain thing and walk out through the same open door.

That is why I can talk to you with complete confidence. I know how this feels from your side of the page, and I know how it looks from the free side. There is no mystery left in it for me, and there will soon be none left for you.

You may be thinking: “Too good to be true? Stop sweets without misery? Without counting and without punishment? That was my reaction too.”

Good. Bring that doubt with you. I do not want blind faith from you. I want only your attention for a few hours, and a willingness to look honestly at what sweetness actually does in your life.

Consider what it costs you to look. You are not being asked to hand over money you cannot afford, or to sign up for years of meetings, or to white-knuckle your way through tonight with an empty cupboard. You are being asked to read, to think, and to notice. If I am wrong, you will close this book and carry on exactly as before, with your tastes unchanged. If I am right, a weight you have carried for years lifts off your shoulders. In that sense, you have absolutely nothing to lose and everything to gain by reading on with an open mind.

That is not a slogan. That is arithmetic. A few hours of reading against a lifetime of tug and bother around food. No danger in the looking. Great prize in the seeing.

We have all been conned in one way or another in life, and there is no shame in having been conned. The shame would be in keeping our eyes shut once someone offers to show us the trick. I am offering to show you the trick around sweetness. All I ask is that you let me show it fully before you decide.

WHAT I MEAN WHEN I SAY SUGAR

Before we go one step further we must agree on terms, or we will talk past each other for a whole book.

When ordinary people say sugar they mean a dozen different things, and the confusion keeps them trapped. One person means white spoonfuls in tea. Another means chocolate. Another means bread and pasta and fizzy drinks and breakfast cereal and biscuits and cake and ice cream. Another says, with a clever smile, “But fruit is sugar too, so nothing matters,” and uses that line to keep the whole merry-go-round turning.

Let us remove the fog now, cleanly, once, so we never need to argue about words again.

> When this book says sugar, take it to mean BAD SUGAR.
>
> BAD SUGAR means refined sugar plus added free sugars plus junk processed carbs and starchy snack-carbs eaten as sweet hits, including sugary drinks, confectionery, biscuits, cakes, desserts, sweetened cereals and equivalent grazing doses.
>
> Natural sugars inside whole fruit, vegetables and plain meals are not the target. They belong to real food, eaten when hungry, enjoyed, stopped at satisfied. They are part of the freedom we are walking toward, not part of the trap we are walking out of.

Read that box again, slowly. It matters more than any other paragraph in this opening.

I am not talking about an apple. I am not talking about a carrot, or potatoes in a proper dinner, or milk, or a home-cooked meal that leaves you satisfied and ready to get on with your evening. No sane person needs a book to escape from an apple. The apple does not call you back an hour later. It does not whisper from the shelf at night. It does not leave you foggy and reaching.

I am talking about the engineered dose. The bar, the packet, the bottle, the box, the bag that is designed to hit fast, fade fast, and call you back. The sweet top-up that poses as food while behaving like a fix. The grazing that never satisfies. That is BAD SUGAR. Two short words to name one clear target, so you and I always know what we are discussing.

“Do I have to weigh it? Do I have to read every label with a magnifying glass? Do I have to fear a crumb?”

No. You do not. This is not pedantry, and I am not turning you into a label detective. Any ordinary adult knows a dessert from a dinner and a fizzy drink from water. If a plain meal contains a little sweetness as part of cooking, your body knows what to do with dinner. Our business is the deliberate sweet hit, the dose taken for lift, for reward, for comfort, for the buzz. You know the difference perfectly well, and once you see the con you will feel the difference even more clearly.

And to settle the clever line before it troubles you again: fruit does not let BAD SUGAR off the hook. Saying an orange contains sugar so chocolate must be fine is like saying water contains oxygen so you can breathe under the sea. It sounds logical for half a second and collapses the moment you live it. No one binges on oranges until the box is empty and sits in regret. No one hides orange peel in the bin. The problem was never sweetness in nature. The problem is the concentrated, refined hit that was built to keep you returning.

Hold that line steady and the rest becomes simple. BAD SUGAR is the target. Real food is your friend. Hunger and satisfaction are your guides, not your enemies.

WHY I CAN PROMISE YOU EASE

“Easy? For me? You don’t know me. You don’t know how bad I am in the evenings.”

I hear you. I heard that line from myself for years, and I have heard it from every sweet-lover I have ever helped. Let me answer it directly.

I was not a casual user. I was a firm believer. I believed a sweet treat made a hard day bearable, made a celebration real, made an afternoon workable. I believed some people could take it or leave it and I was simply not one of them. I believed my evenings proved something dark about my character.

Every one of those beliefs turned out to be false. Not partly false. Wholly false. And when they fell, stopping was not a battle. It was relief. The day I saw clearly, I stopped, I ate dinner with real appetite, I slept, I woke clear, and I have never needed to revisit the question.

That sounds outrageous. I know it sounds outrageous. An adult who has failed on Monday, and again on Thursday, and again after the birthday, hears the word easy as an insult.

So let me earn the word.

It was not that I suddenly grew willpower. Quite the opposite. I stopped using willpower entirely, because I saw willpower was never the instrument for this job. It was not that life became grey and I learned to endure greyness nobly. Life became brighter, food became more enjoyable, hungers became cleaner, afternoons became steadier. It was not that I served a sentence and graduated. Freedom arrived at the start, not at the end of a long trial.

This is why I speak with such certainty. I am not guessing about you. I am remembering me. We fell into the same hole by the same false information, we told ourselves the same stories to explain the hole, and we can climb out by the same clear seeing.

Do you want to stop living in that tug-of-war around sweets? Of course you do — that is why you picked up this book. Do you believe at this moment that life without sweet treats would be poorer and flatter? Of course you do — otherwise you would have simply stopped already and would not need me. Those two truths sit side by side in every sweet-lover: the wish to be free, and the fear that freedom means loss.

My promise to you is that the second truth is mistaken, and I will prove it to you so thoroughly that the first truth can happen by itself, easily, immediately and permanently, without force and without mourning.

Notice those three words and hold me to them. Easily, because there is nothing to endure once you see there is nothing to lose. Immediately, because freedom does not need to be earned over months; it begins the moment the false belief dies. Permanently, because when you see a confidence trick clearly you do not need to remind yourself daily not to fall for it again. You simply do not fall.

I do not ask you to believe that yet. I ask only that you allow the possibility. Keep reading as an investigator, not as a defendant. You are not on trial here. BAD SUGAR is on trial, and you are on the jury. Hear the evidence fully before you deliver your verdict.

HOW TO READ SO THAT THIS WORKS

There is a right way to read this book and a wrong way, and the difference is everything.

The wrong way is to skim for tips, to jump to the end for the trick, to argue with each page from inside your old beliefs, to cut down while you read to prove your seriousness, to test yourself tonight to see if you are cured already. That way keeps the old fear in charge and blocks the seeing.

The right way is simpler than you expect.

First, follow the reading as it comes. This book is built like a combination. Each part turns a little of the lock. Skip a part and the lock does not open, not because I am punishing you, but because understanding needs its full shape. Read in order. Do not skip. Do not rush to the last page for the punchline. Let each point land before you reach for the next.

In short, All you have to do is follow all the instructions.

Read that line again and feel how light it is. All you have to do is follow all the instructions. Not follow a diet. Not count, weigh, confess, or punish. Not become a different person with a different life. Just read as directed, think as invited, and let the argument do the heavy lifting. I have made the path; your part is to walk it attentively to the end.

Second, eat as you normally eat while you read, and judge with a clear head. This will surprise you, but it is essential.

You came here clutching moderation rules: be good, allow a little, earn a treat, hide the packet, start again Monday. I am not asking you to tighten those rules while we talk. I am asking you to lay the whole burden of rules down for a few hours and listen. You cannot judge sweetness clearly while you are congratulating yourself for refusing it or punishing yourself for taking it. Read fed, read comfortable, read honest. Notice what you actually feel, hour by hour, without commentary.

That honesty leads to the happiest part of our agreement, the eating contract that will carry you all the way out and keep you free afterwards.

Eat what you want, when you are hungry, stop when you are satisfied, and never punish yourself again.

Let those words settle. Eat what you want, when you are hungry, stop when you are satisfied, and never punish yourself again. Not what a chart allows. Not what a guru permits. What you want, when hunger calls, until satisfaction answers, with no punishment before or after. That is not a diet rule. That is the natural way every small child eats before the world teaches confusion, and it is the way you will eat again when BAD SUGAR no longer interferes.

“But won’t that mean I eat sweets constantly?”

No, and you will soon understand why with perfect clarity. At this moment you do not eat from hunger and stop at satisfaction around BAD SUGAR — none of us did — because the sweet hit scrambles those signals. Once the scramble is removed, hunger and satisfaction return as reliable guides, and real food satisfies in a way doses never did. I am not asking you to trust that blindly now. I am asking you to keep the contract in view as our destination: a life where you eat with appetite and stop with pleasure, with no fear and no guilt attached to eating.

If you live with diabetes or pre-diabetes, take medicine that affects blood sugar or appetite, are pregnant, live with an eating disorder past or present, or have any medical condition where changing what you eat could carry risk, talk to your clinician first and follow their advice above all else in this book.

That single caution is not part of the belief argument; it is simple care for your body while your mind does the investigating.

So here is our working pact, stated plainly. You read in order, you keep your mind open, you eat normally and notice honestly, and you let me lead you to the point where stopping feels like walking out of a stuffy room into fresh air. My side of the pact is to waste none of your time, to scold you never, to frighten you never for effect, and to prove every step before I ask you to take it.

THE FEELING TO BRING TO PAGE ONE

Bring elation if you can. Bring curiosity if elation feels too far.

You have not picked up a punishment. You have picked up a key. Think how marvellous it will be to shop without that inner argument, to pass an afternoon without watching the clock for the next lift, to end an evening without that heavy, foggy regret, to wake without promising to be good. Think how proud you will feel to like food again simply, to trust hunger again fully, to enjoy meals, markets, kitchens, restaurants as a free eater.

You may not feel that excitement yet. Most readers do not on page one. They feel nervous, and a little defiant, and a little sad at the thought that their one comfort is under discussion. I understand that sadness completely, because I felt it. We all felt it. It is the feeling of someone who believes a friend is about to be taken away.

Keep open the possibility that the friend was never a friend. Keep open the possibility that what looks like comfort is really the source of the discomfort. You do not need to accept that now. You need only permit the investigation.

Ask yourself one straight question, and answer honestly: if sweetness were truly doing something wonderful FOR you, would you need a book to help you stop thinking about it? Would free choice need instructions? The very fact you are here, reading, hoping, doubting, proves something in you already senses the truth. Something has taken hold of your eating that has nothing to do with appetite and nothing to do with joy.

We will look at that something together, calmly, completely, without panic and without gloom. You will not be hurried. You will not be shamed. You will not be left to fight yourself.

There is one instruction that governs this whole first movement, and I give it to you now with warm authority, as the first of our short numbered agreements that will guide you out.

1. KEEP AN OPEN MIND
Question what you think you know about sweetness.
