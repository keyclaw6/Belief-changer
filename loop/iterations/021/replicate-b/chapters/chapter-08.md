Chapter 8
WHO BUILT THE WANT

**IN THIS CHAPTER**
- A supermarket aisle at 6pm, bright packs and the checkout ambush
- A mirror held up to family habit and the personality excuse
- Needing the family size and hearing a wrapper from two rooms away
- An open cell door and a lion pacing by habit
- A seatbelt clicked for the road

*You did not invent this want for sweetness, it was built inside you, and the fear that holds you there belongs to the trap.*

You have watched the evening grumble for what it is.

Walk with me down a different aisle and you will see where that grumble learned to speak.

THE AISLE THAT WANTS FOR YOU

Six in the evening. Tired feet. A trolley with one wobbly wheel. You came in for bread and milk.

Look up. Both sides blaze. Cartoon tigers grin from boxes at a child's eye height. Chocolate bars hang like flags at the checkout where you must queue. The word treat shouts from every second pack. New. Creamier. Double choc. Limited edition. The air near the bakery vents warm vanilla straight into your face before a single bite.

We tell ourselves we chose this. We tell ourselves the want rose from inside us, personal and natural.

The fact is it was manufactured.

I have spent years inside food factories on paper, listening to how a sweet dose is built. Armies of clever, well-paid people test crunch against melt, salt against sweet, colour against craving, until the bite hits what they openly call the bliss point — the exact mix that lights up want and dulls fullness. Then designers wrap it in love. Advertisers tie it to laughter, to kindness, to being a good parent. Shops place it where your tired hand must pass. This is not food that happens to be sweet. This is sweetness engineered to create repeat want.

Did you ask for that engineering when you were five?
Did your grandmother, who boiled potatoes and served fruit from a bowl, know this blaze of packs?
Has a single child ever walked past that checkout without being taught by bright eyes and small hands what to beg for?

You know the honest answers. Nobody asked. Nobody knew. Every child is taught.

That is the Sweet Con at work before you ever open your mouth. A belief installed by loving hands and loud machines: sweetness equals love, equals reward, equals normal life. We were conned, you and I, from the cradle and the commercial break. There is no shame in having been conned. There is only anger to place where it belongs — not on you, not on your appetite, but on the Sugar Trap that profits while you blame yourself.

Ask yourself in that aisle, trolley squeaking: if this want were truly mine, why does every maker spend billions to keep it alive in me? Who is hungry here — me, or their ledger?

It is they who are hungry. You were simply fed phoney information until you mistook their hunger for yours.

NORMAL IS NOT PROOF

“But everyone eats it,” you say. “The whole office has biscuits. My family has pudding every night. It must be fine.”

I hear you. We all leaned on that wall. If so many decent, sensible people do it daily, how can it be a trap?

Let me ask you plainly.

If everyone in the street wore shoes a size too small, would your feet stop aching?
If every office at 3pm queued for the same short lift then sat flat, foggy and never satisfied by four, would that fog become energy?
Does a common ache stop being an ache because we all limp together?

You know the honest answers. Common is not healthy. Common is only common brainwashing.

Here is a truth to steady you, not to frighten you. On simple food questionnaires, about one adult in seven and about one child in eight ticks enough boxes to look caught in this same loop with highly tasty food — a share much like drink and smoke. I speak of ticked boxes, not a doctor's label on you. I speak to tell you that you are not odd, not weak, not uniquely broken. You are not the addictive exception in a free world. You are a normal person caught in a normal trap.

Look around that aisle again with clear eyes. Mothers yawning over trolleys. Teenagers drinking bright bottles like water. Men my age patting bellies and joking about willpower. Do they look free? Do they look lit from inside by pleasure? Or do they look tired, run to the sweet shelf on schedule, relieved for ten minutes and then dull again?

Pity them, do not envy them. You once stood where they stand. You are learning to see the bars they still call home.

The Sugar Trap needs normal. Normal stops questions. Normal says: do not investigate, do as we do. The moment you question normal, the con loses its cover. You do not need the crowd to tell you what hunger feels like. Your body told you cleanly before the packs taught you otherwise.

THE PERSONALITY EXCUSE

Then comes the quieter mirror. Not everyone. Me.

“It's in my family. My mother had a sweet tooth. I have an addictive personality. I am the type who gets hooked.”

That line has kept more kind people trapped than any advert. It sounds like honesty. It sounds like wisdom about yourself. It lets the trap off the hook by putting you on it.

We believed it too. We said: others can take one biscuit, I feel like I cant stop once I start, so something in me must be faulty.

The fact is it is the other way around.

The traits we call addictive — the evening grazing, the secret eating, the short temper before the fix, the flat mood after — are the RESULT of living inside the loop, not the cause of it. Feed any living creature sweet hits on an on-off schedule and bingeing, craving and edginess follow. Seen clearly in animals, the pattern follows the schedule. I speak of the pattern, not a label on you. The schedule teaches the behavior. The behavior then gets mistaken for personality.

Tell me: were you born needing a 9pm chocolate fix?
Did your “sweet tooth” exist before sweet doses became daily?
If I took a child who never lived with packs and put her in your kitchen for a year, do you doubt what her evenings would start to look like?

You know the answers. The tooth was made. The type was trained.

And hear my graceful concession, because I want no quarrel on this ground. Even if you did carry a more sensitive nature, even if your family did pass down a quicker hook, this method still frees you completely. Why? Because we are not asking your nerves to fight a battle. We are removing a false belief. A sensitive reader who sees the con clearly walks out just as easily as a stolid one. The door is not locked by character. It was never locked at all.

You are not the addictive type. You are a deceived type, and deceptions lift.

MORE FOR LESS, AND A RUSTLE THAT FIRES

There are two lived signs that fool us into believing in personality. Meet them and they become proof of manufacture.

The first is needing more. Do you remember when one square did the job? When a small bar felt like a feast? Now you buy the family size and finish it without ceremony. You call it greed. I call it crowding.

Each regular hit dulls the answer a little, so the next hit must be larger to chase the same brief lift. The pleasure does not grow. The dose grows while the pleasure shrinks. That is why the box empties faster each year and satisfaction arrives later or not at all. Brain pictures are consistent with this dulling, they prove nothing about your worth, and I leave the pictures to the laboratory. You live the truth at the table: more and more for less and less.

Is that enjoyment? Does a man who needs three cups to feel what one cup once gave praise the third cup? Or does he sense the trap tightening?

The second sign is firing before the bite. A wrapper crackles two rooms away and your mouth waters. A bakery smell rolls down the street and your feet turn. Three o'clock strikes and the hand moves. You think: my body needs it, see how it calls.

That call is learned, not command. After years of pairing clock and wrapper and smell with a quick lift-then-drop, the bell rings on its own. The cue fires before hunger has spoken. It feels urgent because it was trained to feel urgent. Training can be unlearned in days once the belief that backs it dies.

Notice with me: you do not fire at plain potatoes. You do not fire at a boiled egg. You fire at the packs that hired scientists to make you fire. Who built that firing? You, or they?

It was they who built it, drop by drop, jingle by jingle. You only carry the echo. And an echo needs no willpower. It needs truth.

THE LOW MOOD IT PROMISED TO LIFT

“It comforts me when I am low,” you say. “Sugary somethings to get me through. A treat when I feel I deserve one.”

No sentence in this book deserves more tenderness. We all reached for sweetness on grey days and believed the cloud lifted.

Here is the hard line, spoken flat because you deserve plain speech. Men who took a daily sweet dose were more likely to sit in low mood years later, even when earlier mood was taken into account. I speak of a link seen across years in men, not a sentence on you, not proof for every reader, not a promise of gloom if you ever taste sweetness. I speak to return a belief to its true owner.

Does comfort create later heaviness, or does heaviness invite comfort? In this case the arrow was checked both ways, and the daily dose pointed forward to the low, not away from it. The rescuer behaved like the perpetrator again.

Look at your own week as investigator, not as judge. You take a hit for stress at four. Ten minutes bright. Then flat. Then edgy. Then another hit to lift the flat the last hit left. Evening comes and you feel low and blame the day. Was the day grey, or was the lift-crash-repeat painting it grey?

I am not diagnosing your spirits and I am not promising mood by menu. If low mood weighs on you, seek care promptly and put book talk aside until you are cleared — belief work is not care. I am only asking you to stop crediting BAD SUGAR with a rescue it never performed. It borrows brightness from tomorrow and charges interest in fog.

What comforted you on hard days? The pause. The breath. The kindness you gave yourself permission to feel for a minute. The sweet dose only sneaked a ride on that pause and left you lower for the ride. The pause was yours. Take the pause. Leave the pusher.

THE TWO FEARS WITH ONE OWNER

Now we touch the ropes that hold you in the aisle even when you see the packs clearly.

Every trapped eater lives inside the tug-of-war of craving and fear. One arm pulls toward the dose: go on, you need it, just one, life will be grey without it. The other arm pulls away in dread: you will fail, you will binge, you will ruin your health, you will look foolish trying.

You call that wisdom. You call that being realistic about yourself. You wait for fear to settle before you move.

I tell you with total authority: both ropes are held by the same hands.

Fear of failing first. Picture a prison with its door wide open. Sunlight on the yard. Birds. A free road. The prisoner inside grips the bars from the inside and begs to stay. “Outside I will starve. Outside I will cope with nothing. Better a known cell than an unknown road.”

We laugh at him until we hear our own voice in his. “I will fail without my treats. Evenings will stretch unbearable. Work will stop. I need my crutch.” That is not prudence. That is the Sweet Con speaking through prison bars it painted to look like safety. Succumb to that fear and you guarantee the very cell you dread — hooked for the rest of your life, not by need but by refusing to walk through an open door.

Then fear of succeeding, stranger and stronger. Picture a lion born in a cage, door now lifted. He could lope to the plains. He paces the old square instead, round and round, by habit, not by hunger. An onlooker says: see, he loves his cage. He does not love it. His feet remember it.

We pace that square when we whisper: who will I be at birthdays without pudding? Will I still be fun? Will I still belong? Will life be smaller? That is identity fear, not bodily need. It says freedom will steal fun, love, belonging. It mistakes the cage floor for home.

Did the lion need bars to eat, to sleep, to stretch in sun? Does a free eater need a dose to laugh, to love, to belong at a table? Was the laughter ever in the chocolate, or in the faces around it?

Both fears belong to the Trap. Both use your own voice. Both vanish when seen.

Stand in that aisle and feel both pulls at once — want tugging one wrist, dread tugging the other — and name the holder. Not you. Never you. The holders are makers and myths that fed you phoney information for the rest of your life as a hooked customer, while offering you the opposite for the rest of your life as a free eater if you will only walk out. Which rest-of-life do you want? The pacing, or the plains?

There is nothing to brave here. The door is ajar. The lion is already free. Habit paces for a day or two, then feet learn the open ground and wonder why they ever circled.

A SEATBELT, NOT A STEERING WHEEL

“But what if a crumb slips in?” you ask. “A sauce sweetened without my knowing. A bite at a kind grandmother's table. Does one slip mean I am hooked again?”

Hear me clearly, because panic is another rope.

Your body can cope with an accidental trace without drama. Life cooks in shared kitchens. Labels hide. Kind hands offer. Think of a seatbelt. It clicks for the sudden jolt you never planned, not for driving fast on purpose. The body absorbs the jolt. The mind must not steer toward the wall.

A slip of the tongue revives nothing unless the belief is let back in. If the Sweet Con stays dead — if you do not tell yourself the trace proved need or pleasure — the tiny echo passes and you walk on free. Guard the belief, not the bite with panic. Deliberate doses are another matter, and we will close that door together where it belongs.

No guilt. No drama. Only clarity: accident is accident, belief is everything.

CUT THE WIRES

You now see the wires: packs that taught want, normal that silenced questions, personality that took the blame, larger doses that chased smaller lifts, cues that ring like bells, low mood misread as need for rescue, and two fears holding both wrists.

Other voices will rush to rebuild those wires the moment you loosen them. Friends with kindness. Strangers with slogans. Articles with scares. Sellers with new “healthy” sweets. Ex-users with tales of struggle who swear freedom must hurt. Each voice sounds like care. Each replants one wire.

You cannot borrow their eyes. You have your own investigator's eyes now.

I speak as one who escaped and watched thousands walk out after me — not strong-willed thousands, ordinary thousands who saw the manufacture and refused to be its customer again. I promise you with cheerful certainty: the want was never yours, and what was never yours can be handed back without loss.

So hear my command at the height of this clearing, given in warmth and without apology:

9. IGNORE ANY ADVICE THAT CONFLICTS WITH THIS METHOD
Other voices rebuild the Con.

**SUMMARY**
- The want for sweetness was engineered by recipe, pack and placement, then taught as love and normal life.
- Everyone doing it does not make it free or fine, shared habit only proves shared teaching.
- Evening binge and growing doses are the schedule at work, not proof of a faulty personality.
- Needing larger hits for less lift and firing at wrappers and smells are learned echoes of the loop.
- A daily sweet habit points forward to low mood rather than lifting it, the pause comforted while the dose charged fog.
- Fear of failing without sweets and fear of a smaller life without them are both held by the trap, habit pacing by an open door.
