Chapter 12
ORDINARY DAYS

**IN THIS CHAPTER**
- A bright kitchen table on the first free morning
- The supermarket aisle and the checkout queue
- A plain favourite meal eaten hungry to satisfied
- Faces around a table with chocolate in the dim cinema air
- A passing sweet thought met with joy
- A stumbled spoonful and the steady ground after

*There is nothing to watch and nothing to miss; ordinary days are simply yours again.*

BRILLIANT! I'M FREE! You meant it.

THE FIRST MORNING

Wake up and notice what is not there.

No count in the head. No bargain about being good today. No heavy promise to carry. The kettle boils. Light comes through the window. Your body wakes as it always woke, hungry or not yet hungry, and you meet it plainly.

Eat when you are hungry. If you wake hungry, eat. Porridge, eggs, toast, fruit, leftovers from last night — real food that fills and satisfies. If you wake not yet hungry, wait till hunger calls. No rule, no clock, no make-up for yesterday. Hunger tells you. Satisfaction tells you to stop.

That is all there is to do. You do not need to fix breakfast, improve breakfast, earn breakfast. You only need to have it when your body asks and enjoy it till your body is done.

You will notice the morning tastes cleaner. Not because you force it. Because no bright dose came first to shout over it. An apple is crisp. Bread is warm and deep. Tea is tea. We spent years pouring a loud syrup over every flavour and then wondering why food seemed grey without it. The grey was the syrup, not the food.

If you live with diabetes, take medicine that affects blood sugar or appetite, are pregnant, or carry any history of troubled eating or medical risk, talk to your clinician first and follow their advice.

I tell you this as one who lived the same mornings: nothing in you needs coaxing today. Drink water if thirsty. Eat if hungry. Get on with enjoying your life. The day does not need a sweet launch to move. It moves on its own, and you move with it, clear-headed and light.

There is marvellous relief in that plain start. No debt from last night to pay. No fog to lift with another hit. You begin even, and you stay even, because you have walked out of the Sugar Trap.

THE SHOP IN DAYLIGHT

Go to the shop as yourself, not as a guard on duty.

Walk down the same aisle. See the same bright packs, the cartoon smiles, the big family bags shouting value, the little bars crowded by the till to catch a tired hand. Nothing has changed on the shelves. Everything has changed in the eyes that see them.

You do not need to look away. You do not need to grip the trolley and march past chanting. Look straight at them and see them for what they are: doses dressed as treats, each one promising lift and delivering the low that calls for the next.

Watch the other trolleys for a moment, kindly, without a word. A mother throwing in extra packs for the week. A man tired after work reaching for a quick fix for the evening. A teenager filling a basket for a night of grazing. We were all there. We know the roller of bright minute then flat hour then second reach. We know being flat, foggy and never satisfied by seven o'clock and blaming the day.

You feel no envy. How could you envy a queue for a fix you no longer need? You feel pity, warm and genuine, the way you would pity someone still queuing in the rain for a bus you have just stepped off. Poor souls. They still believe the wrapper holds comfort. You know comfort never lived there.

Take what you came for. Fruit that smells of itself. Vegetables with earth still on them. Bread, cheese, fish, meat, rice, plain yoghurt — food that feeds hunger and leaves you satisfied. Hold the packs you love and read them once with open eyes if you like, then put back what is BAD SUGAR and keep what is food. No drama. No mourning. A free person choosing food.

At the checkout, with the small bars winking at tired eyes, let the thought come if it comes: "a little something for later." Let it come and let it go. It is a dead echo, not an order. Smile inwardly. Feel the lift in the chest that says you need never join that queue again. That ease is freedom doing its ordinary work.

A MEAL THAT IS FAVOURITE

Sit down hungry to a meal you love.

Not a perfect meal. Not a worthy meal. Your meal. Hot soup on a cold day. Market vegetables roasted till sweet in their own way. Fresh bread torn, not counted. A bowl of berries. Meat or beans, rice or potatoes, cooked simply and eaten with appetite.

Notice how hunger is the seasoning we forgot. We chased stronger and stronger hits till we could hardly taste dinner. Now hunger returns, honest and clean, and satisfaction returns with it — that quiet full stop that says: enough, lovely, done. Like a child dropping a peach mid-bite to run off laughing, the body stops when it is done, with no guilt and no counting.

Stay with the plate. Talk. Laugh. Take seconds if hunger still calls. Stop when satisfaction arrives. There is no prize for emptiness and no punishment in fullness. Eat what you want, when you are hungry, stop when you are satisfied, and never punish yourself again. That is not a diet rule. That is the body heard again.

We used to call dessert love. We said food is love and meant a sweet finish. Look now: where was the love? In the sugar, or in the faces, the warmth, the hour together, the hands that cooked? Even the chocolate in the dim cinema air never made the laughter; the laughter and the company and the story made the hour glow, and the sweet only rode along.

Let the meal be the pleasure. Let company be the pleasure. Let hunger met be the pleasure. BAD SUGAR added nothing to any of them. It only borrowed their light and sent the bill later in fog and craving.

When you rise from the table satisfied, with a clear mouth and a clear head, mark it: this is what eating feels like when no dose stands between you and your appetite. Wonderful in its plainness. Yours for good.

EVENINGS ON THE SOFA

Evening was our tender hour. Tired, a little hollow, the day done. The sofa, the screen, the hand to the cupboard before the mind had decided.

Live that hour now, in the same place, with nothing changed but you.

Make tea. Have fruit if hungry. Have a proper supper if supper is due. Sit down. Feel the evening settle. If a faint hollow brushes you — that empty, twitchy, slightly shaky, need-something-sweet-now feeling we all knew — greet it for what it is.

It is not hunger. It is not need. It is the Nibbler dying.

Small, faint, already starving for lack of food. A grumble with no teeth. It passes within minutes when you do nothing at all, and within a few days it scarcely visits. Do not feed it. Do not fight it. Smile at it. Drink water. Stretch. Keep watching the programme. Get on with the evening you are already enjoying. Each time you smile past it, it weakens, and you grow clearer.

Will it shout? Will it test you? Will it prove you still need the dose?

No. That was the old lie that kept us paying. The physical echo is tiny and brief. The belief was the giant, and the belief is dead. What visits now is memory in the body, fading fast because there is nothing left to believe in.

And the joy underneath grows. No wrapper rustle to hide. No box empty before the end of the show. No regret before the swallow ends. You watch to the end with a clear head, you sleep deeper, you wake cleaner. The evening is yours, not the cupboard's.

This is not vigilance. Vigilance is standing guard over a treasure you fear to lose. You stand guard over nothing. The treasure — bright afternoons, calm evenings, clean hunger — is inside you, and no passing pack can take it unless you hand it back with your belief.

WHEN SWEET THOUGHTS PASS

They will pass. A smell in the street. A birthday table. Someone eating beside you on a train. A tired hour whispering: "just one."

You cannot stop thoughts coming, and you would not want to. The mind thinks. Let it think.

You change what the thought means.

Before, a sweet thought was an order. It pulled the hand. It started the tug. Now the same thought is a bell. It rings, and you answer with joy, at once, without debate:

BRILLIANT! I'M FREE!

Think it the instant the picture crosses your mind. In the shop. At the table. On the sofa. Mean it. Feel the chest lift. Feel relief flood in that you need never take it, never chase it, never count it again.

Do you miss it? Look honestly. What is there to miss — the flare then the demand for another flare, the mouth coating, the fog after? Does a free person miss a cage? Do you envy the hand reaching for the box? Or do you pity it, kindly, knowing the reach ends in emptiness while you sit satisfied and clear?

That pity is your shield. Envy reopens the door. Pity keeps it closed without effort. Say inwardly: poor thing, still caught. I am happy to be free. And turn back to your life, lighter than before the thought came. Each passing thought, met this way, makes freedom stronger, not weaker. The dead enemy becomes the reminder of escape.

Never argue with the thought. Never weigh it. Never say: "maybe, maybe not, let me think." Thinking is the trap's doorway. Rejoicing is freedom's doorway. One joyful sentence closes the first door and opens the second.

IF A SPOONFUL SLIPS

Hear me now, because kindness here keeps you free.

If some day, by accident or in a tired moment, a bite of BAD SUGAR passes your lips — a sauce you did not know, a cake pressed on you before you could speak, an old reflex taking a sweet from a bowl — do not freeze. Do not wail. Do not declare the freedom broken.

An accidental crumb passes through the body like a bump in the road. It revives nothing on its own. What matters is the very next thought.

If the next thought is: "Well, that proves I am still hooked, I may as well carry on," that thought is the Sweet Con knocking, not the bite talking. Slam that door with joy. Remind yourself: it tasted cloying, loud then dull, and gave nothing. I see it. I am still free. And rejoice you are free.

If the next thought is: "I got away with it, so a deliberate one will be fine," hear that too as the same knock in a brighter coat. Getting away with a bump is no reason to steer into the ditch. A deliberate dose re-teaches the mouth that relief lives in a wrapper and wakes hours of chatter for nothing gained. An accident teaches nothing unless you let the old idea climb in with it.

So notice the slip. Learn from it the way you learn from a rumble strip — a small shake that says: keep eyes open, stay happy, keep going straight. Close the door at once. Eat next when hungry. Smile that the freedom holds because the belief holds. Guard the meaning, and the doing looks after itself.

You have not failed. You have not restarted a count. There is no count. There is only a free eater who stumbled on a stone, laughed, and walked on with clearer eyes.

One deliberate exception kept on purpose is different. That is not a stumble. That is reopening the decision and inviting the whole Trap back in uniform. Bring no sweet with you on purpose. Let accidents be accidents, lessons be lessons, and freedom be freedom.

TELL NO ONE HOW TO LIVE

You will be tempted to preach. Hold back, for your own sake and theirs.

When you feel bright and clear, you will want to shake the tired eater beside you and say: "Can't you see? Look what it does to you." Say nothing of the sort. Preaching breeds argument, and argument drags you back into defending what needs no defence. The newly free mind debating the Trap aloud can talk itself back toward doubt.

Let your ease do the talking. Eat with appetite. Refuse a dose with a smile and no sermon: "No thanks, I don't need it — I'm happy as I am." Take fruit if hungry. Take laughter anyway. People notice lightness more than lectures. If someone asks, truly asks, tell your story simply: how trapped you felt, what you saw, how easy escape feels now. Then leave them to investigate. You cannot free a person while pulling their arm. You free them by showing freedom looks wonderful.

And keep your ears closed to opposite sermons. Trapped eaters, kind as they are, will tell you willpower tales: how hard they found it, how they allow Fridays, how fruit is sugar too so nothing matters. Smile and let those voices pass. Their struggle was the wrong method talking, not truth about you. You escaped by seeing, not by straining, and seeing does not unsee.

Live your ordinary days visibly free. That is the only teaching that ever worked.

CHANGE NOTHING ELSE

This matters more than it sounds: change nothing else in your life.

Do not start a new regime to protect freedom. Do not avoid birthdays, cinemas, holidays, cafés. Do not clear the house like a fortress or ban others from eating as they eat. Inhabit every old place confidently, as a free eater, to prove to your own eyes that no place owns you.

Go to the same café. Sit at the same table. Order what a free eater orders when hungry. Walk the same evening street. Celebrate the same birthdays, with the same love, minus the dose that never made the love. Each ordinary hour lived without BAD SUGAR is proof that life never needed it.

Your body returns to its clean rhythm on its own. Hunger sharpens. Satisfaction deepens. Afternoons brighten without the lift-crash. Evenings calm without the chase. Weight, skin, teeth, energy — whatever was strained steadies in its own time, without you chasing it. You did not quit to earn a bonus. The bonus comes because you stopped poisoning sweet hours. Let it come. Do not count it.

And when doubt whispers, as it may once in a blue hour: "Was it really nothing? Is life duller now?" Answer with your own days as witness.

Are mornings duller without fog? Are meals greyer without the coating that dulled them? Are celebrations colder without the box that borrowed their warmth? Are you weaker without the hit that made the low?

You know. Duller would be flat, foggy and never satisfied at dusk, hand in the box. Brighter is this: hunger met, food enjoyed, evening clear, mind quiet. Freedom is not less. Freedom is more of everything the Trap pretended to give.

13. NEVER REOPEN THE DECISION
A passing thought is a dead enemy, not an order.

Say it once and keep it. The question is closed. A thought may visit; the decision does not move. You do not weigh, you rejoice. You do not mourn, you pity. You do not debate, you celebrate. That single stand keeps ordinary days ordinary — marvellous, free, and yours for good.

Walk on now, light and certain. Morning, shop, table, sofa — all lived, all easy, all free. Whenever sweetness crosses your mind, let the old bell ring the new joy:

BRILLIANT! I'M FREE!

**SUMMARY**
- Mornings need no sweet launch; hunger eaten to satisfaction tastes cleaner and starts the day even.
- Shops hold no power when doses are seen plainly; other trolleys invite pity, never envy.
- Real meals eaten hungry are favourite on their own; the scene gave the joy the sweet only borrowed.
- Evening hollows are faint dying echoes to smile at, not hunger and not orders.
- Any passing sweet thought is answered at once with joy, not debate, and leaves freedom stronger.
- A stumbled bite is a rumble lesson to close at once, never proof of failure and never licence for a kept exception.
- No preaching and no avoidance are needed; change nothing else and live every old place as a free eater.
