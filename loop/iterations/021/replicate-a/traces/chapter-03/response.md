Chapter 3
WHAT DOES IT DO FOR YOU

**IN THIS CHAPTER**
An ordinary Tuesday. Two columns on the kitchen table. Teeth in the morning mirror. The hand that lifts you out.

*Forget what it costs you for a moment — tell me what it ever actually gives you.*

You were caught in the Sugar Trap, not weak.

That seeing lets us ask a colder question, and I want you to meet it head-on with an open mind. Up to now you have argued the case the way every sweet eater argues it, the way I argued it for years. Yes, it harms me. Yes, teeth, weight, money, fog. But it gives me something back. It makes life sweeter. It helps. The pleasure outweighs the price.

That is the wrong courtroom. While we argue harm against benefit, the benefit never has to prove itself. It sits in the room unexamined because we are too busy weighing it.

So we will change the court.

THE WRONG COURTROOM

Think how we defend BAD SUGAR in ordinary talk.

“I know it is bad for me, but I love it.”

“I know I should cut down, but I get so much from it.”

“I know the crash comes, but without it the day would be grey.”

Do you hear what has happened in each sentence? The harm is admitted. The benefit is assumed. No one asks for evidence for the second half. The whole debate becomes how bad is the harm, how much can I get away with, what does the latest headline say, can I offset it with brushing or exercise or being good all week.

That debate can run forever. That is why it suits the con.

A conned mind loves the harm debate because it never touches the load-bearing lie. You can read warnings for years, nod at every one, feel a chill at the dentist, feel shame at the bill, promise to be careful — and keep reaching. Why? Because underneath you still believe there is something real to lose. You believe BAD SUGAR, for all its costs, hands you a genuine good.

What if that good is not there at all?

I am not asking you whether BAD SUGAR does more harm than good. I am asking a simpler, harder question. What good? Name it. Point to one true benefit that belongs to the dose itself and to nothing else, that a free person without BAD SUGAR would lack.

This is the only ledger that matters, and I want you to hold it in your hands today.

THE TUESDAY AUDIT

Do this with me in your head, not as theory but as your own yesterday.

Take an ordinary Tuesday. Not a birthday. Not a binge you are ashamed of. A normal moderate day, the kind you call harmless.

Morning. Tea with two sugars and a sweetened cereal you eat standing up. Mid-morning biscuit with the second cup. Afternoon chocolate bar from the drawer to get through the emails. A fizzy drink on the way home. Evening dessert because the meal feels unfinished without it, then a few sweets on the sofa while you watch.

Write down what each dose did TO you. Be honest and concrete.

Money out, dose by dose, small coins that make a large pile by month end. Time out, queueing, unwrapping, chewing without tasting, clearing wrappers. Teeth coated, that furry film by late afternoon. Stomach bloated after the evening round. Mouth dry in the night. Morning breath sour. Sleep broken and light. Mood up for minutes, then flat and edgy, then reaching again.

Any moderate eater knows that list. We have all lived it. We pay it daily without calling it payment.

Now write down, in the next column, what each dose did FOR you. Not what the hour did. Not what the rest did. Not what the meal did. What did the BAD SUGAR itself add that was real and lasting and yours?

You reach for the usual words. Taste. Lift. Treat. Break. Comfort. Reward.

Hold each one up for a second. Did the morning cereal taste wonderful, or did you barely notice it while thinking about the day? Did the mid-morning biscuit lift you, or did it pause a gnawing restlessness for ten minutes and then leave you foggier? Did the afternoon bar fix the emails, or did the emails get done despite the bar while the bar took the credit? Did the evening dessert complete the meal, or did it blunt the clean satisfaction the meal had almost given you and leave you hunting the packet?

Hour by hour, the first column fills. The second column stays empty.

Ask yourself, with your own Tuesday as witness:

If each dose truly helped, why do you feel less steady at 4pm than at 9am before the first dose?

If each dose truly satisfied, why does one always call for the next, and why does the evening end with more wanting, not less?

If this is enjoyment, why is so little of it enjoyed — eaten fast, half-tasted, followed by regret before the swallow has gone?

There is only one honest reading of your own log. Plenty is being done. Nothing is being given.

WHAT IT DOES TO YOU IS PLENTY

Let me speak plainly about the TO side, because softening it would keep you negotiating.

Frequent free-sugar doses bathe the teeth in acid through the day. That acid is what rots teeth. Brushing and fluoride matter, of course they do, and no decent person would say otherwise — but they cannot cancel a mouth kept sweet all day. Any dentist will tell you the pattern: not one feast, but the steady drip, the sip and nibble habit, keeps the acid working long after the taste is gone. That is a real bodily stake, paid in enamel, paid in mornings in the chair.

And the wider picture is just as flat. Public guidance sets the daily ceiling for free sugars far lower than most of us eat without thinking, because ordinary eating now sits far above it. Feeling fine proves nothing here. We all felt fine while the pile grew. I am speaking of population risk, not a sentence on you — and if a change in eating could carry medical risk for you, your clinician’s word comes first.

Do you feel the chill in that? Good. Let it land fully. Tooth by tooth, coin by coin, foggy hour by foggy hour, for the rest of your life hooked and paying and calling it normal — that is one future the Trap holds out to you.

I do not want you to change from fear. Fear makes you clutch. I want you to see why fear is not needed, because the other side of the ledger is worse for the Trap than any warning. Even if none of that harm touched you, even if teeth never ached and guidance never existed, you would still have paid all day for nothing back.

Clear and light and at ease in your own mouth and mood, for the rest of your life free and no longer paying — that is the other future, and it asks nothing from you but seeing.

Do you see the switch? I am not balancing harm against benefit. I am showing you harm is not the point. The point is there was never a benefit to balance it with.

NAME THE THING YOU THINK YOU WOULD MISS

I know the objection rising in you, because it rose in me.

“But I like it. It is my treat. It gets me through. Take everything else, but do not tell me there is nothing in that chocolate at midnight, that cake with friends, that sweet tea when I am done in.”

Is that true? Let us test it, not with slogans but with your own mouth.

You call BAD SUGAR a genuine treat or fuel.

Say the phrase slowly and hear what it claims. A genuine treat or fuel. Genuine means real, not borrowed, not pretended. Treat means something that adds joy beyond the ordinary. Fuel means something that powers you.

When was the last time a dose did either?

Think of the midnight chocolate. Did you taste the first square, or did you taste the idea of it, the day finally over, the sofa, the quiet? Strip the sofa and the quiet away and eat that same bar standing in a cold garage under a bare bulb. Is it still a treat? Think of the sweet tea when you are done in. Is it the sugar that rests you, or the sitting, the warmth, the pause, the work finished? Give a free child that same over-sweet drink when he is rested and happy and playing. He sips, grimaces, leaves it. Where is the genuine fuel then?

We credit the dose for what the moment does. We have done it so long we cannot separate them without looking.

And here is the question that cracks the phrase open.

If BAD SUGAR were a genuine treat or fuel, would you need to promise, limit, hide, and restart around it? Do you set rules for peaches? Do you eat a proper dinner in secret and vow never again? Does real food leave you flat, foggy and secretive, hunting more?

You know it does not. The foods that truly feed you end in satisfaction. Only the con ends in wanting more and calls the wanting pleasure.

What if the hand that lifts you out each afternoon is the same hand that pushed you down that morning? Just ask it. Do not argue it yet. Let the question sit beside your Tuesday log. Who made the low that the next dose claims to rescue?

It is the other way around from everything you were told. The rescuer is the perpetrator. The relief you call benefit is debt collection.

It does plenty TO you. It does nothing FOR you.

Let that verdict stand. Do not soften it, do not add but or except, do not file it for later. A sentence that clear either survives your own day as evidence or it falls. Test it against tomorrow’s first three doses and watch which column fills.

3. JUDGE ONLY BY WHAT IT DOES FOR YOU
Forget harm versus benefit and ask for the benefit.

**SUMMARY**
- The harm debate keeps the assumed benefit safe because it is never asked to prove itself.
- A normal day of doses fills the cost column with money, time, teeth, mood and sleep, while the benefit column stays empty.
- Frequent sweet doses keep acid working on teeth through the day, and ordinary intake sits far above public guidance, though feeling fine hides it.
- The words treat and fuel claim something real and lasting that the dose itself never delivers in your own mouth.
- Credit for rest, warmth, company and pause belongs to the moment, never to the dose that rode along.
- Once the benefit is missing, stopping is not loss but the end of paying for nothing.
