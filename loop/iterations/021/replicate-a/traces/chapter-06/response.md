Chapter 6
HUNGER, SATISFACTION AND REAL FOOD

**IN THIS CHAPTER**
The morning market. A plain table at noon. Small hunger answered.

*Hunger met by real food is the feast itself — BAD SUGAR only muted it.*

You know now the lift was never fuel.

So a quieter fear speaks up, and I want to meet it kindly because we have all felt it:

“What if eating goes grey? I love food. Dessert is love. Surely a meal with no sweet finish will be dull?”

I understand that voice. I lived with it for years. We learned to call the sweet finish the point of the meal, the warm glow at the end that made it all worthwhile. We learned to say company is nice, the main course is nice, but the dessert is love.

Is that true?

Let me ask you plainly, from inside your own kitchen memory:

When you were truly hungry, did plain food ever taste dull?

When you sat down empty and ate the first mouthfuls of something simple and fresh, did you need a label to tell you it was marvellous?

And when you finished a pile of BAD SUGAR after you were already full, did you feel loved — or stuffed, coated, and oddly untouched?

There is only one honest answer. Hunger makes food sing. Fullness piled on fullness mutes it.

WHAT IF EATING GOES GREY

We were conned into believing joy lived in the sweet hit at the end.

Think of your favourite ordinary meal, not the birthday cake photographed for others, but the meal you actually dream about when you are empty. Bread with a crackling crust. Soup that steams the glasses. Tomatoes that smell of the field. A peach that runs down the chin.

Why does that memory glow? Not because a dose was added. Because hunger was met.

BAD SUGAR never gave that glow. It borrowed it. It arrived after hunger was already fading, shouted over satisfaction, and left you too full to remember what satisfied felt like. Then we blamed plain food for being grey, when grey was what the last doses left on the tongue.

The fact is, a child does not need teaching to eat with joy. Hunger calls, food answers, delight happens, and then delight ends. No counting. No guilt. No bargaining for one more bite to make it better.

We did not lose that ability. We buried it under fixes.

THE MARKET TABLE AT NOON

Come with me and live it once, hour by hour, as it truly is.

Morning. The market air cool. Crates of greens, peppers glossy, herbs sharp, bread still warm from the oven stacked in brown paper. Your stomach gives that clear hollow signal. Not panic. Not pain. A clean emptiness that says: now.

You buy what catches the eye. Nothing measured. Nothing forbidden. Real food you actually like.

Noon. A plain table. No ceremony. You sit down hungry and eat.

First bite of bread. Crackle, chew, salt. It tastes bright because you are empty enough to taste it. Vegetables cooked simply, olive oil, lemon. Sweetness where sweetness belongs — inside a tomato, inside a fig, inside an apple eaten whole, juice and fibre together as nature packed it. You notice. You savour. You slow without trying to slow.

Mid-plate. The edge leaves hunger. Warmth spreads. Thoughts clear. Mood lifts in the quiet way real refuelling lifts — no buzz, no shout, only return to yourself.

Then comes the signal we forgot to trust. Satisfaction. Not stuffed. Not regretful. Just done. The body says enough, and enough feels marvellous. You stop, like the child who drops the half-eaten peach and runs off to play, because play is more interesting than stuffing.

Ask yourself inside that meal:

Did you miss the dose? Or did you forget it existed until thought put it back?

Did plain food taste grey — or did hunger colour it brighter than any icing could?

And who made that pleasure — the packet, or your own empty stomach meeting honest food and friends at a table?

Credit where credit belongs. The love was the hunger answered. The love was the company, the daylight, the bread still warm. BAD SUGAR was only ever sneaking a ride on hunger and calling the ride its own.

That is not theory. That is your own mouth remembering what it was built for.

YOUR BODY ALREADY KNOWS HOW

Here is the simple bodily truth, stated flat so you can lean on it.

Your body makes the glucose your brain needs from ordinary meals, hour by hour, from protein and stores and its own steady making. You do not need a sweet rescue to keep the lights on.

Take that in. It means a life with no BAD SUGAR is not deprivation. It is coherence. The machine was designed to run on hunger and real food, and it still knows how.

Let me be exact about the line, so no confusion steals your ease. When this book says sugar, take it to mean BAD SUGAR — refined and added free sugars and junk processed snack-carbs eaten as sweet hits, drinks, confectionery, biscuits, cakes, desserts, sweetened cereals and their grazing cousins. Natural sweetness inside whole fruit, vegetables and plain meals is not the target. It belongs on your table. Enjoy it hungry and stop at satisfied, with nothing to prove.

(If you live with diabetes or take anything that touches blood sugar or appetite, are pregnant, or live with any risk around food, follow your clinician first and take this book as belief change only.)

Eat what you want, when you are hungry, stop when you are satisfied, and never punish yourself again.

Read that again with fresh ears. It is not a diet rule. It is permission to be an eater again. No clock to obey. No good-food sermon. No bad-food shame. No punishment after pleasure, because real pleasure needs no punishment.

Watch what returns when BAD SUGAR stops shouting over it.

Morning hunger returns, clear and friendly. Food tastes more, not less. You eat with appetite and leave the table light. Afternoon stays even without bargaining. Evening finds you satisfied rather than hunting. This is not a system to maintain. This is baseline coming home by itself.

I promise you this from my own escape: the first hungry meal after the con lifts is a revelation. Colours brighter. Bread more bread-like. Fruit sweeter than you remembered, because your tongue is clean enough to taste it. You will wonder how you ever called that grey.

So hear the old whisper when it comes —

“But I deserve a little treat to make the meal special” —

and answer from the table, not from theory: the meal was already special. Hunger made it special. You made it special by bringing appetite to it. A dose after satisfaction cannot add love. It can only mute the love you just felt.

It does nothing for the meal. The meal does everything for you.

Rejoice that you see it. Pity the hunt, do not obey it. Eating itself is joy, and joy was never in the packet.

6. TRUST HUNGER AND SATISFACTION
Eat when hungry, stop when satisfied, enjoy real food.

**SUMMARY**
- Food joy never lived in the sweet finish. It lived in hunger answered by real food noticed and enjoyed.
- A plain favourite meal eaten hungry tastes bright, satisfies cleanly, and ends itself at satisfied without guilt or counting.
- Your body makes what the brain needs from ordinary meals, so living with no BAD SUGAR leaves nothing missing.
- Natural sweetness inside whole fruit, vegetables and plain meals belongs on the table and is to be enjoyed as part of hunger met.
- Satisfaction is a friendly signal to stop, not a rule to obey, and stopping there leaves eating marvellous.
