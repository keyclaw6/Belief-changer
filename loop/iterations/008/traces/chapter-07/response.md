### "IT'S NORMAL, IT'S SOCIABLE" — THE LAST LINE ON YOUR FOR LIST

"It's just normal."

"Everyone does it."

"It's the sociable thing — dessert is the point of the meal."

Do you hear how different this reason sounds? The others at least pretended to be about you. Taste. Energy. Reward. This one is about everyone else. We tell ourselves it is not really our decision at all. We are just fitting in. Being polite. Doing what is done.

We have already crossed three supposed benefits off your ledger and found them empty. Taste was not the hit — it was the occasion sneaking a ride. Energy was the other way around — the hit creates the tiredness it relieves. Reward was not love — it was the Lifeguard who pushed you in and then sold you the rescue. If those three were genuine, you would not need this fourth. But we reach for it anyway. Why?

Because this last one feels safest. It asks nothing of your body and promises the warmest prize: belonging. If everyone does it, it must be okay. If everyone does it, refusing would be odd. Who wants to be odd at a birthday?

I understand that wish. I honour it. You want to belong. Of course you do. We all do. We learned to belong early, before we ever learned to choose sweets. So this belief borrows its power from a good place — your kindness, your sociability, your wish not to stand apart.

But ask the plain trap question whose only honest answer concedes the point.

If everyone doing something made it good for you, would you need a book to help you stop doing it?

If it were genuinely normal — like drinking water, like laughing, like sleeping — would anyone ever say "I can't stop once I start"? Would anyone hide the empty packet?

You know the answer. You have always known. The fact that everyone does it is not a benefit to you. It is a description of how many of us were caught. Does a crowded trap make the trap less of a trap? Or more so?

We need to audit "normal" together. Not with willpower. Not with guilt. Just by looking. For five minutes, look at normal with clear eyes. Then see whether you still want to call it a reason to keep feeding the Sugar Trap. And see whether sociability needs BAD SUGAR at all to stay warm. It does not. It never did.

You were not choosing BAD SUGAR because it was a genuine treat or lift. You were calling it a genuine treat or lift because everyone around you called it that first. Words taught before you could weigh them. That is brainwashing, not belonging. And brainwashing can be undone the moment you see it.

Shall we look?

### THE SUPERMARKET AUDIT — WHAT NORMAL ACTUALLY LOOKS LIKE

Do the audit with me now. Not later. Now.

Picture your normal day where you tell yourself you are moderate. You say "I don't keep it in the house" or "just a treat" or "once a week is fine." Now count what normal actually puts in the trolley without you noticing.

Morning coffee with two sugars. Juice that feels like fruit. A "healthy" granola bar. White bread at lunch. Ketchup, sauce, yoghurt with added sweetness. Biscuits in the office tin. A soft drink at 3pm "to get me through." Cake because someone brought it in. Then dessert because it is dessert. None of those felt like decisions. They felt like normal.

Do you see? Normal is not one cake on birthdays. Normal is a quiet river of BAD SUGAR running through the whole day, every day. The supermarket tells the same story. Walk one aisle. How many packets need sugar to be palatable? How many would sit uneaten without it? The lonely carbs — white bread, crackers, puffed things — that need jam or chocolate spread to be wanted at all? We have seen them before. They are gatecrashers. They invite company over because alone they bore you.

The fact is, we live in a food environment where free sugars are added far beyond what population guidance ever intended. The World Health Organization recommends less than ten percent of energy from free sugars, with extra benefit below five percent — about fifty grams and then about twenty-five for many adults. That is not a personal prescription — your clinician guides you there — but as a population picture it is stark. Most of us eat far above it, every week, without feeling we are doing anything unusual. Is that moderate? Or is that saturated?

Now picture the children's party. Table covered. Fizzy drinks, sweets, cupcakes with bright icing. Parents smiling tightly while kids spike and crash and cry. Is anyone choosing? Or is everyone following the script that says party equals sugar? Does the child who eats the apple first feel deprived? No. Joy is in the games, the friends, the being together. The sugar is just along for the ride. Again.

Look at the office biscuits. Does anyone crave them at 9am before they appear? Rarely. Then someone opens the tin. The smell, the crinkle, the cue fires. You think "go on, be sociable." Was that hunger? Or was it a cue doing its job? You know.

We call this normal because it is common. But common is not the same as natural. Poison ivy is common. Do we call it normal to eat it?

If your friends all limped because their shoes were too tight, would you keep wearing tight shoes to be sociable? You would pity the limp, not envy it.

So ask. Does "everyone does it" describe a benefit you receive? Or does it describe availability that made a choice for you before you arrived? Which is it?

We were not born wanting biscuits at meetings. We were taught that biscuits belong at meetings. That is not culture. That is conditioning. And conditioning, once seen, loses its grip.

### WHEN EVERYONE'S SICK, NO ONE CALLS IT SICK

Let me give you the image that holds this whole chapter, as Carr would. You need it.

A frog sits in cool water. The heat rises so slowly it never jumps. Degree by degree. The frog calls the warming water normal. By the time it would notice, it is cooked. Not because the frog is stupid. Because the change was too gradual to trigger a jump.

That is what has happened to us with BAD SUGAR. Call it the Boiling Frog.

When everyone's breakfast is sweet, no one calls breakfast sweet. When every child expects a sweet after savoury, no one calls that expectation strange. When every afternoon ends with a hit "to get me through," no one calls the 3pm crash a warning. We call it life. We call it normal. We say "the roller coaster" as if it were weather, not a ride we were strapped to.

But step out of the water for a second. Look with a non-user's eyes.

See thequeue at the coffee shop where almost every drink is sweetened. See the lunchbox where the "treat" is the main event. See the evening where rest needs a wrapper to count as rest. Does that look like a free people freely choosing sweetness? Or does it look like a population managed by availability?

The fact is, on validated scales that ask addiction-like questions about highly palatable food — not a diagnosis, just a careful questionnaire — about fourteen in a hundred adults tick enough boxes to cross the threshold, and about twelve in a hundred children do too. That does not label you. It does not say you are diseased. It says something kinder and more useful: you are not uniquely weak. The trap is common. Common enough that calling it normal hides how common the difficulty is.

Would you call that scale a proof of safety? Or a measure of how many of us were taught the same wrong lesson and now live the same tug?

Think of other normals that stopped being normal. Smoking in offices was normal. Everyone did it. Did that make it healthy? Did that make the room fresh? No. It made the air thick while everyone called thick air normal. One day we opened a window. The air was always meant to be fresh. We had just forgotten.

Your body has a fresh-air baseline too. We will return to it soon with Nature's Guide. For now, hold this: normalised is not natural. Frequent is not healthy. Common is not chosen.

Do you feel the shift? From "everyone does it so it must be okay" to "everyone does it so the trap must be big"? One keeps you in. One lifts you out.

Look at normal again. Not with fear. With clarity. If everyone limped, you would not keep limping to fit in. You would be relieved you could walk freely.

Shall we test that relief now?

### SOCIABLE? THE PEOPLE MADE IT WARM, NOT THE DOSE

Grant this fully, because it is true. Sociability is real. Love of sharing food is real. Eating together is one of the most beautiful things humans do. I am not here to make you solitary. I am here to show you that sociability never needed BAD SUGAR to be sociable.

Take your warmest "sociable" memory. Birthday cake with singing. Barbecue with friends. Office cake for someone's farewell. Cinema sweets passed along the row. What made it warm?

Was it the sugar? Or was it the singing? Was it the light of candles on faces you love? Was it the laughter, the break from work, the feeling of being included? Was it being shoulder to shoulder, doing the same thing at the same time?

Do the isolation test honestly, as we did with taste and energy.

Keep everything. Remove BAD SUGAR only. Keep the table, the people, the talk, the rest, the pride, the relief of being together. Keep the fresh foods you genuinely like — berries, fruit, cheese, nuts, bread that still tastes like bread, sparkling water, coffee that tastes like coffee. Keep the ritual: plates, serving, passing, toasting. Does the warmth vanish? Does the love leave the room? Does the film get less funny?

Of course not. Your chest knows before your brain argues. The occasion stays sweet when the hit is gone. Sweeter, because no one is sticky, queasy, or quietly negotiating the next dose.

Now test the reverse. Keep BAD SUGAR. Remove the people, the occasion, the warmth. Sit alone in an empty kitchen with a bag of sweets and no celebration at all. Is that sociable? Is that belonging? Or is that just you and a hit, faintly lonely, wanting more?

You see the credit error. The Sugar Trap sneaks a ride on your belonging. You do the work — you show up, you love, you laugh — and it claims the credit after. We mislabel who made us feel good. We say "dessert made the meal." Did it? Or did the meal, made with care, eaten without rush, with people you like, make the meal — and dessert just hitched a lift?

Will your friends like you less if you eat fresh food freely with them and feel light after? Will your child love the party less if the games are great and the food is real and abundant? Would you love them less?

Be careful with the fine print of this excuse. It says "I don't want to be awkward." But who is being awkward? The person who eats happily and freely from what they like? Or the trap that taught you that belonging must be sweetened to count?

You do not need to attack friends or family to see this. I am not asking you to judge them. I am asking you to pity the trap, not the people. They were conned in the same way we were. Warm to the person, harsh to the con. Their plates do not prove a benefit to you. They prove availability did its work on everyone before anyone asked.

So ask the plain question that ends the debate. Does sociability work with any food or none? Has a single good conversation ever needed a biscuit to hold it together?

No. Never. The conversation held the biscuit. Not the other way around.

You can keep all the warmth and lose the debt. That is not deprivation. That is reclamation.

### RECENTLY NORMALISED, NOT NATURALLY NORMAL

When did this normal begin? Our bodies did not change. Our shelves did.

Your grandparents ate sweets. Of course they did. But not like this. Not sweetened drinks with every meal. Not added sugar in bread, sauce, yoghurt, cereal, and snacks designed to be more-ish. Not a bakery on every corner and a vending machine in every corridor. Not a children's world where sweetness is the default language of love. Genes did not change in two generations. Availability did. Portions did. Engineering did.

Is that natural? Or normalised?

Think of sugar at the historical scale. A hundred years ago, free sugars were occasional. Now they are environmental — present, pushed, cheap, everywhere. Did your sweet tooth evolve in that time? No. Your exposure did. So the "I have always had a sweet tooth" story reverses cause and effect. It was not your tooth that called for more sugar. It was more sugar that trained your tooth.

We tell ourselves "a little of what you fancy" is natural wisdom. But what if what you fancy was installed? What if the fancy was manufactured and then called normal so you would stop questioning it? Would that still feel wise? Or would it feel like following a sign someone else painted?

Look at any other appetite by contrast. Do you crave fresh foods in the same frantic, "I need something RIGHT NOW" way? Or do you feel hunger that is calm and open — "I could eat"? One is need. One is urge. Normal hunger says you could eat an apple, a carrot, nuts, a meal. The Sugar Trap says only one very sweet hit will do. Is that hunger? Or is it a whisper that learned the word hunger?

And notice how normal protects itself with language we all repeat. You have heard them — we have all said them:

"just a treat"

"once a week is fine"

"I don't keep it in the house" — while the house still fills every shop you enter

"I've been good this week, so I deserve it"

"it's fuel not a treat" then "it's a treat not fuel" — whichever justification fits the hour

Do those lines prove freedom? Or do they prove the trap's dialect is shared? When everyone uses the same lines to explain the same behaviour, does that prove choice? Or does it prove a shared script?

We were not foolish. We were taught the script before we could read the label. That is not shameful. It is common. But common is precisely why we must be heroic enough to be apart for a moment.

Who wants their freedom to depend on what the aisle does? Do you want the aisle to decide when you feel sociable? Or do you want you to decide?

You get to decide. Now.

### THE LAB BEHIND THE AISLE — WHO MADE NORMAL SWEET

Let me take you, briefly, behind the shelf. We will go there fully in the next chapter. For now, just a glimpse through the lab door so you understand why normal tastes the way it does.

Have you ever wondered why one biscuit leads so reliably to three? Why "I can't stop once I start" feels true in your own mouth? It is not weak will. It is weaponised abundance.

In quiet labs, people in white coats dial sugar and fat and crunch and melt against each other until the product hits the "bliss point" — the ratio your brain finds hardest to refuse. They test colour. They test crinkle. They test the pause between bite and want. They engineer food to be more-ish by design, not by accident. Not to nourish you faster. To make you reach again.

I do not want you to feel frightened now. I want you to feel exonerated. You were not out-eaten. You were out-designed. You, one person with a life to live, facing teams whose job is to make "just one more" feel inevitable. Of course the aisles look normal. They were designed to look normal while acting like traps.

Does that sound like sociability? Or does it sound like a system that rents your sociability to sell you doses?

Think of the money and hours. Not to scare you — to show cost plainly before we put fear aside. Every normal day with normal hits costs money, time, attention, and a little of that clear, light feeling you call yourself. Do it for years. Do it for the rest of your life. The price is not just the coins. It is the queue. The plan. The negotiation. The regret ten minutes later, then the promise to be good tomorrow. Is that belonging? Or is that rent paid to a landlord who never fixes the house?

The fact is, that price is optional. Not because you must live like a monk. Because you can eat as much of your favourite fresh foods as you want, whenever you want, and feel satisfied without that queue. We will make that practical soon. Here, just see the shape: normal was built to keep you paying. Freedom is built to let you keep living.

So when the voice says "everyone does it," answer it with sight, not shame. Everyone was invited into the same lab-built aisle and told the aisle was culture. It is not. Culture is people cooking, serving, sharing, talking — with any food or none. The aisle is industry.

You were not weak. You were outgunned. There is a difference. One blames you. One frees you.

Would you envy a person still paying the lab? Or would you pity the trap and rejoice you left the queue?

You know.

### DIGNIFIED APARTNESS — WHY NOT JOINING IN IS FREEDOM

There is a fear under "everyone does it" that deserves tenderness before it dies. It says: if I step apart, I will be alone. If I say no at the table, I will be judged. If I don't eat the cake, I will spoil the moment. That fear feels social. Actually it is borrowed from the Sugar Trap that learned to speak in your mother's voice, your friend's voice, your colleague's voice. It is not their voice. It is the trap using their faces.

We need to reframe apartness now, or this last FOR will linger.

Standing apart when everyone does something that harms them is not rudeness. It is dignity. It is the quiet heroism of the underdog. Picture the table. Everyone reaches because the packet is there. You reach for what you truly like and feel light after. Do you look deprived? Or do you look free? Which person would a child secretly want to be?

Freedom is sociable. Have you not seen it? The person who eats freely, laughs freely, says "Lovely, but not for me — I'll have the berries" and is not performing willpower at all? They are not fighting themselves. They are just not in the Trap. Do they spoil the party? No. They show another way to be at the party without the queue.

We were told apartness would feel cold. The other way around. The warmth you feel when you are not negotiating with a packet in your head is cleaner. You hear people better. You taste food better. You leave the table feeling good, not negotiating tomorrow. Is that lonelier? Or is that more together?

Consider the frog again. One frog jumps. The water feels suddenly cool. He looks back at his friends still sitting in warming water calling it normal. Does he envy them? No. He pities the heat and rejoices in the cool. He does not preach. He just swims.

You do not need to preach either. In fact, do not preach. Let ease do the talking. Eat happily. Enjoy your life. People notice freedom more than they listen to lectures.

Ask the two questions that close the trap quietly.

If sociability truly required BAD SUGAR, how did humans feast and love for thousands of years before these aisles existed? Did celebration begin with the vending machine?

If belonging truly required a dose, why do you feel closer to people when the dose is not on your mind?

You feel closer because you are closer. The packet was between you.

So let us measure belonging differently from now on. Not by "did I eat what they ate?" but by "did I enjoy them and feel good after?" That measure never fails. And it never needs BAD SUGAR to pass.

You are not betraying your people by stepping out of the Trap. You are returning to them.

### NORMALISED IS NOT NATURAL

We have reached the verdict this chapter exists to land. No hedging. No permission language. No "sit with this." Just sight, stated warmly and firmly, as one escaped reader to another.

We believed normal meant natural. It does not. Normal meant available, taught, and engineered until we stopped noticing. Frequent meant common, not healthy. Sociable meant belonging, not a dose that claimed the credit for belonging.

The four classic FORs are now empty. Taste? Not the hit — the occasion. Energy? It's the other way around — the hit makes the tiredness it relieves. Reward? Not love — the Lifeguard who pushed you in. Normal? Not benefit — brainwashing plus availability that proves the trap is big, not that the trap is good.

Does that leave BAD SUGAR a single genuine treat or lift you can name that survives isolation? No.

Does sociability disappear when BAD SUGAR disappears? No. It brightens. The table stays warm. The people stay dear. The laughter stays real. Only the queue leaves.

The fact is, your body never needed this normal to be well, sociable, or satisfied — and the very normal you trusted predicts cost, not comfort, when you follow it daily. That is why zero is coherent. Not as punishment. As return.

So hold these truths kindly and keep them.

You were not choosing because everyone chooses. You were following because everyone was led. Led by aisles, by ads, by labs that dialled your want, then told you the want was yours. That is not your fault. It was never your fault. But it is now your freedom to see.

Do you need everyone to stop for you to be free? Do you need the party to change before you can enjoy the party? Is your freedom waiting on the aisle's permission?

No. You are free the moment you see the trick. And you see it now.

We do not need to attack friends or family to be free of the Sugar Trap. We need to pity the trap, thank the people, and rejoice that we no longer pay a dose to belong.

Is that loss? Or is that reclamation? Is that deprivation? Or is that dignity?

You know. You have always known. Normal was not proof of safety. It was proof of scale.

Let that be the last time you let "everyone does it" speak for you.
