### "I DESERVE IT" — THE SENTENCE THAT KEEPS THE SUGAR TRAP PAID

"I deserve it."

"I've been good this week, so I can have this."

"Celebrating, rewarding myself, going to the cinema, etc with chocolate."

Do you hear your own voice in those lines? We do. We have all said them, warmly, sincerely, without a hint of doubt.

You finish a hard day. The kids are finally down. The inbox is closed. Your feet ache. You say to yourself, softly, kindly: I deserve a little something sweet. You earn it. You go to the sofa, you go to the kitchen, you go to the shop. You choose the bar, the biscuits, the ice cream. You feel, for a second, loved. Cared for. Rewarded.

Who is doing the loving in that moment? Is it you loving you? Or is it the Sugar Trap loving that you pay it?

We need to be tender here before we are clear. Your need for comfort is real. Your need for reward is real. Your need for love after effort is real. I do not question that for a second. You do deserve tenderness after a hard day. You do deserve celebration after a win. You do deserve comfort when you are frayed. The question is not whether you deserve love. The question is whether BAD SUGAR ever gave it.

Is BAD SUGAR a genuine treat or lift that loves you back? Or is it something else that learned to call itself love so you would keep feeding it?

We have already crossed two supposed benefits off your ledger. Taste? Not the hit — the occasion. Energy? It's the other way around — the hit creates the tiredness it relieves. Now we take the tenderest one. Reward. Comfort. Little love.

If this one falls, what's left of FOR? Only "everyone does it." We will meet that next. For now, stay with this one. It matters.

When did you first learn that sweet meant love? Was it you? Or were you taught?

### WHAT ACTUALLY COMFORTED YOU? TEN MINUTES AFTER THE TREAT

Recall your last "treat" moment precisely. Not my story. Yours. Do it now.

Where were you? After what? Was it stress or victory? A deadline met, a difficult conversation survived, a Friday finally arrived? You told yourself: I deserve it. You had the dose. A bar. A bowl. A bag.

Now split the film into two parts.

First five minutes: what actually comforted you? Was it the sugar? Or was it sitting down? Was it the quiet after noise? Was it the warmth of the blanket, the light of the film, the company laughing beside you, the fact the day was done and you had survived it? Was it permission to stop? Was it you finally being on your own side for a moment and saying, "Well done. Rest now"?

Be honest. If I had swapped the BAD SUGAR for a different pleasure you like — fresh berries, cheese, nuts, a hot bath, a funny show, a hand held — would the comfort have vanished? Or would it have remained, perhaps even cleaner, without the cloy?

Now run the next ten minutes. What did BAD SUGAR do after the first sweetness faded?

Did you feel soothed and settled? Or did you feel a little sticky, a little oversweet, a little restless? Did you feel proud and nourished? Or did you feel that small, familiar tug for more? Did you think, "That was perfect, I'm done"? Or did you think, "I can't stop once I start" and reach again?

We in the Sugar Trap know that second part too well. One escapee said it plainly: "celebrating, rewarding myself, going to the cinema, etc with chocolate." She wasn't lying about celebration. She was misassigning where the celebration lived. The cinema. The rest. The friends. The permission. The hit only sneaked along.

Ask the trap questions whose only honest answer concedes the point.

Did the comfort arrive before the first bite, when you sank into the sofa and exhaled? Did the need for more arrive after the bite, when the taste turned from pleasure to push?

Does love make you want more love immediately and then regret having had it? Does love leave you tired, foggy and drained and faintly ashamed ten minutes later?

You know. You have always known. The comfort was yours. The comedown was the hit's.

It's not your fault you paid the wrong account. We were taught to pay it.

### THE LIFEGUARD WHO PUSHED YOU IN

Let me give you one image to hold this whole chapter. You need it.

You are in the sea. You go under. Water in your mouth. Panic. A strong arm reaches you. A lifeguard hauls you up. You gasp. You cling. You thank him with all your heart. Of course you do. He saved you.

Then you learn, later, on the beach, dry and clear: he pushed you in.

He pushed you in, then saved you, then accepted your gratitude. Was he your rescuer? No. He was your perpetrator wearing a rescuer's shirt.

That is what BAD SUGAR does as your "reward."

It creates the very state it then pretends to rescue you from. It frays you, spikes you, drops you, empties you a fraction. Then it offers the next dose as comfort. You thank it. You say "I deserve this, it helps me cope." Of course you do. The relief feels real. The relief *is* real — as real as air after being held under. But who held you under?

We fell for it because the rescue is timed perfectly. You feel low after stress. You take the hit. You feel a little less low. You call that love. Is relief from drowning the same as being loved? Is less pain the same as more joy?

Think of a day you did not ride that rescue cycle. You rested. You ate fresh food you like until satisfied. You talked to someone kind. You went to bed early. Did you need to be rescued that evening? Did you need a reward to feel okay? Or were you okay because you were not pushed in?

The fact is, BAD SUGAR borrows the feeling of being rescued and pastes it over the fact it caused the distress. The Lifeguard did not love you. He needed you to need him. The Sugar Trap is the same. It needs you to believe comfort comes in a wrapper, so you will keep unwrapping.

Do you see how kind this makes you? You were not needy. You were tricked into seeking love where love was never stored.

And there is a second kindness here. Your desire for comfort does not make you weak. It makes you human. The trap exploited a beautiful human need. Blame the trick, not the need. Warm to yourself, harsh to the con.

So ask: when you say "I deserve it," who is "it"? Is "it" BAD SUGAR? Or is "it" rest, warmth, praise, ease, company, pride? Which one actually delivered?

You deserve the second list. Every time.

### SNEAKING A RIDE — WHERE THE JOY REALLY LIVED

We need to reassign the credit properly, beat by beat, or the illusion will linger.

Take the warmest "reward" scene you hold. Friday night sofa with chocolate. Or birthday cake with people singing. Or that cinema — dark, warm, film starting, chocolate in your hand. That is the strongest pro-sugar scene we will meet head-on later. For now, take your version.

Replay it in slow motion. Remove BAD SUGAR entirely. Keep everything else.

Keep the sofa. Keep the blanket. Keep the film light on your face. Keep the exhale after a long week. Keep the friend beside you laughing. Keep the singing, the candles, the love in the room, the pride that says, "I did well. We are here together."

Is that scene empty without the hit? Or is it still warm? Is it still reward? Does your chest still soften?

Now add BAD SUGAR back. Does it add love? Or does it add chew, cloy, stick, a brief sweet spike and then a need to sip water, a need for more, a faint dent in the very ease you just felt?

The joy was not in the hit. The hit was sneaking a ride on the joy. That is the phrase I want you to keep: sneaking a ride. The occasion, the people, the rest, you — you did the work of making the moment sweet. BAD SUGAR hitched a lift and claimed the credit after.

Do you doubt that? Test the variable in isolation, as we did with taste.

Would the sofa with no chocolate but with your favourite fresh foods you genuinely like — berries, nuts, cheese, fruit — still be reward? Would the birthday with no cake but with singing, hugging, laughter, still be love? Would the cinema with no sweets but with a great film and a friend's shoulder still be celebration?

Of course. You know it in your body before your brain argues.

And test the other way. Would BAD SUGAR alone, in an empty room, after a brutal day, with no rest, no kindness, no pause — just the hit standing on a table — feel like love? Would you feel comforted? Or would you feel you had eaten something sweet while still exhausted and still alone, now also faintly disappointed with yourself?

Love does not behave like that. Love settles. Love steadies. Love does not say "more" five minutes later.

So where did the love come from? It came from you and your life. Not from the packet.

Think of that next time the sentence arrives: "I deserve a treat." You do. So give yourself one. Give yourself the real one. Rest. Warmth. Praise. Company. A walk. An early night. A proper meal of fresh foods eaten freely until you are satisfied. Those are treats. They treat you well. BAD SUGAR treats you poorly and calls it love.

We have been paying the Lifeguard with our own gratitude.

### WAS LOVE YOUR FIRST REASON?

Here is a clean question that exposes the retrofitted excuse.

Did you fear being unloved when you had your first sweet? Was "I deserve it" your motive at age five, six, seven, when you first tasted the bright hit of BAD SUGAR at a party or from a parent's hand?

No. You didn't need a reward then. You were given it. You liked the sweet spike, you liked the occasion, you liked the adults smiling. You were not comforting heartbreak. You were not celebrating a promotion. The love story was added later to justify a habit already installed.

When did "I deserve it" enter your inner speech? After the loop was running. After you noticed the low and misread it as need. After the Sugar Trap taught you to interpret every dip, every stress, every victory as a cue to feed the Nibbler and call it self-care.

Is that how genuine love learns? Or how brainwashing installs its script?

We in the trap learned to say "I deserve it" exactly when we felt most deprived, most pushed under. Does that timing prove the hit is love? Or does it prove the script fires when the trap needs feeding?

Try the killer-line pair. Hold both truths until one dies.

It wasn't love that made you eat it. It was eating it that made you call it love.

Say that plainly to yourself. Not cruel. Clear. Does it land? Does it fit more afternoons than the love story ever did?

If BAD SUGAR were love, more would mean more loved. Do you feel more loved after the third biscuit? After the empty bag you hide? Does that feel like being loved well? Or does it feel like being used?

You are not hard to love. You were easy to con. There is a difference. One shames you. One frees you.

And notice this: calling it love keeps the loop alive. As long as you name it reward, you must reward yourself daily. The name creates the need. Keep the name and you keep the queue. Change the name and the queue dissolves. What you call love, you will not want to lose. What you see as payola to a trap, you will be happy to stop paying.

So rename it now. Not my command — your sight. Is that packet a little love? Or a little tax the Sugar Trap levies on every hard day and every good day alike?

You know.

### THE TREAT THAT PREDICTS THE LOW

The fact is, there is a measured pattern you should see flatly before you put it behind you.

In a long study that followed thousands of adults for years, the people who ate more sweets had higher odds of developing common mental strain in the years that followed — and that link was clear in men even after the researchers checked whether feeling low first led to eating more sweets. It did not. The sweets predicted the low. The low did not predict the sweets.

Hear the limit honestly, as your body would want. That study was observational. It followed men. It showed prediction, not proof that one sweet causes disorder on its own. It does not mean you are ill. It does not diagnose you. It does mean something simple and useful: the daily "treat" is a poor predictor of relief tomorrow. It predicts its opposite.

Does that surprise you after your own ten-minutes-later audit? Or does it fit exactly what you have lived: short lift, fast drop, queue again, and over weeks, a lower baseline you call normal?

I do not want you to quit out of fear of feeling low later. I want you to see the trick, then put fear aside. The fact did its work by correcting the story. The treat that calls itself comfort predicts discomfort. The love that calls itself rescue predicts the need for rescue. So the mover is not fear. The mover is relief. You are not escaping a future illness in panic. You are escaping a present con that never loved you.

You were taught that BAD SUGAR soothes mood. What if it mortgages it? What if the "I deserve it" today borrows from "I feel low" next week?

You don't need that loan. Your body and your days repay better in steady, fresh, kind ways.

Do you feel the shift? From "I deserve a hit because I feel bad" to "I feel bad partly because I keep taking the hit"? One keeps you in the Sugar Trap. One lifts you out.

Let that settle. Not as scare. As clarity. Then let the clarity turn to lightness. You don't need to pay for comfort with future comfort. You can have comfort without debt.

### FRIEND OR ENEMY? CHOOSING HOW YOU FEEL ABOUT LOSING IT

There is one more piece to lay before we land the verdict. It is not about sugar. It is about you and how you will feel when you no longer reach for it.

Picture two funerals for the same loss. In both, the same thing is gone: no more BAD SUGAR hits. In one, you mourn a friend. You stand by the grave of your little love, your reward, your comfort after hard days. You miss it. You feel deprived. You count days. Will that feeling keep you free? No. Mourning a friend makes you want the friend back.

In the other, you celebrate an enemy's death. You stand by the grave of the Lifeguard who pushed you in, the tax collector who called himself love, the gatecrasher who kept sneaking a ride on your joy. You feel relief. You feel clean. You feel proud. Does that feeling keep you free? Yes. Rejoicing at a dead enemy cements freedom.

Same loss. Two opposite emotions. Only one is relapse-proof.

Which will you choose? You cannot choose by willpower. You choose by sight. If you still believe BAD SUGAR was a genuine treat or lift that loved you, you will mourn it. If you see it was payola you paid to the con, you will celebrate its end.

So I ask you, with warm pressure, to choose sight now.

Do you want to miss a trick? Or do you want to be happy the trick is dead?

Think of your next hard day, coming soon. You will do well. You will be tired. The old sentence will try to speak: "I deserve it." You will hear it. Will you answer with deprivation — "I can't have it"? Or with freedom — "I don't need to pay the trap to feel loved — I'll give myself real rest instead"?

Think of your next celebration. Candles, laughter, film, sofa. The old sentence will try to speak: "Celebrating with chocolate." Will you feel something missing? Or will you feel everything present because you finally let the moment be the reward?

You are not losing a friend. You are burying an impostor. Let yourself feel the right feeling.

I know this tender idea is hard to release. You cherished it. You were sincere. I honour that. You deserved love. You reached for what you were told was love. Now you see where love actually lives — in you, in your people, in rest, in pride, in a meal that truly nourishes. That is not less love. It is more.

Close your eyes for a second and picture tomorrow's evening. Same sofa. Same film. Same you, breathing out. No wrapper. No chase. Warmth without cloy. Rest without debt. Joy without queue. Is that emptier? Or fuller? Which feels more like being loved well?

You know.

So let's land this chapter's one job with no hedging and no permission language. No "sit with this." No "if you wish." Just sight.

### YOU WERE NOT LOVING YOURSELF — YOU WERE FEEDING THE TRAP

We have stripped another FOR bare.

Bad days do not need BAD SUGAR to be soothed. Good days do not need BAD SUGAR to be celebrated. Comfort does not live in a packet. Love does not live in a packet. Rest lives in rest. Reward lives in meaning. Celebration lives in people and pride.

The credit for your warmth was always yours. The debt for the ten-minutes-later restlessness was always the hit's. The Sugar Trap taught you to swap the labels.

We have all stood at the cinema with chocolate and called it reward. We have all hidden the empty bag and called it comfort. We have all paid the Lifeguard and thanked him.

Not anymore. Not because you are strong. Because you see.

The fact is, the treat that calls itself love predicts the low — not because you are broken, but because the trap runs on that gap. And the gap closes the moment you reassign the credit where it belongs: sneaking a ride is not love. Love stays when the ride is gone.

Do you need a packet to love yourself after a hard day? Do you need a packet to celebrate being alive with people you love? Has a single hit ever loved you back?

Then why call it love?

Hold that clarity kindly and firmly. Let self-compassion replace self-payment. Tonight, when the sentence "I deserve it" arrives — and it will — answer it with the true treat. Rest. Water. Food you like that loves you back. A kind word to yourself said out loud: well done. You survived. You did well.

That is love. That is reward. That is comfort. It was always yours. It needed no wrapper.

Say it not to shame yourself but to free yourself. You were not loving yourself — you were feeding the trap. See it, and the need to feed it dies with the name.
