### "I NEED IT TO GET THROUGH"

"I need something quick and sweet."

"I need it to get me through the afternoon."

"To get me through."

Have you said that this week? At 3pm, with the email still open, eyes heavy, brain fuzzy? Have you felt that little command arrive like a fact: "I need something RIGHT NOW or I won't function"?

We all have. I hear you. That feeling is not imaginary. Your tiredness is real. Your fog is real. Your need to concentrate is real. The question is not whether you feel low. The question is who made you low.

Does BAD SUGAR give you energy? Is it a genuine treat or lift that helps you focus, powers you through, steadies you? Or is that the same mislabelling we just exposed with taste?

We fell for it because the timing seems to prove it. You feel flat. You take a hit. You feel a bit brighter for a little while. Must be fuel, right? Must be energy? But does timing prove cause? Or can timing hide the trick?

Let's map your own afternoon, not my theory. No willpower needed. Just honest attention.

### MAPPING YOUR AFTERNOON: DOSE, LIFT, FOG, NEXT DOSE

Think of yesterday. Be precise. When did the flatness first arrive?

For we in the Sugar Trap, the day has a shape. Morning: okay, perhaps coffee. Mid-morning: a biscuit, a pastry, a juice. Fine for a while. Then lunch — often a white roll, fries, a sweet drink. Then the clock hits two-thirty. Three. The head gets heavy. Concentration thins. You feel, in your own words, tired, foggy and drained. Have you described it that way? That triple is the addict's permanent state, not a passing mood.

What do you do then? You reach for the fix. A chocolate bar. A sweet coffee. A handful of sweets from the desk tin. "Just something quick and sweet." You tell yourself it will help you focus. And for ten, twenty minutes, the fog lifts a fraction. You feel a little sharper. A little revived. Is that proof it gave you energy?

Watch what happens next. An hour later, where are you? Are you bright and clear? Or are you heavier again? Do you feel more settled or more restless? Do you need another hit to get to dinner?

That lived loop is everywhere. One escapee put it plainly, and I keep her sentence because it is your sentence: "I would have something quick and sweet but now know that will only give a short term lift which will inevitably be followed by a fast drop." Do you recognise that? Short lift. Fast drop. Queue again.

If that is energy, why does it need constant topping up? If that is fuel, why does the tank empty faster the more you pour in?

Ask the trap question that closes this before we explain the mechanism. If BAD SUGAR gives you energy, why do you feel most tired *after* you have had it most often? Why do heavy sugar days end earliest on the sofa? Why do holidays built around grazing leave you more exhausted, not less?

And here is the inversion you already felt forming when we previewed tight shoes. The relief you credit as energy is often just the low leaving for a moment. Is less tiredness the same as more energy? Is quiet the same as power?

We need to see the machinery once, clearly, then you will never unsee it.

### THE ROLLERCOASTER YOU QUEUE FOR — AND PAY TO RIDE AGAIN

Come with me to the fair. You know this ride. You have ridden it daily for years. We call it the Rollercoaster because that is what you call it — "the roller coaster" — in your own dialect.

You queue. You are a little flat, a little restless, faintly insecure, waiting for the climb. The attendant hands you a sugary hit — a biscuit, a sweet drink, a pastry. Up you go. Brief climb. Brief view. For a few minutes the world looks brighter. You think, "Ah, that's better. I needed that."

Then the drop. The stomach lurch. The fog returns, a fraction heavier than before. The queue forms again. Your brain says, "Again. I need another." So you queue again. And again.

Is that ride giving you height? Or is it manufacturing the dip it then relieves?

The fact is, every dose guarantees the next dip. The lift *is* the drop waiting to happen. The same surge that lifts you drives the fall. It's the other way around.

We were conned into thanking the ride for the relief it created the need for. Does that make you weak? No. It makes the trap clever. We all queued. I queued. For years I called that queue "to get me through."

Look at the queue itself. Do non-riders queue? Does a child who spent the afternoon on apples, cheese, nuts and water ride that same violent up and down? No. She runs, plays, concentrates, gets tired honestly, recovers honestly. Is she deprived of energy? Are you more energetic than she is after her apple and your bar?

Who is more stable? Who Reaches 5pm lighter?

You know the answer without a laboratory. You have lived both days. Have you noticed how a day on fresh foods feels steadier, calmer, less spiky — not buzzy, but clear? Have you noticed how a day on grazing feels wired then wiped? Which day needed a rescue?

The Rollercoaster does not create energy. It creates the *need* for energy. It spins you, then sells you the brief pause in spinning as if it were flight.

Do you want to get off? You can. Getting off is not loss of thrill. It is end of nausea.

### IT'S THE OTHER WAY AROUND — THE SURGE THAT LIFTS YOU IS THE SURGE THAT DROPS YOU

Let me state the body's workings as settled fact, plainly, without review and without hedging.

When you flood your blood with BAD SUGAR and stripped carbs, your body does a brilliant, prompt thing. It releases insulin — a surge — to clear that flood and keep you safe. The fact is, that lift you feel IS insulin working. The surge is the lift. You feel a brief brightening as sugar rushes and hormones mobilise. Is that energy imported from the packet? No. That is your own system working hard to manage a crude flood.

And here is the turn that frees you. The same surge that handles the flood drives the after-dip. Insulin does not stop precisely at perfect. It overshoots a little. Sugar falls, sometimes below where you started. You feel that fall as fog, irritability, hunger again, a pull for another quick hit. The after-dip is real. You feel it because it is real.

But keep the limit honestly where your body keeps it. In healthy people without diabetes, that dip rarely measures as a clinical low. Doctors reserve "low blood sugar" for levels at or below about 55 mg/dL, and everyday 3pm slumps almost never reach that. Do you need a clinical low to feel crummy? No. You feel crummy well above it. Your brain reads the *drop* and the hormonal swing as tiredness and craving. Is that medical diagnosis? No. It is your body's warning that you rode the Rollercoaster again. If you have diabetes, low sugar, or clinician-prescribed pattern, follow your clinician — this book does not override that guidance. For the rest of us, the slump is not disease. It is consequence.

So see the reversal whole. BAD SUGAR did not cure your tiredness. It created it, then briefly muted it. The rescuer was the perpetrator.

It's the other way around.

Not: you are tired so you need sugar.
But: you need sugar so you are tired.

Not: sugar gives you focus so you perform.
But: sugar fragments your focus, then sells back a few minutes of slightly less fragmentation.

Do you see it? Take the afternoon you mapped. Dose → brief lift → heavier fog → next dose → evening regret. Who added the fog? Was it the work? Was it the morning itself? Or was it the second hit you used to fix the first?

If work itself made you that tired, why do you not crash the same way on days you eat fresh? Why does an apple not produce that same frantic "RIGHT NOW"? Is that coincidence? Or is that isolation proving the variable?

We credit the packet for the lift and blame the day for the drop. Reverse it. Credit the packet for the drop. The day was steady until the flood.

Consider the deferred load, too. That lift is not free. The fact is, high added sugar loads are linked at population level to heavier burdens on liver, blood pressure, inflammation and appetite control — the body pays interest on every quick loan. Does a single biscuit create heart disease? No. Does living on quick loans create a heavier system to carry? Yes. The lift is present joy borrowed against future steadiness. Is that energy? Or debt?

Once seen, this reversal cannot be unseen. You will feel it next time the 3pm whisper arrives. You will think: "This faint restless tiredness is not proof I need a hit. It is proof the last hit is leaving." Will that thought scare you? Or will it make you smile with relief that you understand?

You don't need to fight it. Understanding is enough. But do you feel the relief forming? The burden lifting even before behaviour changes? That relief is the truth arriving.

### THE BODY THAT ALREADY KNOWS HOW TO MAKE STEADY ENERGY

Let me show you something marvellous, quietly. A fragment of the awe catalogue we will open fully later.

Your body is sophisticated about sweetness. Taste buds read sweet as signal, not command. Gut reads fibre, speed, volume. Liver and muscle store steady glucose and release it smoothly. And — this surprises many — your body manufactures glucose itself from protein and glycerol when needed, without a single spoon of BAD SUGAR. The fact is, dietary BAD SUGAR is not essential fuel. Your system makes what it needs and meters it beautifully when you let it.

That is reverence-worthy. Is that machine crude or elegant? Is it panicked and spiky or calm and metering? Which picture feels more like health?

Now contrast the crude flood. You pour in stripped sugar, fibre gone, structure gone. It hits fast. It demands a fast answer — that insulin surge. Up. Down. Queue again. Is that elegant metering? Or vandalism of elegant metering? Do you feel proud overriding that quiet sophistication with a packet, or faintly embarrassed you ever thought the packet was smarter than your body?

Nature's method is steady. Trap's method is spike, crash, crave. Which would you trust to get you through an afternoon if you had never been taught the trap's story?

Have you ever watched a child eat before the trap taught her its script? She eats an apple. She runs. She eats cheese and bread that still has grain. She drinks water. She concentrates. She does not say "I need something sweet to focus." Is she low on energy? Does she lack will? No. She has not yet learned to mistranslate a natural dip in attention as a need for a hit.

Did you need BAD SUGAR for energy as a child? When you were eight, did you need a sweet drink to finish homework? Did you need a biscuit to play? Be honest. What did you need? Food when hungry, water when thirsty, rest when tired, movement when restless. Was that not enough? When did "energy" acquire a wrapper?

The trap installed a new definition: energy equals hit. But energy is not a hit. Energy is the steady capacity your body already provides when you feed it fresh, varied food freely and let its gauge do its job. Is that deprivation? No. It is return.

I want you to feel the steadiness you already own. Think of a day you ate simply — not perfectly, just simply. Fresh foods you like. Enough water. Sleep. Did the 3pm wall arrive as a cliff? Or as a gentle slope you walked over? Did you need to be rescued? Or did you just keep going?

Your body is not deficient. It is interrupted. Remove the interruption and steadiness returns by itself. Not superpowers. Not instant buzz. Just clear, calm, reliable afternoon light.

Do you want buzz? Or do you want clear?

### DID YOU NEED THIS TO RUN AS A CHILD? THE BASELINE TEST

We promised to compare with children's and fresh-food days as baseline. Let's do it now, as your own experiment — no willpower, just observation.

Pick your baseline day in memory. Not an ideal. A real one. A day you were out, perhaps coastal or in a park, and you ate fresh — fruit, salad, nuts, eggs, fish, vegetables with salt, water. Perhaps you walked a lot. Did you feel wired then drained? Did you queue for the Rollercoaster? Or did you feel, at day's end, honestly tired — the good tired that sleeps well — rather than tired, foggy and drained and still restless?

Now place beside it a grazing day. Biscuits with coffee. Juice. White sandwich. Cake at three. Sweets to "get me through." How did the evening feel? Was focus better? Was mood brighter? Or were you snappier, sleepier, still hungry, still thinking about the next sweet while saying "I can't stop once I start"?

Which day gave you more usable energy for life? Which day borrowed from tomorrow?

You already know. You have always known. You just mislabelled the debt as fuel.

Another test. Next time the "I need something RIGHT NOW" thought fires, run the Raw Test in your mind. Ask: would I eat a plain apple, a carrot, a handful of nuts, or plain steamed veg right now? If yes, that is true hunger. Eat fresh food freely until satisfied and notice steadiness return without a spike. If no — if only BAD SUGAR appeals — that is not hunger. That is the whisper plus the story that sells it as "energy." Will you argue with the whisper? No. You will smile at it. It is just a whisper. It passes.

Does that mean you failed if you have the hit anyway? No. It means the experiment gave you data. What did your mouth feel after? Clean or coated? What did your head feel an hour later? Clearer or foggier? Did the hit deliver energy you could measure in steady work? Or did it deliver a few bright minutes and then heavier fog?

We are not shaming a slip. We are auditing a claim. Does the claim survive audit? Ask your own afternoons. They have already voted.

One more plain question to settle it. If BAD SUGAR truly gave you energy beyond baseline, non-users would be sluggish and users would be bright. Is that what you see in life? Are the heaviest grazers the most clear-eyed and steady? Are fresh-food eaters the ones nodding at 3pm? Look around the office, the family table. Who is steady? Who is spiky? Who needs the rescue at three daily? Does that look like energy given, or energy taken?

There is no case left for BAD SUGAR as fuel. There are only two stories. One says a packet powers you. The other says your body powers you and the packet interrupts. Which story matches every afternoon you have ever mapped?

You know.

So let's land this chapter's one job with the same clean cut we used on taste. No both-sides. No permission language. Just sight.

Either BAD SUGAR lifts you above the steady baseline of a person who never needed it — in which case heavy users would outshine fresh eaters, children would need it to run, and more would mean more steady power. Or it is a loan at high interest that creates the very tiredness, fog and restlessness it then briefly relieves, and never once beats the non-user baseline.

Which did your afternoons show you? Which will tomorrow show you when you watch without the old story?
