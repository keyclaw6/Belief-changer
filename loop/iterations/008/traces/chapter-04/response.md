### "I LOVE THE TASTE"

"I love the taste."

Have you said that? This week? Today?

"I just love sweet things. I can't help it. It's delicious."

Of course you have. We all have. That sentence feels true in your mouth because your mouth remembers something sweet. Can you feel it now, just thinking about it? A little watering? A little lift?

I hear you. I am not going to tell you that sweet taste does not exist. It does. Sweet is one of the five tastes your tongue was built to read. Your taste buds know sweet the way your eyes know light. I love sweet taste too. A ripe mango? Wonderful. A fresh fig? Marvellous. Does that surprise you?

So why are we questioning taste at all? Because the trap needs that sentence intact. The trap needs you to believe BAD SUGAR equals delicious, and delicious equals happiness. Have you noticed how quickly that equation becomes identity? "I'm just a sweet-toothed person."

But is it true? Do you love BAD SUGAR? Or do you love what was around it?

Let's be precise. When this book says SUGAR, we mean BAD SUGAR — the stripped, refined, added stuff and the junk carbs that come with it. White bread, biscuits, cakes, sweets, ice cream, sugary drinks, pastries, ultra-processed snacks. Not the sweetness inside a whole apple you wash and bite. Not the clean sweetness of berries or carrots. Do you taste the difference? Your body does.

Now ask yourself the trap question that closes the whole taste argument before we even start. If BAD SUGAR is such a genuine taste pleasure, why does it so often need to hide another taste to be enjoyed?

Would you eat a spoonful of raw white sugar with pleasure? For its own sake? Would you chew a mouthful of plain white bread, dry and cottony, and call it delicious? Would you sip that bitter, burned coffee and then say the sugar you tipped in was the star? Be honest. What did you actually taste?

We fell into the Sugar Trap because we credited the wrong guest. We gave the prize to the wrong name on the label. Is that your fault? No. We were all taught to. But are you willing to see it now?

Let's look.

### THE LONELY GATECRASHER THAT CANNOT BE ENJOYED ALONE

Picture a house party.

Music is on. People are laughing. The table is full. And in slips a gatecrasher. He is not funny. He is not interesting. He stands awkwardly by the crisps. Alone, no one would have invited him. No one would stay to talk to him. But he knows a trick. He attaches himself to the funniest person in the room. He laughs loudly at their jokes. He borrows their warmth. By midnight, you think he *was* the party.

That gatecrasher is the lonely carb.

Think of it. White bread. A plain cracker. A dry biscuit. A dull, puffed cereal. Taste one alone. Really taste it. Is it delicious? Is it a genuine treat or lift? Or is it flat? Papery? In need of help?

We know the answer. We always have. That is why we never eat it alone. Have you ever made toast and thought, "This needs something"? Have you ever opened a packet of plain crackers and reached instantly for cheese, or jam, or butter? Have you ever said "I don't keep it in the house" precisely because you know you would need to dress it up to enjoy it?

The fact is, the lonely carb needs company to be palatable. It invites company over. It needs the jam. It needs the chocolate spread. It needs the butter and salt. Is that the behaviour of a food that tastes wonderful on its own? Or is that a guest who cannot hold the room without stealing someone else's charm?

Try the audit with your own mouth tonight. No willpower needed. Just attention.

Take that white roll or cracker you usually love. Eat it plain. No topping. No spread. Chew. Notice. Does it thrill you? Or does your hand hover, already looking for the fix to add?

Now add the jam. Ah. Now it sings. Who sang? Was it the bread? Or was it the jam, the butter, the texture, the occasion, the relief from the faint, restless whisper that had been nudging you since you opened the kitchen door?

You see the trick. We said "I love the taste of bread" when we loved the taste of butter and jam *despite* the bread. We said "I love the taste of biscuits" when we loved the crunch, the melt, the ritual, the break, the reward moment — all of which would have been delightful with any fresh favourite food if we had believed it. Have you ever finished a fresh peach and felt that clean, bright pop? Is that not taste? Is that not *more* taste, not less?

Do you see how generous your taste buds already are? They don't need BAD SUGAR to be alive.

And what about sugary drinks? We tell ourselves we love the taste. Do we? Or do we love the cold, the fizz, the sweet masking of water that would otherwise taste like tap and habit? Would you drink warm, flat, sugary water for pleasure? You wouldn't. You never have. The cold and the bubble did the work. The sugar just signed its name on top.

We are not denying pleasure. We are moving the credit. The pleasure is real. The cause was mislabelled. Your body loves taste. BAD SUGAR was only sneaking a ride.

Does that feel true yet? Shall we isolate it completely?

### ISOLATE THE VARIABLE: TASTE THE SUGAR ITSELF

I want you to isolate the variable. One moment. Honest tasting. No story, no wrapping, no occasion. Just the substance itself on your tongue.

Remember BAD SUGAR as you actually meet it at the end of a packet. Not the imagined idea of it, but the fact of it.

How does pure sweet taste when it is too much? Think of the last bite of the over-sweet cake. Was it light? Or cloying? Was it clean? Or sticky? Did your mouth feel fresh after? Or coated? Did you want water? Did you want to scrape your tongue?

Have you felt that? The oversweetness that turns? The mouth-coating that needs a glass of water to clear? The slightly sickly edge? The heaviness after the third biscuit that was not there after the first?

We remember the first bite as wonderful. We forget the tenth. Why do we forget the tenth? Because by the tenth, the truth is speaking loudly. And we had already left the table.

Ask yourself a simple test we use for any supposed benefit. If this truly gives me taste pleasure, why does more of it not give me more pleasure? Why does the second biscuit taste less bright than the first? Why does the family-size bar feel necessary but never satisfying? Why does the last sip of the large sweet drink taste thinner, almost unpleasant, and still you finish it?

Is that pleasure? Or is it chasing?

Do you love the taste itself? Or do you love the idea of taste you learned to expect before you tasted?

Let's strip away even the texture. Take white sugar itself. A spoonful. Put it on your tongue. Sweet, yes. Delicious? Is it delicious like a strawberry is delicious? Or is it simply sweet, sharp, then gone, leaving stickiness and thirst? Is that a taste you would choose for its own sake for the rest of your life? Would you sit down to a bowl of sugar as a meal?

No. You never have. You never would.

And so the claim collapses without any need for willpower. We are not *giving up* deliciousness. There is no deliciousness in the isolated substance to give up. The deliciousness belonged to crunch, to cream, to cold, to company, to the break in your day. Those things remain yours. They were always yours. Your wonderful fresh foods give you all of that, brighter.

Have you ever eaten raspberries straight from the punnet, slightly cool? Have you ever bitten a crisp apple and felt that snap? Have you ever had toasted nuts with salt and felt fully satisfied? Is that tame? Or is it vivid?

You know the answer in your mouth already. Your body prefers clean taste. Oversweet dulls it. Have you noticed how fresh food tastes flatter the day after a binge? And then brighter two days later when the cloy clears? Who dulled whom?

The trap taught you that BAD SUGAR *is* taste. The audit shows it borrows taste. Do you see the inversion forming? Keep it light. We will land it fully later. For now just hold this: isolation kills the illusion. Taste tested alone tells the truth.

And still, you may say: "But my mouth waters when I think about it. That must be taste love." Does it? Let's look at that watering precisely.

### WHY YOUR MOUTH WATERS BEFORE THE FIRST BITE

Do you know that little flood? You are not hungry. You are passing a shop, or you see a wrapper, or someone mentions biscuits, or the clock hits three. Suddenly your mouth waters. Your attention snags. "I need something RIGHT NOW."

Is that taste? You have not tasted anything yet.

The fact is, that watering is not your tongue reporting joy. It is your brain reacting to a cue. The wrapper. The smell. The time of day. The sight of the office tin. Those signals have learned to fire the wanting before the bite ever lands.

Have you lived this? You feel fine. You were not thinking about food. Then you see the packet. Now you want it. Was that hunger? Was that taste? Or was that the cue doing its job?

We call that cue hyper-reactivity. It means the brain lights up for the sign of the hit long before flavour arrives. The mouth waters for the *idea* of the hit. Does that prove the hit is delicious? Or does it prove the wanting system has been trained to anticipate relief?

Think of Pavlov if you like, but simpler. A dog can be trained to drool at a bell. Does the bell taste good? No. The drool proves learning, not deliciousness.

The same is true for you. The shine of the wrapper makes you drool. The jingle makes you imagine sweetness. That is learned wanting, not proof of pleasure. Do you see the difference? Wanting is not liking. Your body can want what it does not even enjoy once it gets it. Have you finished a packet and thought, "That wasn't even that nice"? Have you said "I can't stop once I start" even as the taste got worse?

That sentence is your own evidence. "I can't stop once I start" does not describe great taste. It describes a trap that grabs after the first bite. Is that love? Or is that being hooked?

The fact is, imaging studies that look at how the brain responds to food cues find the same split. The regions that drive *wanting* light up fiercely at the sight and smell, while the actual liking at the moment of taste is smaller and brief. The cue shouts. The taste whispers. And then the cue shouts again.

Does that mean your brain is broken? Not at all. It is not damage. It is misfiring. A learned reflex. The brain was doing its job — predicting what used to relieve a low — and it got good at it. Is a well-trained reflex a life sentence? No. It is a habit that fades when you stop feeding it and stop believing its advertisement.

So when your mouth waters tomorrow, will you call it proof you love the taste? Or will you smile and call it proof you were trained? Which label frees you?

You are not tasting in that moment. You are remembering a promise that never delivered. Have you checked that promise recently with honest tasting? Does the bite match the advertisement in your head? Rarely. Thin, isn't it?

The watering proves the trap is clever, not that BAD SUGAR is delicious. You saw that cleverness installed early. We all did. Do you remember your first sweet being about taste? Or was it about grown-ups, celebration, love, belonging? Was that taste, or was that meaning we poured over it?

We pour meaning on sugar, then call the meaning its taste. Let's pour it back where it belongs.

### A PREVIEW OF TIGHT SHOES — RELIEF DISGUISED AS PLEASURE

Let me give you a small, vivid picture we will use fully very soon. It is a preview, not the whole argument. Hold it lightly.

Imagine wearing tight shoes all day. Not painful enough to cry. Just tight. Dull. Distracting. You walk, you work, you forget them consciously, but your feet ache in the background. Then you slip them off. Ah. Relief floods. Wonderful! Do you love tight shoes? Would you say tight shoes give you pleasure?

No. You love *taking them off*. The pleasure is removal of a discomfort the shoes themselves created.

Have you felt that pattern with BAD SUGAR? A faint, restless edginess builds. You feel not quite right. You think "I need something sweet." You have the hit. For a few minutes, the edginess lifts. Is that taste pleasure? Or is it the low leaving? Is that a genuine lift? Or is it the temporary end of a low BAD SUGAR made inevitable hours earlier?

We will demolish energy properly in the next chapter. This is only a taste preview of that inversion: the brief sweetness that seems to help is often just the sticky mouth and the faint unease going quiet for a moment. Is quiet the same as joy? Is less discomfort the same as more pleasure?

Ask your mouth again. After a sweet overload, does your mouth feel more alive? Or does it feel coated, needing clean water? Does your body feel fresher? Or tired, foggy and drained?

The tighter the shoe, the sweeter the removal. But who put the shoe on? Have you ever asked that before?

You don't need to answer fully now. You only need to feel the question forming. Does the relief you call "tasty" actually prove deliciousness? Or does it prove you were a little uncomfortable before, and the hit briefly stopped the discomfort it had been ensuring would return?

Keep that picture. We will walk you completely out of those shoes later. For now, just notice how often your "I love the taste" moment arrives not when you are free and genuinely hungry, but when you are slightly flat, slightly insecure, slightly restless and looking for something to lift you. Is that your tongue calling? Or something else?

You see. Once you isolate taste from context, from cue, from relief, there is surprisingly little left that BAD SUGAR itself reliably adds.

What remains is yours anyway. Crunch. Cold. Cream. Company. Laughter. Rest. The film. The sofa. The break. Would any of those lose their joy if BAD SUGAR never arrived? Try imagining tonight's sofa without the chocolate bar. The sofa is still soft. The film is still funny. Your body is still yours. What exactly would be missing that you could notice with your tongue alone?

Nothing you could name cleanly.

So let's land this plainly, as a settled fact, and close this chapter's one job.

We said we would test the first excuse in your TO/FOR ledger. You brought that ledger with you. Remember? The right column titled FOR. You wrote "taste." We tested it. Isolate BAD SUGAR and it needs help. Watch the cue and it fires before taste. Review the pure sweetness and it cloys. Is that a genuine benefit that survives isolation? Could you build a life's happiness on that stickiness and thirst?

You already know you couldn't. Not for a single day, let alone for the rest of your life.

There is pleasure in eating. Profound, daily, wonderful pleasure. Your taste buds are not going quiet. Your favourite fresh foods — fruit bright from the fridge, veg with salt, nuts warm from the pan, plain foods that smell of themselves — give you true taste, clear and finished, without the coated aftermath. Is that deprivation? Or is it return?

You are not losing taste. You are reclaiming it.

So here is theEither-or that ends the taste debate. No both-sides. No permission language. Just sight.

Either BAD SUGAR is a deliciousness that stands alone and rewards you beyond any other food — in which case you would happily eat its most basic forms alone, plain, for joy. Or it is a gatecrasher who borrows delight from texture, temperature, occasion and relief, and then claims the credit while leaving you sticky, thirsty and wanting more.

Which did you see tonight? Which will you see tomorrow when the wrapper winks?

**IT WAS NEVER THE TASTE.**

Say it with me, not as loss but as relief. It was never the taste. The taste was the crunch, the cold, the company, the moment — all of which stay yours. What you called **a genuine treat or lift** was not genuine. It was a loan in fancy dress. BAD SUGAR was only **sneaking a ride** on pleasures that were already yours.

Does that make you sad that you leave it? Or excited that you see it?

Hold that lightness. We have crossed one FOR off your ledger. Together. Cleanly. No willpower needed. No sacrifice made.

Another FOR waits tomorrow. We'll take energy next, and you will see it's the other way around. For now, let taste stay where it belongs — in your wonderful, sophisticated body, not in the packet that dulls it.

You have proved a method today. Isolate the variable, and the illusion dies. Will you bring that method with you?

You will. Because now you know it works.
