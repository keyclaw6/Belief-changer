### WHY YOU STILL WANT IT AFTER EVERY BENEFIT HAS COLLAPSED

We have taken your ledger apart.

The lift was the loan. The love was the moment. The flavour was the company BAD SUGAR had to bring to be tolerated. The calm and the connection were yours and the people around you — BAD SUGAR was sneaking a ride.

So your FOR column is empty. Do you agree?

You do. And yet a puzzling voice remains. It says, "If there's nothing in it for me, why do I still want it? Why is there a churn at three o'clock? Why does my hand still move toward the tin?"

Is that voice lying? Are you weak?

No. You are not weak. You are caught in a trap with a mechanism. And once you see the mechanism, the wanting changes shape forever.

Can we look at that mechanism together, calmly, as investigators rather than as judges? I have freed myself from it and I have watched thousands walk free easily, immediately and permanently. Shall we name them so you can see them?

### MEET THE TWO MONSTERS: THE WHINE AND THE STORY

Think of a diagram. At the bottom, a tiny creature. At the top, a loud storyteller.

The tiny creature is the Sugar Gremlin. It is not a metaphor for you. It is the almost-imperceptible physical restlessness that appears as the last dose of BAD SUGAR wears off. Scientists who study rats given intermittent access to sugar — twelve hours with the sweet, twelve hours without it — see this clearly. On that schedule, the animals binge, they show craving, they show a little withdrawal when the sweet is blocked, and they become more sensitive to other rewards. On steady, all-day access, they do not. The schedule is the trap's form. The binge is not proof that sugar equals drugs of abuse in you. It is proof that intermittency itself escalates intake. It echoes your own diet-binge yo-yo. Restrict, binge, promise, repeat. Does that rhythm feel familiar?

When those binging rats get their fix, there is a flicker of dopamine in the nucleus accumbens. That is part of the brain's reward circuitry lighting up. It is real. It is measured by microdialysis in animals. It is not proof that dopamine equals pleasure. And the size of that flicker is smaller than with drugs of abuse. Keep scale in mind.

When researchers then block the brain's own opioid chemistry to precipitate withdrawal in those same binging rats, they measure a dip — low dopamine there with a rise in an opposing chemical that feels like unease. A real measured low after the sweet. Opioid-like. Brought on in the lab by a drug. It is not proof that you will face a terrifying human withdrawal. Human sugar withdrawal is not an established diagnosis. You will not go through a frightening detox. One careful lab has described the whole dependency seen in those animals as mild but well-defined. Do you hear how small that is? Mild. But well-defined. Real. Yet tiny. And self-resolving.

What does that mean for you?

It means the Sugar Gremlin is tiny, starving, and already dying between doses. It does not roar. It whines. Almost below hearing.

Now the storyteller.

The storyteller is the Sweet Brainwashing. It is every message that taught you what the whine means: "Sugar is pleasure. Sugar is treat. Sugar is energy. Sugar helps you cope. Sugar is love. Sugar is what makes the party the party." Family, advertising, the supermarket aisle, the bliss-point labs that engineered the crunch and melt to hit your brain at exactly the right second. We were all conned. Not foolish. Conned. We chose our first sweets on phoney information as children, and the trap removed our choice from there.

The Sweet Brainwashing hears the faint whine of the Sugar Gremlin and translates it. It says, "You need BAD SUGAR. You are low. Get a fix. You will feel better."

And you do. For minutes. So the translation feels true.

Do you see the trick? The Gremlin creates a faint low. The Brainwashing sells you the cure that guarantees the next low. Rescuer and perpetrator are the same actor.

The Gremlin is physical and trivial. The Brainwashing is mental and domineering. Starve the Gremlin and it dies in days. Unlearn the Brainwashing and the whine loses its meaning forever. But feed either one and both live.

Which one has frightened you until now?

Mostly the second. Most of the fear you call craving is not the body at all. It is the story about the body.

I want you to feel the difference. It matters.

### A FAINT, EMPTY, SLIGHTLY RESTLESS, SLIGHTLY OFF FEELING — LEARNING ITS TRUE NAME

Let me give you the exact name for what the Sugar Gremlin feels like when it whines. Hold these words exactly, because you will use them to re-label every churn from now on.

It is a faint, empty, slightly restless, slightly off feeling.

That is all it is.

Is it agony? No. Is it gripping pain? No. Is it even hunger? No. True hunger builds slowly. It is specific. It is satisfied by real food. This feeling appears on a schedule. It is vague. It is inseparable from a thought: "Something sweet would be nice right now."

Notice where you feel it. Is it in your stomach? Often not. Is it a headache? No. It is a faint, empty, slightly restless, slightly off feeling behind the eyes, in the jaw, in the hands, like a radio tuned just off station. Slightly insecure. Slightly uptight. Almost nothing. Yet the Sweet Brainwashing shouts about it as if it were urgent.

Have you ever been distracted and forgotten to eat something sweet for hours? You were busy. The film swallowed you. The walk took you. And for an hour or two you felt fine. Was the Gremlin roaring then?

No. It was quiet. Because attention was elsewhere, the Brainwashing had no microphone.

Have you ever sat near an open tin and felt the wanting become loud even though you were not hungry and your last meal was an hour ago? The cue — wrapper, smell, sight — fired wanting before your body asked for anything. In rodent work, that cue-reactivity has a neural analogue. Cues can fire the "get it" circuit even when the body is quiet. That does not mean you are physically compelled. It does not prove human dependence. It does not promise you will never notice a cue again after you stop. Cues are echoes. They are not commands.

So the feeling is not a command to eat. It is a faint, empty, slightly restless, slightly off feeling that the Sweet Brainwashing taught you to obey.

Can you feel how deflating that is? You have spent years thinking "I need sugar" was a powerful need. The fact is it was a tiny feeling plus a loud story about that feeling. The loudness came from belief, not from the body.

Does that make you weak? No. It makes you brainwashed. And brainwashing can be undone easily, immediately and permanently once seen. That is good news.

Shall we see the inversion that makes the story collapse?

### THE RESCUER AS PERPETRATOR — WHY THE LIFT NEVER ROSE ABOVE NORMAL

Remember tight shoes? You wore shoes two sizes too small all day. At four o'clock you took them off. Huge relief. Was that pleasure?

No. It was the end of pain you created by wearing the shoes.

BAD SUGAR is the tight shoe you forgot you were wearing.

You dose. For a few minutes the faint, empty, slightly restless, slightly off feeling lifts. You call it lift, energy, calm, reward. Then, because the dose guarantees the next dip, the shoe tightens again. Slightly empty again. Slightly off again. You call that dip "need," "stress," "low energy," "I deserve a treat."

Who created the dip?

BAD SUGAR did.

Who then relieves it?

BAD SUGAR does.

Do you see the perfect circle? The thing you reach for as cure is the cause. The rescuer is the perpetrator.

Picture this other image with me. A man pushes you into the river when you are not looking. Then he pulls you out, wraps you in a blanket, and you thank him with tears. You say, "He saved me." You feel grateful. Were you saved?

No. You were pushed. Then retrieved. The gratitude is the con.

That is what happens every time you feel the sweet "save" your afternoon. The sweet pushed you into the dip two hours earlier. Now it pulls you out for five minutes and you thank it. Would you thank anyone else for that?

It is the other way around.

The boost comes from the low it created.

You never rose above the baseline of a person free from BAD SUGAR. You were simply returned toward normal — to how you felt before the first sweet you ever had — and then dragged below normal again so you would dose again.

Think of a non-user. A child who never learned to use BAD SUGAR as fuel or calm. Does that child sit at normal? Yes. Does that child feel a faint, empty, slightly restless, slightly off feeling every three hours demanding a biscuit?

No.

Then where did your three o'clock churn come from? From life? Or from the last dose?

It came from the last dose.

Here is the liberating arithmetic. If the lift never exceeded normal, you lose nothing by stopping. You are not giving up a genuine pleasure or treat hovering above normal. You are escaping a loan scheme that steals normal and rents it back for interest.

Is that sacrifice? No. It is escape.

I must be honest with you here as a friend. There is disagreement among scientists about the word addiction for sugar in humans. Honest Cambridge neuroscientists look at the same animal bingeing and say it reflects the access pattern — the intermittency — more than sugar neurochemistry itself, and that human evidence is thin. The camp that runs those binge models says look, the bingeing, the dopamine flicker, the opioid-mediated dip, the cue reactivity are real and measurable. Both camps grant the behaviours under intermittent access. Both distinguish wanting from liking in the brain. Dopamine is not pudding.

Why do I tell you both ropes? Because the Sugar Trap holds with either rope. Even if you grant the most sceptical view — that sugar is not pharmacologically addictive in the way drugs are — the trap still holds. Why? Because belief plus intermittency is enough. You believe BAD SUGAR gives you something, and you eat it intermittently — diets, treats, carve-out rules, "I don't keep it in the house," "just one won't hurt." That schedule alone escalates wanting and makes each hit feel like relief. And even if you grant the stronger view — that there is a real, mild but well-defined physical loop — it is still small, brief, and self-resolving. Not severe. Not frightening.

So both sides of the argument point one way: the wanting is not proof that BAD SUGAR benefits you. It is proof you were looped.

Can you feel the kindness in that? You need not decide which lab is right to be free. Either way, freedom is the same: starve the tiny Gremlin and unplug the loud Brainwashing. The tug-of-war of fear — "am I an addict or am I weak?" — was always the trap holding both ends. We drop both.

You are not an addict for life. You are not diseased. You are not uniquely weak. Many people experience addictive-like patterns with highly palatable food when asked on validated scales — about one in seven adults — similar in numbers to other common traps. That should not label you. It should comfort you. You are not alone. And you are not broken.

What you are is someone whose body can cope perfectly well without BAD SUGAR — because your body manufactures its own glucose from protein and glycerol — and whose mind was taught to misread a faint, empty, slightly restless, slightly off feeling as a need for a fix.

One more step, and the mechanism is fully installed.

### WHY YOU MUST UNPLUG EVERY CORD, NOT NINETY-NINE

This is why moderation feels so hard and total freedom feels so easy. Cut down and you keep the Brainwashing alive on a drip. Totality is the mercy. When you stop feeding the Sugar Gremlin at all, and you stop believing the Sweet Brainwashing at all, the Gremlin starves. Quickly.
