### "I LOVE THE TASTE" — DO YOU?

We say it without thinking. "I love the taste."

Do you hear yourself say it? Do you love the taste of sweets, chocolate, biscuits, cake? Do you say that is why you do it?

I did. We all did. That is the first entry we put in the FOR column without proof. Taste. Pleasure. Deliciousness. It feels so obvious that we never test it.

But have you ever tested it? Really tested it?

If you love the taste, why do you hide the taste? Why do you mask a bitter coffee with sugar? If the taste is so wonderful, why does the coffee need disguising? The fact is… you are not tasting sugar. You are tasting coffee made less like coffee.

Think of your own day. When you reach for something quick and sweet, what are you tasting? Are you tasting BAD SUGAR? Or are you tasting chocolate, vanilla, salt, crunch, melt, cold, cream?

Do you see the trick? We credit BAD SUGAR for pleasures that belong to other things.

I want you to be your own investigator now. Not my convert. Your own judge. We will isolate the variable. We will take BAD SUGAR out of the moment and see what is left.

Do you love the taste? Let's find out.

First, do you love sweet taste at all? Yes. Of course you do. Sweet taste is real. A ripe mango is sweet. A fresh fig is sweet. Your tongue was made to notice sweetness. I am not here to tell you sweetness does not exist. I am here to show you that BAD SUGAR borrowed its reputation from those natural sweetnesses and then overstayed its welcome.

Would you eat a spoonful of plain white sugar with the same delight you eat a fresh mango? Would you chew dry white bread and call it delicious on its own?

No. You wouldn't. And that answer matters more than any argument I could make.

So we will not argue that you do not like sweet taste. We will ask a sharper question. Does BAD SUGAR, by itself, taste good? Or does it need help?

Take the strongest case you hold in memory. That first bite that made you sigh. Was it the sugar? Or was it everything else?

Hold that memory kindly. We will walk into it together and move the credit where it belongs.

### THE LONELY CARB THAT NEEDS COMPANY

Have you ever seen a lonely carb at a house party?

Picture it. White bread. Plain crackers. A dry digestive. A naked white bun. It stands by the wall, awkward, dry, a little cardboard. No one talks to it. No one wants it.

What does it do? It invites company. It needs jam. It needs cheese. It needs butter, peanut butter, ham, chocolate spread. It cannot be enjoyed alone so it brings friends to be liked.

That is the House-Party Gatecrasher. And it is the perfect picture of BAD SUGAR's taste claim.

We do this every day and call it normal. Toast that needs jam to be palatable. Crackers that need dip. Cereal that needs sugar to be edible. A biscuit that needs tea to wash it down. Do you see? The vehicle that carries BAD SUGAR cannot stand on its own.

Ask yourself honestly. Would you eat a slice of plain white bread, dry, by choice, and call it a treat? Would you eat a spoon of granulated sugar from the bowl and smile? If BAD SUGAR truly tasted good by itself, why does it always need a disguise?

The fact is… refined carbs are lonely. Stripped of fibre and structure, they are bland, pasty, dry. Food engineers know this. That is why they must add fat, salt, crunch and melt to make you want more. The lonely carb needs company over to be noticed.

Now replay your favourite hit. That biscuit. Was the pleasure the sugar? Or was it the butter, the crunch, the salt, the smell of baking that filled the kitchen? That cake. Was it the sugar? Or was it the moist crumb, the cream, the celebration, the people laughing around the table?

Isolate the variable. Take the BAD SUGAR out. Does the warmth remain? Does the texture remain? Does the occasion remain?

Yes. It does.

And when you put BAD SUGAR back in alone — just the white crystals, just the white bread without spread — does the magic remain?

No. It doesn't. It tastes flat. It tastes oddly empty. It tastes like something that needs help.

Hear how your own justifications prove it. You say "just a treat" while eating a food that needed three other foods to become a treat. You say "I don't keep it in the house" because you know the lonely carb will call friends if it is there. Your own language tells you the truth. The carb is lonely. It is not a genuine treat or lift. It is a gatecrasher sneaking a ride on flavours that were lovely before it arrived.

We were conned about taste the way we were conned about choice. We credited the gatecrasher for the party.

Do you feel that faint amusement rising? That slight shift from mouth-watering memory to "oh, wait"? Good. That is the beginning of the turn. Keep it.

### THE CLOY, THE STICK AND THE GLASS OF WATER

Let's audit the actual taste. Not the memory. The mouth-feel.

Close your eyes and remember the last time you ate something very sweet. Two biscuits. A big slice. A fizzy drink. What happened after the first second?

Was it pure delight? Or was it cloy? Stick? An oversweet edge that made you reach for water?

Do you know that feeling? Teeth a little fuzzy. Tongue a little coated. Throat calling for a drink. "Too sweet." We say it ourselves.

Why do we need water after sweets? Why do we never need water after fresh food in the same way? A peach does not leave you parched. An apple does not coat your mouth. Fresh foods finish clean. BAD SUGAR finishes sticky.

Do you remember needing to brush your teeth after sugar? That urge is not moral. It is mechanical. Your body notices.

Think of that last binge — the one that went from "tiny sliver" to empty box. Did the fifth biscuit taste better than the first? Did the tenth chocolate taste better than the second?

No. It tasted less. It tasted cloying. You were not tasting more pleasure. You were chasing the memory of the first bite while the actual taste got worse.

If BAD SUGAR tasted genuinely good, more would taste better. It does not. More tastes sickly. More needs water, needs tea, needs to be finished so the sensation stops.

Have you ever masked bad coffee with sugar and then called sugar delicious? We have. We take a bitter, burnt drink and pour in sugar to hide the bitterness, then thank the sugar. Who deserves the credit there? The sugar hid a flaw. That is not deliciousness. That is concealment.

Try this small isolation test in your mind. Imagine eating a spoonful of plain icing sugar. Just that. No chocolate. No cake. No coffee. Just sweet dust on your tongue. Is it delightful? Or is it overwhelming, then faintly nauseating?

You know the answer. Your body knows the answer. Too much sweetness is not more pleasure. It is less.

So why do we call it "I love the taste" when the taste itself, isolated, is cloy, stick and oversweet? Because we never isolated it before. We tasted the whole scene and gave the gatecrasher the applause.

I am not asking you to believe me. I am asking you to remember your own mouth. That cloy. That stick. That glass of water you always reach for. Was that delight? Or was that your body telling you the dose was crude?

You see it now. Even the taste argument, on its own terms, does not hold. The pleasure you remember was sensation, texture, temperature, company, relief of sitting down — everything except the BAD SUGAR itself. The BAD SUGAR was only sneaking a ride while those lovely things did the work.

And here is the little preview I promised you. Remember tight shoes? Wearing tight shoes all day for the pleasure of taking them off. The relief feels wonderful. But did the shoes give you pleasure? No. They gave you pain that the removal relieved. We will meet that inversion fully when we talk about energy. For taste, just hold the shape in mind. What if the "deliciousness" you feel is not a gain at all, but relief from the subtle discomfort the last hit created?

Keep that thought lightly. We will prove it soon.

For now, ask this. If the taste were genuinely wonderful on its own, why would your body ask for water, not more?

### WHY WRAPPERS MAKE YOUR MOUTH WATER BEFORE YOU TASTE

Do you know this moment? You see the wrapper. You hear the rustle. You smell the bakery as you walk past. Your mouth waters before you have taken a bite.

Is that taste? You have not tasted yet.

What is happening? Your brain is firing before the tongue. The sight, the crinkle, the smell, the 3pm clock, the petrol-station aisle — all of these learn to fire wanting before flavour arrives. Much modern work shows the same pattern: the cues around sugar and highly palatable foods can trigger hyper-reactivity — mouth-watering, attention snapping, urge — before you eat, while the blunted reward of the food itself means you need more to feel the same. That picture is consistent across labs, though imaging work is often in obesity and binge contexts and its interpretation is debated. It is not proof your brain is broken. It is proof your brain learned cues.

Do you recognise that cue-fire in your own day? You are not hungry. You say so yourself: "I don't need it, I just want it." Then you walk past the office biscuits and suddenly you do want it. What changed? Not hunger. The cue.

Think of your own line: "I need something RIGHT NOW." Is that hunger? Would a plain carrot, a handful of nuts, plain steamed fish right then satisfy it? If not — if only BAD SUGAR will do — then it is not hunger. It is the cue whispering, plus the old belief that BAD SUGAR is a genuine treat or lift, selling that whisper as a need.

The fact is… you began watering before you tasted. That means the pleasure you anticipate is not coming from taste. It is coming from expectation. And expectation was installed.

When did you install it? Not by choice. From childhood, from adverts, from "you've been good, have a treat." The belief was taught. The cues were trained. The mouth-watering you feel proves the training worked, not that BAD SUGAR tastes wonderful.

And here is the gentle cruelty of it. The cue promises delight. The bite delivers cloy. The cue promised relief. The bite leaves you wanting another. So the cue fires again. That is not taste pleasure. That is a loop.

Have you ever opened a packet you did not even want, just because you saw it? Have you ever finished a bag and thought, "That wasn't even that nice"? Do you see what that proves? The anticipation was stronger than the taste. The wrapper did more than the flavour.

We were taught to call that anticipation "love of taste." It is not. It is learned wanting, firing before bite, borrowing the memory of a scene that was lovely despite the sugar, not because of it.

So isolate again. Next time the wrapper crinkles and your mouth waters, pause. Ask: "What am I tasting? Nothing yet. So what is this feeling?" It is cue, not taste. It is brainwashing, not delight.

Does that not make you smile a little? The gatecrasher sent the invitation before it arrived.

And notice what this means for your FOR column. The strongest evidence people offer for taste — "I just crave it, I love it" — happens before taste exists. The craving is not tasting. The craving is remembering a ride you took and miscrediting who drove.

You know the lonely carb needs company. You know the cloy asks for water. You know the wrapper waters your mouth before the tongue has work to do. What is left of "delicious" that belongs to BAD SUGAR alone?

Very little. Almost nothing.

### IT WAS NEVER THE TASTE

Let's close the audit where we started.

We asked, what does BAD SUGAR do FOR you? Taste was the first and easiest promise on the list. The comfort eater, the secret binger, the daily grazer — we all put taste at the top, because it feels safest to defend. Who can argue with "I just like it"?

We have argued with it. Calmly. Jointly. By isolating the variable.

We asked whether you would eat the lonely carb alone. You wouldn't. We asked whether plain sugar by the spoonful delights. It overwhelms. We asked whether more tastes better. It tastes stickier. We asked why you need water after sweets but not after fresh food. Your body answered. We asked why wrappers make you water before taste. Your cues answered. Each answer moved credit away from BAD SUGAR and back to where it belonged: texture, temperature, aroma, fat, salt, crunch, company, warmth, rest, celebration — everything that was sneaking a ride while BAD SUGAR took the applause.

Do you feel the shift? From mouth-watering memory to faint amusement? From "I love it" to "it needed so much help to be liked"?

That amusement is important. It is not shame. It is freedom peeking through. You are not weak for being fooled about taste. We all credited the gatecrasher because we never asked it to stand alone.

Is BAD SUGAR tasteless? No. I promised we would not claim that. Sweet taste exists. But the crude, stripped hit of BAD SUGAR — the white bread, the icing, the fizzy blast — is not a wonderful taste. It is a cloying hit that borrows, masks and coats, then asks for water.

Could that be called a genuine treat or lift?

If it were, would it need jam, cheese, cream, chocolate, coffee and celebration to be enjoyed? Would it leave your mouth coated and your teeth fuzzy? Would the wrapper need to water your mouth before the tongue agreed? Would the fifth bite taste worse than the first?

No. No. No.

A genuine delight leaves you satisfied. A gatecrasher leaves you wanting another invite.

So we land this chapter's verdict flat and warm, without blame, as settled fact for you to carry into the next audits.

**IT WAS NEVER THE TASTE.**

Say it gently to yourself. Not as deprivation. As clarity. The taste you loved was real — but it was not BAD SUGAR's. It was the food, the moment, the people, the rest, the warmth. BAD SUGAR was only sneaking a ride.

Look at your ledger. In the last chapter you saw the TO column heavy and the FOR column empty. This chapter tested the first FOR you wrote in — taste — and found it empty too.

Do you feel the method working? We are isolating each promise and moving the credit. Taste is crossed off. One cord unplugged.

We will do the same for energy — where the tight-shoes inversion becomes the whole story — then for reward and for normal. By then your FOR column will stay empty because empty is what it is. And an empty FOR column makes the decision obvious: there is nothing to weigh up.

For now, rejoice in this small, cheerful demolition. You have not given up deliciousness. You have seen that deliciousness never belonged to BAD SUGAR in the first place.

And what borrows its pleasure cannot be a pleasure you will miss.
