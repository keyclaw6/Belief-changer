### COUNTING A "NORMAL" DAY

Let's do something simple. Let's count.

I want you to count a normal day with me. Not a bad day. Not a binge. Your normal.

You wake. Do you have tea or coffee? Spoon in? Splash of something sweet? That's one. Glass of juice that feels healthy? That's two. White toast or a cereal that snaps and crackles? Check the packet — added sugar. That's three. A mid-morning biscuit with the tea that someone brought to the meeting? That's four.

Lunch. Bread that looks plain but lists sugar. Ketchup. A yoghurt that says low fat so you trust it. That's five, six, seven and you haven't done anything wrong. You have done what we all do.

Afternoon. Do you know that 3pm voice? "I need something RIGHT NOW." "Just a treat." "To get me through." The chocolate bar from the desk drawer. The sweet drink that lifts you for twenty minutes. That's eight. Dinner — a sauce from a jar. That's nine. Evening — "I've been good this week" and the sofa and the family bag. That's ten.

Did you "keep it in the house"? Do you say "I don't keep it in the house" while bread and sauce and yoghurt and biscuits live in your cupboards? Do you say "once a week is fine" while you dose ten times before Friday?

I'm not counting to shame you. I'm counting to show you something you haven't seen. We never added it up. We were never meant to.

When you lay it out, does that look like free choice? Or does it look like a loop you never agreed to?

Ask yourself honestly. Do you choose each hit because you love it? Or do you take the next hit because the last one set you up for it?

You say "I could stop if I wanted to." I know you say it. We all said it. It's what I said for years while I grazed all day and binged at night and hid the wrappers. "Just a treat." "I need it for energy." "It's fuel not a treat." Hear your own phrases coming back to you? Good. That means you are in the right book. That is the community dialect — dozens of lines, all saying the same thing: the rollercoaster is speaking, not you.

Try this honest question. How many hours yesterday did you spend negotiating with BAD SUGAR? Planning it? Delaying it? Justifying it? Recovering from it? Promising tomorrow will be different? Is that freedom? Is that what a free person does with food?

Think. Be precise. Count again, slowly. How free is a day with ten automatic doses you never consciously chose?

### IF YOU FREELY CHOOSE IT, WHY WOULD YOU NEED THIS BOOK?

Here is the disarming question I promised you in Chapter 1.

If BAD SUGAR is a genuine pleasure you freely choose, why would you need a book to stop it?

Would you need a book to stop listening to music you dislike? Would you need instructions to avoid a hobby you find boring? Would you need willpower to stop eating food that you freely enjoy or freely leave?

You wouldn't. We don't. We simply don't do things we don't want to do. No struggle. No countdown. No rules.

So what does the fact you are holding this book prove?

It proves the choice is not free. It proves you feel a tug. It proves a part of you wants to stop and a part of you fears losing something if you do. That tug feels personal. It feels like weakness. It is not. It is the signature feeling of being trapped.

Do you feel that tug right now? A little defence rising? "But I do enjoy it." "But everyone eats it." Of course you feel it. I would worry if you didn't. The trap taught you to defend it. The defence is installed.

Let me ask you the trap questions that only have one honest answer.

If you truly enjoy being tired, foggy and drained between hits, why do you keep trying to fix it?

If BAD SUGAR truly gives you freedom, why do you need rules to control it?

If you are truly choosing, why does "just one" scare you?

See? Each honest answer concedes the same point. You are not choosing. You are looping. You are caught.

And here is the tender hint that will return again and again in this book. That relief you feel when you finally have the sweet — is it pleasure? Or is it the feeling of a discomfort ending? Does the biscuit create joy? Or does it quiet an unease that was not there before the last biscuit?

Hold that thought lightly. Don't fight it. Just notice it. We will prove it together, benefit by benefit, in the chapters ahead. For now, notice only this: the relief fits the shape of a discomfort the habit itself created. That is not love. That is relief from a private itch the last dose lit.

The fact is… you have nothing to lose and everything to gain by questioning the idea that you freely choose this. Why? Because if choice was removed, then blame leaves with it. And if blame leaves, freedom becomes possible without shame.

### THE CONMAN'S PITCH — HOW CHOICE WAS REMOVED BEFORE YOU CHOSE

Let me give you the picture that dissolves the shame and the illusion of choice in one move.

I call it the Conman's Pitch.

A well-dressed fraud knocks on your door. He is charming. He carries a chart. He says, "Pay me every day and I will give you a wonderful lift. A boost. A little reward to get you through." You pay. You feel a little lift. You thank him. You tell your friends. You pay again tomorrow. The lift fades. You pay again to get it back.

What you don't see is simple. He stole that lift from you the night before. He takes a little of your normal calm and your steady energy and your appetite peace, then sells you back a fraction of it and calls it a treat. You thank the person who robbed you.

Would you call yourself stupid for falling for him? Would you call yourself weak? No. You would call him a conman. You fell for a pitch delivered on phoney information. We all did.

That is what happened with BAD SUGAR. We did not choose it the way we choose a favourite meal as adults. We chose the first sweets as children because adults we trusted said it was a treat, a reward, love, energy. "Have a sweet for being good." "Just a treat won't hurt." The information was phoney. Then the trap fed itself.

This is what I mean when I name it plainly for the first time in this book: the Sugar Trap. Take those two words and keep them. the Sugar Trap is not sugar as a molecule. It is the closed loop: phoney belief plus engineered more-ishness plus daily repetition plus the low that the last hit guarantees. Once the loop closes, it powers itself. The belief says "this helps." The hit creates the next low. The low proves the belief. Round and round.

Do you see why "I could stop if I wanted to" is the trap speaking? You have wanted to. You have tried. You used the only method anyone ever taught you — white-knuckle and rules — and it left you more obsessed, not more free. No wonder you concluded you were the problem. You were never the problem. The method was the problem. The con was the problem.

And here is the moment when unease turns to exonerated anger.

You were not greedy. You were not broken. You were outgunned.

An industry of flavour labs tuned sugar to its bliss point — sugar plus fat plus crunch plus melt — precisely to make you reach again. Packaging, placement, price, habit, advertising to children, portion creep — all tilted. You were a child when the pitch started. Do you blame a child for believing a trusted adult who says "this is a treat"?

Of course not. So don't blame the adult you became for acting on that early lesson.

Ask yourself now, gently but firmly: if the first lesson about BAD SUGAR had been true — that it does nothing for you and only takes from you — would you have taken the second dose? The tenth? The ten-thousandth?

No. None of us would have. So the choice was removed at the start, not at the moment you tried to stop. The trap used your trust. That is not shameful. That is common. And common is the point.

You are not uniquely weak. Large pooled surveys using validated food questionnaires find that about 14 in 100 adults and about 12 in 100 children report patterns of highly palatable food use that score in the addiction-like range on those scales. Not a diagnosis. Not a label on you. Self-report answers on a checklist. The score simply means the behaviour feels driven, not freely chosen, and it is strikingly common — as common as the traps for alcohol or tobacco on the same kinds of scales. Do you feel less alone hearing that? You should. A trap that catches one in seven adults is not a character flaw. It is a trap.

So let's land the verdict we have earned, flat and warm, wound and bandage in the same breath.

You are caught in the Sugar Trap. It is not your fault. It was engineered. And because it was engineered, it can be dismantled. Easily. Without shame.

**YOU WERE CONNED — YOU DID NOT CHOOSE THIS.**

Read that again and feel the weight lift. You did not choose this. We fell in. Now we get out.

### THE TUG-OF-WAR THAT ISN'T TWO SIDES AT ALL

Inside you there is a rope pull.

One side says, "I want to stop. I feel tired, foggy and drained. I want freedom for the rest of my life." The other side says, "But I love it. But I need it. But what will I have left?"

We call that the Tug-of-War. You've lived it for years. Rules, bargains, restarts. "I'll be good Monday." "Just this once." Pull left. Pull right. Exhausting, isn't it?

Here is the liberating discovery that ends the war instantly.

Both ends of the rope belong to the same hand. the Tug-of-War is not you versus you. It is the Sugar Trap versus you, pulling both sides.

One side pulls with fear — "you'll fail again." The other side pulls with desire — "you'll lose your treat." Both are the same lie in different costumes. Both say BAD SUGAR does something for you that you cannot be without. That lie is the Sweet Con we will name fully soon, but you don't need its full anatomy to see this today.

Do you see? If BAD SUGAR did something genuine for you, the rope would have a real anchor on the "keep it" side. We will test every claimed anchor in Chapters 4 to 7 — taste, energy, reward, normal life — and you will find there is no anchor there. Nothing holds that side. The rope is not fixed to a real benefit. It is looped around the belief that there is a benefit. Belief is all that holds it.

So what do you do with a tug-of-war where the enemy holds both ends?

You don't pull harder. You don't get stronger. You cut the rope.

Cutting the rope does not take strength. It takes seeing. When you see that both voices are the trap talking, you stop debating the trap. You stop negotiating doses. You stop asking the wrong question, which is "how much can I have?" You ask the right one, which is "what does it actually do for me?" And the answer, which we will audit to zero together, is nothing.

Do you feel how that changes the feeling of stopping? It is no longer deprivation. It is release from a pointless pull that tired you out for years. You have spent real life energy holding that rope. Imagine that energy back in your hands. How wonderful will that be? Think of the peace when you no longer spend your day in negotiation. Think of the quiet after the war ends.

And you have nothing to lose and everything to gain by cutting it now, in your mind, as a decision about truth — even while you carry on eating exactly as now until the end of the book, as you agreed. No threat. No sacrifice to white-knuckle. Just understanding.

### WHEN EVERYONE'S FOGGY, NO ONE CALLS IT FOG

We have dissolved individual choice. Now dissolve normal.

Look around. Everyone does it. Office biscuits. Vending machines. Children's parties where sugar is the party. Supermarket aisles where nine out of ten packets contain added BAD SUGAR. If everyone does it, how can it be a trap? Isn't it just normal eating?

This is where the trick gets clever. The trap hides in the crowd.

I use a simple image. The Boiling Frog. Put a frog in cold water and heat it degree by degree. The frog doesn't jump. It calls the warming water normal until it is cooking. It mistakes a slow drift for a natural state.

We did the same.

We grew up in water that got sweeter every decade. Portions grew. Drinks grew. Snacking frequency grew. Marketing grew. And as everyone's baseline drifted down — a little more tired, foggy and drained, a little more restless between meals, a little more in need of a fix to feel okay — we called it "just how I am." We call it tired. We call it stress. We call it age. We don't call it the Sugar Trap, because when everyone is on the rollercoaster, no one calls it a rollercoaster. They call it life.

Does that ring true? Do you know the heavy 4pm feeling that everyone at work shares? Do you hear colleagues say "I need sugar to get through the afternoon" and nod because you feel it too? When a feeling is shared, we assume it is natural. It is not. It is normalized.

Do you want a check? Think of a child playing outside before the habits set — steady energy for hours on plain food, no bargaining, no 3pm crash. Think of a day when you ate fresh, simple food and forgot to think about BAD SUGAR for hours. Did you lack anything? Or did the need only appear after the sweet hit?

The trap persuades you that its low is your personality. It is not. tired, foggy and drained is not who you are. It is the cost of staying on the ride. And the ride now runs for the rest of your life if you stay on it — not as a single dramatic disaster, but as a daily tax: money every single day, time every single day, attention every single day, appetite bargained every single day, for the rest of your life, for something that never once did anything for you.

That phrase — for the rest of your life — can land two ways. In the trap it means a sentence: trapped, tired, paying, negotiating, forever. In freedom it means release: free, clear, steady, eating with pleasure, forever. Same words. Opposite futures. Which will you choose? The choice is no longer between sweet and not sweet. The choice is between a trap for the rest of your life and freedom for the rest of your life.

And here is the schedule truth that seals why "normal" grazing is not harmless. Laboratory work on eating schedules finds a clear pattern: it is intermittent access — brief restriction, then a hit, then restriction again — that drives bingeing, craving and the low-high cycle, while steady, free access does not show that driven pattern. Read that in plain English. "I'll be good until 3pm" then "just one biscuit" then "I'll start again tomorrow" is not self-control. It is the exact rhythm that powers the trap. Do you see why cutting down made you think about BAD SUGAR more, not less? Do you see why "I don't keep it in the house" followed by an evening empty box is not lack of character but schedule?

There is nothing wrong with you. There is everything wrong with the trap you were normalized into.

So let's name normal plainly.

Normalised is not natural. Common is not chosen. Popular is not beneficial. The Boiling Frog calls the warming water home until someone points to the thermometer. I am pointing to it now. Your steady, natural baseline — clear, calm, hungry at mealtimes, satisfied after eating, energetic between meals — is still there under the noise. You haven't lost it. It has been drowned out by hits.

You were conned. The crowd was conned. The warmth of the water is not proof of safety. It is proof the heating was slow.

Do you feel the shift? From "I choose this" to "I was taught to want this"? From shame to anger at the deceiver? From "what's wrong with me?" to "what was done to us?" Hold that shift. It is the beginning of freedom. It is also the belief you will guard for life.

You do not need to fix normal. You need to see it. And when you see it, normal loses its power to justify the next hit.
