### "EVERYONE DOES IT" — HEARING YOUR OWN VOICE

Do you say this to yourself?

"Everyone does it."

"It's normal."

"It's the sociable thing."

"It's just a treat."

"Once a week is fine — everyone has pudding."

"I don't keep it in the house — but at parties, at work, of course."

We all said it. I said it. We said it to make the unease go quiet. We use the crowd as a blanket. If everyone does it, it must be safe. If everyone does it, it must be natural. If everyone does it, how could it be a trap?

Is that true?

We fell into this together. We learned to call BAD SUGAR a genuine treat or lift because the world around us kept calling it that, offering it, pressing it on us with a smile. We did not decide after careful thought. We absorbed. We copied. We normalised.

Hear the menu in your own dialect. "Just a treat." "I've been good this week, I deserve it." "To get me through the afternoon." "I can't stop once I start" — but then "it's sociable, it's sharing." Do you see the contradiction? It is a solitary compulsion and a sociable ritual in the same sentence. Which is it?

You already know the answer. You have lived both. You have eaten alone, quickly, a little ashamed, and you have eaten together, laughing, passing the plate. Same dose. Two stories. So the story is not in the dose.

The question is not whether people are sociable. They are. Wonderfully. The question is whether BAD SUGAR is what makes them so. Does togetherness need it? Or was it only ever there because it was placed there?

Think. Look around.

### COUNTING NORMAL — THE AISLE, THE TIN, THE PARTY BAG

Let us audit "normal" together. Not with opinions. With eyes.

Walk the supermarket aisle in your head with me. You go in for bread, milk, fruit. What is normal? Thirty metres of biscuits, cakes, sweets, sugary drinks, confectionery, ultra-processed snacks that cannot sit alone without sugar or fat or salt to make them more-ish. The fresh food is at the edges. The engineered hits fill the middle. Is that nature? Or is that placement?

Count your own normal day. Not a binge day. A "good" day. The sweetened coffee. The juice. The white bread that is a junk-processed carb — lonely, needing jam to be palatable. The biscuit with tea because the tin is on the work surface. The 3pm hit "to get me through." The sauce with added sugar you did not ask for. The dessert because everyone else is having it. How many doses of BAD SUGAR is that? Four? Six? Eight? And you call that moderate.

Now count the office. The tin that never empties. Someone's birthday. Doughnuts on Friday "for the team." You say, "once a week is fine." But it is not once a week. It is once a week plus the aisle plus the coffee plus the bread. Normal is not one dose. Normal is a rhythm.

Now count the kids' party. The bag. The cake. The juice boxes. The table that is almost entirely BAD SUGAR and nothing else a body recognises as food. We call it normal for children. The fact is… international guidance for free sugars is to keep them below ten percent of energy, and below five percent for an extra benefit — roughly a few spoonfuls in a whole day's eating — and typical intakes in our countries sit far above both. I do not give you a personal prescription here; for any medical specifics, follow your clinician. I want you to see the gap. What we call normal sits above what population guidance calls a ceiling. Normal exceeds guidance. That is the fact, plainly. Normal is not measured. Normal was marketed.

Do you see? Normal is not natural. Normal is available. Frequent. Pressed upon you. And frequency is not a benefit. Frequency is just proof of supply.

Ask the plain question. If everyone jumped off a cliff, would you jump? No. You would ask whether the cliff is safe. So why, when everyone eats BAD SUGAR, do we not ask whether normal is safe? Why do we use the crowd to answer a different question — "does it actually do anything FOR me?"

We use "everyone does it" because we have no better answer. That should worry us. An argument with no argument inside it is not an argument. It is a habit wearing a crowd as a costume.

We were conned, not foolish. The con works best when it is crowded. A crowded con feels like culture.

### WOULD A NON-USER CALL THIS NORMAL?

Picture a non-user. Not a saint. Not a monk. Just a person who never fell into the Sugar Trap and who eats fresh food freely until satisfied. She likes sweetness — berries, mango, a peach that drips down her wrist — but she has no cue for BAD SUGAR. She walks your day with you.

She watches the aisle. She sees a wall of packets engineered to say "more-ish" before the first bite. She thinks, why would food need engineering to be wanted? Fresh food does not need a lab to be wanted.

She watches the office tin. She sees hands reaching without hunger, without asking "would I eat a plain apple right now?" She sees a hit that does not satisfy, that creates the queue for the next hit. She thinks, that is not eating. That is feeding.

She watches the party bag. She sees children given a bag of hits that will spike, then crash, then need another hit, and she wonders why we call that a treat for children. Would we call it a treat to give children anything else that leaves them tired, foggy and nagging for more? We would not. We would call it strange.

What does she call normal? Not unusual. Not rare. She would say, yes, it is common. Very common. The fact is… when researchers use validated self-report scales for highly palatable foods, roughly fourteen in a hundred adults and about twelve in a hundred children report enough addiction-like experiences to cross the scale's threshold — a figure that sits near the figures for alcohol and tobacco, and it is not a clinical diagnosis of you, it is a signal that the trap is common. I say that not to label you, but to lift shame. You are not uniquely weak. You are not the outlier. The trap is wide. When a trap catches that many, the crowdedness is proof of the trap's scale, not proof of safety.

Think of that. Fourteen in a hundred is not "everyone." But it is enough that "everyone does it a little" hides the fact that many are doing it unhappily, covertly, feeling "I can't stop once I start." Normal hides distress inside averages. Ask yourself: if so many meet that threshold while calling it normal, what does normal hide?

The non-user sees what we stopped seeing. We stopped seeing because we were inside it. She sees a population exceeding guidance, slowly cooked, calling the heat "normal."

And once you see through her eyes, you cannot pretend you do not see.

### IS IT SOCIABLE, OR JUST PRESENT AT SOCIABLE MOMENTS?

I will grant you this freely, because it is true. Sociability is real. Belonging is real. Sharing food is one of the oldest human joys. The warmth of a table, laughter, a birthday song — those matter profoundly. I do not diminish them. I honour them.

Does that warmth require BAD SUGAR?

Isolate the variable. Take your most sociable sweet scene. The shared dessert. The cinema chocolate passed along the row. The office cake where you all sing. Now replay it with the company, the light, the laughter, the rest — and without the hit. Add fresh favourite foods eaten freely if hunger is there. Or add nothing but the moment itself. Is there any joy left?

Of course there is. Almost all of it.

Do you sing because of sugar? Do you laugh because of sugar? Do you love your people more because a hit is on the table? No. You love them because they are yours. The hit was present. It was not the cause.

Another trap question: if BAD SUGAR were the sociable agent, why does the same dose eaten alone at the fridge, in the dark, feel the opposite of sociable — secret, flat, a little ashamed? Same substance. Different company. Different feeling. So the feeling was never in the substance. It was in the company. BAD SUGAR was sneaking a ride.

And a second: if sociability needed BAD SUGAR, how do sociable meals without it succeed so completely? Think of the meals that were joyful without a single hit — a summer lunch of fresh foods, bread, fruit, laughter, second helpings because you were hungry and happy. Was that not sociable? Was that not belonging? It was. It needed no packet.

We were taught to credit the rider and forget the horse. The horse is the occasion. The rider is BAD SUGAR. Remove the rider and the horse still gallops.

Do you see the trick? The Sugar Trap did not need to make BAD SUGAR genuinely sociable. It only needed to make it *present* at every sociable moment, until we misread presence as necessity. Availability did the brainwashing. The biscuit tin on the counter did more teaching than any advert. We learned "sociable equals sweet" the way we learn any association: by repetition, not by truth.

Would you say oxygen is sociable because you breathe it at parties? No. You breathe it everywhere. Sociability is in you and your people, not in what happened to be on the table while you were being together.

You lose nothing sociable when you stop BAD SUGAR. You gain clarity. You will still sit, share, sing, laugh. You will pity, not envy, the hands that still reach for the tin — not because your friends are foolish, but because they are still inside the same crowded con you just stepped out of. Your freedom does not separate you from them. It returns you to them.

It was never a genuine treat or lift. It was a passenger. Let it walk.

### THE BOILING FROG — WHEN EVERYONE'S FOGGY, NO ONE CALLS IT FOG

Have you heard the old story of the frog? Dropped in hot water, it jumps. Placed in cool water slowly heated, it sits. The heat rises degree by degree. The frog calls each degree normal until it is cooked. It never jumps because it never notices a moment to jump.

We are the frog.

The heat in our case is tired, foggy and drained — the addict's permanent state, not the dramatic crash but the low background ache we stopped noticing. The lift-crash-queue we live inside. The 3pm fog. The evening need. The faint restless need for something quick and sweet. We call it normal because everyone feels it.

When everyone's tired, no one calls it tired. When everyone's foggy, no one calls it fog. When everyone's drained, we call it life.

Do you remember when you did not need a hit to get through an afternoon? As a child, you ran, ate, rested, ran again. No tin. No queue. Or think of a holiday week eating fresh foods, sleeping well, moving lightly. Did you need the hit then? No. Your baseline was higher without it. The need appears only inside the trap.

The Boiling Frog does not prove biology. It proves perception. Slow harm feels like normal until you step out and feel the contrast. That is why we need the non-user's eyes. She is out of the water. She feels the heat we stopped feeling.

Ask the trap question that ends the comfort of conformity: if normal is this tired, is normal well? If normal exceeds guidance, predicts the low, and leaves you needing more shortly after every "treat," is normal good? Or is normal just common?

Common is not well. Common is not natural. Common is just crowded.

See it. Name it. The Sugar Trap convinced a whole population to call slow cooking "normal," then sold the briefly cooler water as pleasure. You felt relief when you took the hit the way the frog feels relief when the heat pauses half a degree. That relief is real. It is also proof you were being heated.

You were not weak. You were slow-cooked. Anyone would be. Now you see the pot. You can step out. The water is not you.

### WHO MADE NORMAL? A PEEK INTO THE BLISS-POINT LAB

I promised a peek through the door we will throw wide in the next chapter. Take it now, briefly, so "normal" loses its innocence.

There is a lab. Not a kitchen. White coats, dials, microscopes. Not to feed you, but to trap you. They test sugar plus fat plus crunch plus melt until the brain says "more-ish." They tune the bliss point — the precise peak of "cannot stop once I start" — weaponised abundance. They test the wrapper, the colour, the smell that fires before the first bite so the cue, not hunger, calls you. They place it at eye level, at the till, in the aisle you must walk, in the tin you must pass, in the party bag you must hand to your child. They buy the ad before the children's programme.

Does that look like nature?

Nature is not a lab. Nature's sweetness is fibre, water, structure, slow release, satisfaction that settles. The lab strips structure, keeps the sweet signal, adds crunch and melt and salt to override settle, and sells the spike as joy. Nature makes a peach that satisfies. The lab makes a packet that nags. Which does your body recognise without training?

You were not out-eaten. You were out-engineered. By armies. Systematically. For profit. Normal is not what people chose freely. Normal is what was installed, distributed, discounted, and normalised until you thought you chose it.

Do you feel angry? Good. Be angry at the system, not at your friends, not at yourself. Your friends were cooked slowly too. They pass the tin because it was placed there for them to pass. Their hand reaching is not enjoyment. It is a cue firing.

The fact is… you did not create normal. You inherited it. You can also leave it. The door was engineered shut; it was never locked. And the moment you see engineering, "everyone does it" stops sounding like safety and starts sounding like scale.

How big would a trap have to be for everyone to call it normal? This big.

### NORMALISED IS NOT NATURAL

Let us land this. We have demolished three faces of the same illusion and now the last falls.

Does BAD SUGAR taste that good? No. We saw it is a lonely con that needs company to be palatable. It was never the taste.

Does it give you energy? No. It creates the tiredness it then briefly relieves. It's the other way around.

Is it your little love, your reward? No. It was payola. It was sneaking a ride on love that was already there. It was never a genuine treat or lift.

And now: is it normal, therefore natural, therefore good?

No.

Normalised is engineered. Normalised is distributed. Normalised is advertised. Normalised is not natural. Natural is the peach, the appetite that asks and is answered, the energy that does not need a queue. Normalised is the aisle that never ends.

We love company. We love celebration. We love belonging. Those loves are real and they remain. They never needed BAD SUGAR. They still don't. You can be fully sociable, fully together, fully at the table, without a single dose. More so, because no rider steals the credit and no low follows the laughter.

Do you see the inversion? We thought "everyone does it" proved BAD SUGAR must be okay. In truth, everyone doing it proves the Sugar Trap is working exactly as designed. Crowdedness is not safety. Crowdedness is exposure. The cure for a crowded trap is not to crowd tighter. It is to step out.

Do you need permission to step out? You have it. Do you need to wait for everyone else? You do not. Freedom is not a group decision. It is a private seeing. One person sees the pot and steps out. Then another sees that person and remembers the feel of cool water. That is how normal changes. Not by waiting. By seeing.

Short verdict. Stand apart. See clear. Feel proud.

We were not foolish. We were normalised.

We are not alone. We are awake.
