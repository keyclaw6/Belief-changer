### "I DESERVE IT" — HEARING YOUR OWN VOICE

Do you say this to yourself?

"I deserve it."

"I've been good this week."

"Celebrating, rewarding myself, going to the cinema, etc with chocolate."

"Just a treat."

I hear you. We all said it. I said it. We said it after a hard day, after a boring day, after a win, after a loss, after putting the kids to bed, after finishing the report. The words feel warm. They feel kind. They feel like self-compassion.

Are they?

We fell into this together. We learned to call BAD SUGAR a genuine treat or lift when we felt low, and a genuine treat or lift when we felt high. Low? You deserve comfort. High? You deserve celebration. In both cases the answer is the same hit. Does that not strike you as strange? One substance for two opposite needs?

Think of the last time you rewarded yourself. Not my story. Yours. Yesterday? Last Friday? Picture it clearly. You had done something hard. You were tired. Or you had done something good. You were pleased. You told yourself, "I deserve it."

What did you actually deserve? Rest? Warmth? Praise? Company? A moment that was yours and no one else's? Be honest. You deserved something tender. Something that said, you did well, you matter.

And what did you give yourself? A dose. A hit. BAD SUGAR. A packet, a bar, a bowl that was gone in minutes.

Did you feel more loved ten minutes after? Did you feel more celebrated? Or did you feel a little flat, a little sticky, a little needy for more?

We need to look. Not with shame. With tenderness. You reached for love. You deserve love. The question is whether BAD SUGAR ever gave it.

The fact is… the language you use already confesses the trap. You call it "just a treat." You call it "to get me through" in other hours. You call it "I can't stop once I start." Those are not the words of a treat. They are the words of the Sugar Trap speaking through your mouth.

Do you call a true reward "just"? Do you need to justify a real love?

You don’t. Love needs no excuse. Trap payola does.

### THE TEN MINUTES AFTER — WHAT ACTUALLY HAPPENED?

Let us replay your last reward beat by beat. I will not invent. You supply the facts. I will ask the questions whose only honest answer concedes the point.

Recall the scene. You were stressed or tired or quietly proud. You told yourself "I deserve it." You unwrapped, poured, scooped. Perhaps you were alone. Perhaps you were with someone, on the sofa, at the cinema, at the kitchen counter after everyone had gone.

For a minute or two there is a hit. Sweetness floods. It feels like comfort. It feels like the day softening. You think, yes, this is it. This is my moment.

What happened at minute ten?

Be honest. Did you feel comforted, or did you feel the edge return a little sharper? Did you feel celebrated, or did you feel a little guilty, a little restless, a little empty again? Did you feel satisfied, or did you think about whether there was more?

Do you remember feeling nourished? Or do you remember feeling a little tired, a little sticky-mouthed, reaching for water?

Do you remember thinking, that was wonderful, I am done? Or did you hear the whisper, go on, have a bit more, you've been good?

We know that whisper. It is not love. Love does not whisper for more love ten minutes after love. Love settles. Love stays.

You were not imagining the comfort. The occasion was comforting. The pause was comforting. The warmth of the room was comforting. The film, the laughter, the rest, your own tired body finally stopping — those were comforting. BAD SUGAR was only along for the ride.

Ask three trap questions. Answer them in your head. The only honest answer concedes the point.

One: if BAD SUGAR were your reward, why do you need to repeat the reward moments after you had it?

Two: if it were comfort, why do you feel less comfortable — more restless, more self-critical — shortly after the comfort?

Three: if it were love, why would love leave you wanting to hide the wrapper?

You know the answers. You have lived them. Not once. Dozens, hundreds of times. The reward predicts the need for another reward. That is not how rewards behave.

The fact is… in a long-term study that followed thousands of adults for years, men who ate more sweets were more likely to develop common mental distress later on — and the researchers checked whether feeling low first drove the sweet-eating, and it did not. This is observational, in men, and it does not prove every sweet causes low mood or that sweets are the sole cause of anything. I do not say it to frighten you. I want you to see it plainly then put fear aside. The "treat" predicted the low, not relief from it. Joy is the mover, not fear. The payola did not pay.

Does that sound like love? A love that predicts you will feel worse?

No. You deserve better accounting. Love that costs you in mood an hour later is not love. It is a loan.

Short truth. Love settles. Payola nags.

You were not rewarding yourself. You were feeding the Sugar Trap and calling it love so you would do it again.

### WHERE WAS THE LOVE REALLY COMING FROM?

Let us isolate the variable. This is the core move that frees you. We will take your sweetest reward scene and strip BAD SUGAR out of it, and see what remains.

Think of the cinema. Or the birthday cake with your people around the table. Or the Friday night on the sofa after a brutal week when you finally exhale. Choose your strongest. The one you defend most tenderly. The one that whispers, "surely THAT was genuine?"

Rebuild it now without BAD SUGAR. Same film. Same sofa. Same soft blanket. Same people laughing. Same rest. Same permission to stop being useful for an hour. Same warmth. Is there any love in that scene without the hit?

Is there? Of course there is. There is almost all of it.

The company is the love. The rest is the love. The permission is the love. The warmth, the laughter, the story on screen, the candles, the singing, your own body being held and rested — those are love. They were love before you added BAD SUGAR, and they remain love after you remove it.

BAD SUGAR was sneaking a ride.

Do you see? You made a wonderful moment and then let BAD SUGAR steal the credit, the way a pickpocket steals credit for your smile while taking your wallet. You thank the pickpocket for the evening. He was not the evening. You were. Your people were.

We were conned into misassigning the source. The trap taught us to say "the chocolate made the film cosy" when the film, the sofa, the company and the permission to rest made the film cosy. We were taught to say "the cake made the birthday joyful" when the people, the singing, the being together made the birthday joyful.

Test it. When you were a child at a birthday, what made you glow? The sugar or the being seen, the friends, the game, the noise, the love? You know.

Another trap question: if the love came from BAD SUGAR, why does the same dose eaten alone, tired, in the dark, standing at the fridge, feel not like love at all but like shame? Same substance. Different occasion. Different feeling. So the feeling was never in the substance. It was in the occasion. The substance was sneaking a ride on your own capacity for joy.

You are not losing love when you stop BAD SUGAR. You are reclaiming it. You are giving the credit back to its rightful owners: your life, your people, your rest, your own heart that kept trying to comfort itself the only way it knew.

That is tender clarity. Not cold. Tender. You reached for love. You deserve love. You will still have it. More of it. Without the rider.

It's yours. It always was.

### THE LIFEGUARD WHO PUSHED YOU IN

I want to land the rescuer-as-perpetrator clearly, because comfort is where the con is most tender.

Picture water. You are swimming. Suddenly you are out of your depth. You thrash. You panic. A hand grabs you. A lifeguard pulls you to the side. You cling to him. You thank him. You love him. He saved you.

Then you learn he pushed you in.

Same hand. Same rescue. Completely different meaning.

Would you still call him your comfort? Would you still call him love? No. You would feel a cold clarity. Your gratitude was misassigned. Your relief was real, but the rescuer was the perpetrator.

That is BAD SUGAR as your little love.

You feel low. Tense. Restless. Slightly empty. Slightly insecure. You have learned to call that "needing a treat." You take the hit. The discomfort pauses for minutes. You feel rescued. You call that "comfort," "reward," "love."

Who created the low it rescued you from?

BAD SUGAR did. The previous hit did. The loop did. The Sugar Trap did. Not your day. Not your character. Not your need. The trap created a faint, gnawing unease and then sold you the brief relief from it as love.

The relief is real. The love is not. Relief from self-inflicted discomfort is not love. It is the hand letting go of the throat it was squeezing and calling it kindness.

Do you see why self-payment keeps the loop alive? When you say "I deserve it," you are paying the lifeguard for pushing you in, so he will push you in again tomorrow to be paid again. Your payment is the dose. Your wage is a few minutes of relief from the unease he guarantees will return.

Would you pay that lifeguard? Would you call that pay love?

You would not. You would get out of the water.

We were not foolish. We were conned. The con is perfect because the relief is genuine for a moment, so we misread the source. We thought comfort came from the hit. The hit was just the squeezing stopping briefly.

Once you see the hand is the same hand, you cannot unsee it. And you cannot feel grateful to it again in the same way.

You were not loving yourself. You were feeding the trap. That is not a shameful confession. That is a freed one. Strong words. True words. Now let relief replace them.

Ask: when you truly love yourself, do you give yourself rest, or restlessness? Do you give yourself settling, or craving? Do you give yourself freedom, or a queue for the next hit?

Love settles. Trap payola nags. You know the difference in your body.

**YOU WERE NOT LOVING YOURSELF — YOU WERE FEEDING THE TRAP.**

Say it without cruelty. Say it with warmth for the person who tried so hard to be kind to herself and was sold the wrong kindness. You tried. You reached. You deserve kindness. This was not it.

### DID YOU LOVE IT AT THE VERY FIRST BITE?

This is the origin test. It strips the nostalgia and reveals the learning.

Do you remember your very first sweets? Not the story you tell now, but the actual sensation. Many of us found the first taste too sweet, too sharp, even a little sickly. We learned to like it. We learned because the adults around us called it a treat, a reward, love. "Be good and you can have a sweet." "You deserve a treat." The words installed the belief before the tongue ever decided.

Ask the origin question: at what moment did you decide BAD SUGAR meant love? Did you reason it through? Did you test it? Or did you absorb it while not looking, the way you absorb a lullaby?

We never thought it over when we found it. That is the fact.

If BAD SUGAR were love itself, your first bite would have felt like love — immediate, complete, unlearned. It did not. You learned to interpret a cloying taste plus a brief relief from tension as love because the Sweet Con narrated it as love. The belief fed the bite and the bite fed the belief.

Do you love the taste itself even now? Isolate it. Not the crunch, the melt, the ritual, the occasion, the sugar-plus-fat-plus-salt engineering that makes the brain say "more-ish." Strip it to pure refined sweetness on the tongue. Is that love? Or is it a brief, sharp sweetness that quickly turns cloying, sticky, thirsty, needing water?

We add sugar to mask, not to reveal. We add it to coffee we don't like plain. We pour it over lonely carbs that cannot be eaten alone — white bread that needs jam, crackers that need spread, popcorn that needs sugar. If the taste were love, it would not need company to be lovable.

Think of your favourite fresh foods. A peach. Berries. A mango eaten with juice running down your wrist. Do you need to learn to love that sweetness? Do you need to be paid to enjoy it? No. Your body recognises it without teaching. It settles. It satisfies.

BAD SUGAR is not that. BAD SUGAR is sweetness weaponised — stripped of fibre, structure and water, engineered to spike and then demand more. The food industry did not stumble on that. It tuned it. You were not weak. You were outgunned.

Another trap question: did you need a reward to eat a sweet when you were five, or did you need a sweet to feel rewarded because someone taught you sweets mean reward?

You know. The teaching came first. The need followed. That is not your nature. That is brainwashing. And brainwashing can be undone the moment it is seen.

You do not have a sweet tooth that dooms you. You have a sweet con that trained your tooth to ask for payola.

Would you teach that to a child you love now, knowing what you know? You would not. Offer the same protection to yourself.

### WOULD YOU REWARD SOMEONE YOU LOVE THIS WAY?

We are gentle with others and harsh with ourselves. So try this turn. It makes the payola visible without a trace of shame.

Picture someone you love. Your child, your partner, your dearest friend. They have had a hard day. They are tired, a little flat, a little deserving of kindness. You want to show them love.

Do you show them love by giving them a hit that will lift them for ten minutes and then leave them a little more tired, foggy and in need of another hit? Do you show love by handing them a packet that says "just a treat" while teaching them they need treats to feel loved?

You do not. You would give them rest. Warmth. Food that steadies. Time. Attention. Laughter. A walk. A bath. A cuddle. You would feed them, not the trap.

Why would you accept less for yourself? Why would you call for yourself love what you would never call love for them?

We learned to self-pay with BAD SUGAR because the world paid us that way as children and we never questioned the currency. We thought we were giving ourselves something. We were giving the Sugar Trap its fee and calling it self-kindness. The fee felt kind for a moment because the low paused. That pause is not kindness. Kindness does not create the need it relieves.

Think of the dearest reward you ever received that had nothing to do with BAD SUGAR. Praise that made you stand taller. A night's sleep that made you feel new. A bowl of fresh food after hunger that truly satisfied. A hug that said you belong. Did those need a hit to count? No. They counted because they were love itself, not payola for the trap.

You can still celebrate. Celebrate. You should. You did well. You got through. You won. Celebrate with what celebration actually is: company, music, light, rest, play, food that loves you back — fresh, favourite foods eaten freely without counting. BAD SUGAR does not make celebration sweeter. It borrows sweetness from the moment and then leaves the moment a little less sweet after.

Do you see the inversion? We thought BAD SUGAR added love to the moment. In truth the moment added love to BAD SUGAR, and we credited the wrong guest.

It was only ever sneaking a ride.

### FRIEND OR ENEMY — HOW WILL YOU FEEL WHEN IT'S GONE?

We need to set your emotional choice now, before the final chapters, so the trap cannot dress itself as nostalgia later.

I will preview a distinction we will use fully at the end. Keep it tenderly now.

Imagine two losses that look identical on the facts. In both, you no longer have BAD SUGAR hits. In one you feel you have lost a friend — your little love, your comfort, your treat. You mourn. You feel deprived. In the other you feel you have lost an enemy — the payola you paid to be kept tired and craving. You rejoice. You feel relief.

Same facts. Opposite emotions. Only one keeps you free. Mourning a friend keeps the Sugar Trap alive, because you still believe it was a genuine treat or lift. Celebrating a dead enemy kills it, because you see it never was.

Which will you choose? I invite you to choose celebration. Not because you should be tough, but because it is true. You are not losing a friend. You are losing a lifeguard who pushed you in.

Allow yourself to feel the warmth you were looking for — without the hit. Notice how much warmer it is when no rider steals the credit.

You do not need to fear that life without BAD SUGAR will be flat, grey, joyless. That fear is the trap talking, not your future. Ask the simple truth: did BAD SUGAR ever make your joy, or did it sneak a ride on joy that was already there? If it only ever snuck a ride, removing it does not remove joy. It reveals it.

Picture your next reward moment freely. You have finished something good. You are tired and pleased. You say, "I did well. I deserve kindness." You give yourself kindness — rest, light, laughter, company, a favourite fresh meal eaten until satisfied, a walk, a long exhale. No hit. No queue. No ten-minutes-later dip. The kindness stays. It settles. It does not ask for more.

Does that feel like deprivation? No. It feels like being cared for. By you.

You reached for BAD SUGAR because you wanted love and comfort and the world taught you that was where love lives. You were not weak. You were misdirected. Now you know where love lives: in the occasion, in your people, in your own true hunger and tiredness rightly answered, not in the packet that monetises them.

You have nothing to lose by seeing this. You have the rider to lose and the love to keep.

Let the last verse land gently then firmly. You deserved love. You still do. BAD SUGAR never was it. Your reward was never in the hit. Your reward was in you, your life, your people and your rest. Return the credit to its owners and the trap starves.

You were not loving yourself — you were feeding the trap. Now you can love yourself and be free to enjoy it.
