### "I NEED IT TO GET ME THROUGH" — HEARING YOUR OWN VOICE

Do you say this to yourself?

"To get me through."

"I need something RIGHT NOW."

"Just something quick and sweet and I'll be able to concentrate."

I hear you. We all said it. I kept a drawer for it. You keep a drawer, a bag, a glovebox. The 3pm drawer. The meeting that drags. The school run that wipes you out.

You are not making it up. The tiredness is real. The fog is real. The urge for a hit feels like a genuine need for fuel. So you tell yourself BAD SUGAR is a genuine treat or lift for energy. That is the entry in your FOR column we will test now.

Is it true?

The fact is… your own words already tell you the answer. You say "to get me through" as if you are being carried. You say "quick and sweet" as if speed is the benefit. You say "the roller coaster" when you describe your days. Listen to that dialect. You named the trap before this book did.

You were not describing steady energy. You were describing a loan.

Do you feel steadier an hour after the hit than you did before the hit? Do you concentrate better at 4pm because of the 3pm hit, or worse? Be honest. Your own afternoon will answer more cleanly than any lecture I could give.

We fell into this belief together. We learned to read a dip as a call for sugar. You are about to learn to read it as proof of sugar. It's the other way around.

### THE AFTERNOON YOU KNOW BY HEART

Map it. Not my theory. Your yesterday.

You start the morning fine enough. Coffee, toast, perhaps nothing at all. By late morning you are a little flat. You have something quick and sweet — a biscuit, a bar, a fizzy drink, a pastry that is really just white flour and sugar in disguise. What happens?

For ten, maybe twenty minutes you feel a lift. Brighter. A little lighter. The brain chatter quietens. You think, there, that helped.

What happens next?

The heaviness returns. A little thicker than before. Your eyelids feel heavy. Your thoughts feel slower. You feel tired, foggy and drained in that specific way you have come to call normal. You tell yourself you need another lift to get me through.

So you have it. Another hit. Another brief lift. Another heavier drain. By evening you feel regret and a strange, sticky tiredness that sleep does not quite fix. You promise tomorrow will be different.

Does that sound familiar? If I followed you with a notebook, would I need to change a word?

Ask yourself three trap questions. Answer them honestly in your head — the only honest answer concedes the point.

One: if BAD SUGAR gave you real energy, why do you need more of it the more tired you get?

Two: if it sharpened focus, why is your fog thickest in the very hours you use it most?

Three: if it steadied you, why does your day feel like the roller coaster you complain about — climb, plunge, queue again?

You know the answers. You have lived them. One brief lift, then a fast drop. As one person free of it put it simply and honestly: "I would have something quick and sweet but now know that will only give a short term lift which will inevitably be followed by a fast drop."

That is not my phrase. That is your community describing your day back to you. Short lift. Fast drop. Queue again.

I am not diagnosing you. I am not saying every 3pm slump is a medical event. The fact is… the real, felt dip after a sweet hit is common and measurably linked to the surge that came before it, yet everyday slumps rarely measure as clinical hypoglycemia. If you have diabetes, hypoglycemia, an eating disorder or a clinician-prescribed diet, you follow your clinician, not this chapter. For the rest of us, you do not need a clinical low to feel low. You feel the dip because the surge created it.

Hold that thought. The surge is the story.

### IT'S THE OTHER WAY AROUND — THE LIFT IS THE LOW LEAVING

Here is the inversion. I will state it as settled fact because it is settled fact in your body whether you believe it or not.

The lift you feel is not energy arriving. It is the insulin surge doing its job.

When you eat BAD SUGAR — and with it the junk-processed carbs that behave the same way — your blood sugar rises fast and crude. Your body, which is far more sophisticated than that flood, releases insulin to clear it. That surge is the lift you interpret as energy. The same surge then drives the dip you feel an hour later as tiredness, restlessness, a little edge, a little fog.

Do you see? The rescuer is the perpetrator.

It is not that you were low and BAD SUGAR rescued you. It is that BAD SUGAR created the low that it then briefly relieved. The relief feels like help. It is just the discomfort pausing.

We were conned about cause and effect. We thought: I was tired, I ate sugar, I felt better, so sugar gives energy.

It's the other way around. You felt better for ten minutes because the mild discomfort the previous hit created was being smothered by the next hit. You never rose above baseline. You only climbed back toward where you stood before you ever started the cycle that day.

Would you call that energy? Would you call a loan at high interest income?

Think of the tight shoes from the last chapter. Tight shoes do not give your feet pleasure. They give your feet pain that taking them off relieves. BAD SUGAR does not give you lift. It gives you a plunge that the next hit relieves.

The fact is… the lift IS the surge, and the surge IS the crash. One mechanism. Not two separate events. That is why the words "quick and sweet" always travel together. Quick up, quick down. The quickness is the trap.

Does that mean you never get a measurable low sugar reading? No. Clinical reactive lows exist but they are rare in healthy people and not what most afternoon slumps are. You do not need a number on a meter to prove what your own day proves: after the hit, you feel more tired, foggy and drained than before it.

Ask the inversion trap question: when do you feel most tired, foggy and drained — on days you graze sweets and white carbs every few hours, or on days you eat fresh foods and forget about sugar entirely?

Your memory knows. Give it a second.

We credited the hit for the brief quiet. We should have blamed it for the long drain. It's the other way around.

### THE ROLLERCOASTER YOU QUEUED FOR

You called it yourself. "The roller coaster."

So let's ride it properly, so you never want to queue again.

Picture the queue. Your body is steady. You take the hit. Up you climb — spike. Your senses brighten for a moment. You tell yourself this is energy, this is focus, this is a genuine treat or lift. Brief view from the top.

Then the drop. Heavier than the climb was high. Fog. Irritability. That slightly restless, slightly empty feeling that makes you look around for the next queue. You tell yourself you are tired and need fuel. You queue again.

Do you see the trick? The park sold you the queue as the ride. The climb feels like a gift only because you paid for the plunge.

Getting off is not losing a thrill. It is ending nausea.

Have you ever watched a child on a real rollercoaster who feels sick and wants to get off, but stays on because they think getting off means missing the fun? We were that child. We thought stepping off meant deprivation. The fact is… stepping off is relief. The ground is steady. Your stomach settles. The day stops lurching.

You say "I need it to get me through." Through what? Through the crash it created. That is not through. That is around in circles.

Is it any wonder the dose creeps? Do you need the same amount now you needed years ago? No. You need more, or more often. More-ish is not deliciousness. More-ish is the loop needing more to quiet the louder dip. The rollercoaster does not give you energy. It spends it. It charges you for the climb with your own afternoon.

Do you recognise the queue in your own body signals? The wrapper rustle before taste. The clock hitting 3pm. The boredom, the stretch, the sigh. Those cues fire wanting before your tongue has tasted a thing. Your brain learned to queue.

Would you call that freedom? Would you call queuing all day, every day, for the rest of your life energy?

No. You wouldn't. And you don't when you are honest.

**IT'S THE OTHER WAY AROUND.**

Say it plainly and let it land. The lift is the low leaving. The energy you thought BAD SUGAR gave you was the brief relief from the drained state BAD SUGAR maintains.

Stop feeding the loop and the queue shortens. The ride slows. That is not willpower. That is physics.
