Chapter 4
THE LIFT THAT STEALS THE AFTERNOON

It is ten past two and the rain has started against the office window.

You have closed the spreadsheet and opened the drawer. There is a bar in there, or the end of a packet, or the biscuits someone brought that you moved to your own space so you would not have to stand in the breakroom to get one. You did not put it there this morning because you love that exact flavour. You put it there because you know how the afternoon goes. The screen blurs a little. The shoulders ache. The mind slows over the same sentence twice. You tell yourself you know what this hour needs.

You eat it at the desk while the file loads. Sweet rush. A minute where the head feels clearer. Then the emails again. Then, by half past three, the heaviness back, thicker this time, with an edge under it. By four the head aches behind the eyes. By five you are back in the drawer, or at the machine, or thinking of what waits in the kitchen at home. You walked in this morning believing the drawer gets you to six. You leave in the evening knowing it took you twice and left you lower than it found you.

*The lift you feel is not strength arriving. It is the morning dose collecting its debt.*

IN THIS CHAPTER: the desk drawer at two, the drizzle on the glass, the headache at four, the roller coaster you already named, what the hours after the fix really hold

The Drawer That Gets You To Six

We all know this drawer. I knew mine to the corner where the wrapper stuck.

You will tell me straight, and I want you to tell me straight, because your objection is honest. "I need a pick-me-up." "I keep sugary somethings to get me through." "Without something sweet at three I cannot function. I cannot concentrate. My hands feel shaky and my brain stalls." You are not lying about the feeling. The tiredness at two is real. The flatness is real. The sense that something has gone out of you is real. I believed every word of it with my own hand in my own drawer. The question is not whether the low hour is real. The question is where the low hour came from.

Look at the hour itself without flinching.

You ate lunch. Real food, enough, two hours ago. A plate that would have held any labourer a century ago until supper without a thought of rescue. Yet at two you read your own state as shortage. Tired. Bored. A little hollow. Unable to say no to the sweet within reach. You remember that breakroom plate from yesterday. You ate it standing while reading the notice, and the tiredness walked back to the desk with you. The drawer today is the same plate with a different address. If flavour drove this eating, you would have chosen something worth tasting. You chose what was near. Nearness, not deliciousness, decided.

Ask what the dose did for the hour it was supposed to save.

For three minutes the mouth is busy and the mind has an event. The rush arrives and you call it energy. Then watch what follows, because the hours after are the witness no sweet talk survives. The head clouds. The temper shortens. The limbs feel heavy. Work that was dull at two is dull and effortful at four. You reach again, not because the first dose failed to please the tongue, but because the brightness it lent has gone and taken a little more with it. One writer who left the loop put it plainly, in words thousands recognise: a quick sweet gives a short lift which is inevitably followed by a fast drop. You know that shape in your own body. You have lived it daily.

Ask the sharper question. If BAD SUGAR gave fuel, why does the machine run worse after fuelling.

A car does not drive slower after petrol. A fire does not burn lower after coal. A body given true fuel does not ache for more fuel an hour later while feeling foggier than before. What you call a fuel behaves backwards. It lifts for minutes and charges for hours. It asks to be paid twice for work it never did. That backwards behaviour tells you the name is wrong. This is not a supply. This is a loan with interest collected in your own nerves.

I learned this at my own desk before I understood it. I kept the drawer stocked and called it planning. I told colleagues I worked better with something sweet at hand. The truth, seen later without anger, was plainer. I worked worse with it and blamed the afternoon. The afternoon was innocent. The drawer was billing me.

From here on in this book, when we speak of the supposed benefit, we will call it by one name only. We called it a treat, a fuel or a comfort, and today we try the middle word on its own ground.

Taste had its hearing. Fuel speaks now.

The Roller Coaster You Named Yourself

You already have the right image. I do not need to lend you one.

You call it the roller coaster. You talk about the sugar high and then the sugar crash as if they were two separate weathers that happen to you. The high lifts. The crash drops. The ride runs all day. Up at ten after the sweet tea. Down at eleven. Up at two after the bar. Down at four with the headache. Up after supper with the bowl. Down in the evening on the sofa, reaching again because down feels like need.

See the ride clearly for what it is.

A roller coaster is only fun to people who know they can get off. That is your own wisdom, not mine. You said it laughing once about your eating day, and you were righter than you knew. The climb feels exciting because the drop is coming. The drop feels terrible because the climb promised something. Bound together, climb and drop make one machine. You cannot buy the climb without the drop. The ticket for the lift is the crash.

Ask who built the ride.

Not your character. Not your week. Not your age or your job or your sleep last night, real as those are. The ride is the loop itself. Each dose spikes and then sags, and the sag reads to the brain as need more. You boarded years ago believing each climb was a gift. Seen from the platform, each climb was the start of the next fall. We were conned into calling the climb energy and the fall tiredness, as if they came from different places. They came from the same dose. The lift is the low wearing its morning face.

Isolate the variable and the trick shows.

Watch a child on a morning with no doses in him. He wakes, eats breakfast, runs, argues, builds, laughs, runs again. Nobody hands him a bar at ten to keep him going. Nobody fears he will stall by eleven without a sweet top-up. His energy comes and goes with movement and rest and food eaten as meals, and it returns after a pause without a packet. You were that child. Your body knew how to make a day without hourly rescue. Nothing in your design changed since. What changed is the daily drip that taught you to read every dip as shortage and every packet as supply.

Ask when the ride runs smoothest.

Not on the days you feed it well. Those are the wildest days, high to low to high, with the evening ending in the packet. The smoothest hours you have known lately were the hours when doses were far apart without you noticing. A busy morning that ran to one with no thought of sweet. A walk that cleared the head better than the bar ever did. A meal that closed and left you steady till supper. Smoothness came when the coaster slowed. That smoothness tells you the coaster was never carrying you. It was shaking you and selling the pauses between shakes as fuel.

It causes the dip. It does not cure it. It is the other way around.

What The Fix Does To The Hours After It

Let us follow one ordinary day through to the evening, because the fuel story dies in the hours after, never in the minute of the rush.

Two o clock. The dose. Sweet on the tongue. A brief clearing. You sit straighter. You think, there, that helped.

Three o clock. The clearing thins. The eyes sting. The same spreadsheet takes longer. A faint edge gathers behind good humour. You snap at a message you would have answered kindly at ten. You blame the work. You blame the rain. You blame the night before.

Four o clock. The head aches. Not fiercely. Dully, behind the eyes, with thirst. The mouth feels stale. The drawer looks inviting again. You tell yourself one more will fix what the first failed to hold. You eat again, a little faster this time, with less attention than the first. The rush is smaller. Everyone who lives on this ride knows the second lift of the day is smaller than the first. The hand keeps moving after pleasure has left. That overshoot tells you the driver was never pleasure and never power. It was the sag asking to be lifted off itself.

Evening. Supper eaten, but the sweet after supper still called, because the day of spikes never closes cleanly. You sit foggy, irritable and drained, and you call that state tiredness from life.

Read that triple slowly, because it is the permanent address of the hooked eater, not the exception. Foggy in the head so thinking feels like wading. Irritable in temper so small frictions spark. Drained in the limbs so the stairs feel longer. We arrive there most evenings and blame the job or the years. The job and the years are real, but they did not put that particular fog there. The ride put it there. Morning after morning the doses lift and drop, lift and drop, and evening collects the bill for all of them at once.

Ask what is happening in the body while the coaster runs.

When sweet hits in a rush, the body answers with its own surge to handle it. The lift you feel IS that answering at work. The same surge can overshoot and leave a real after-dip in the hours that follow. The dip is not imaginary. The mechanism for the afternoon sag after a strong sweet is biologically real. The body swings to meet the spike and the swing is felt as cloud and edge and hunger that was not there before the dose.

Read the honesty inside that sentence.

The everyday slump after sweets is the body swinging after the surge. It is not clinical low blood sugar disease. It is not proof you are broken or ill or built different. It is your own regulatory swing, doing its job after a dose it never asked for. Do not change from fear of the dip. The dip frightens only while the loop runs it daily. Once the daily spiking stops, the afternoons steady themselves on their own. The method takes care of it by removing the spike that ordered the swing.

Ask what this sorting proves about fuel.

If BAD SUGAR fuelled, the fuelled hours would be the best hours. Instead the fuelled hours are the foggiest. The clearest thinking you do all week is done far from the last dose, not close to it. The steadiest temper you show is shown on mornings when sweet is distant, not on afternoons when it is fresh in the blood. The strongest limbs you own are owned after real food and movement, not after the bar. Every honest watch reverses the label. The dose does plenty TO your afternoon. It does nothing FOR your strength. The hours after testify against the minute of the rush.

Tomorrow, run the watch that ends the argument.

At two, when the drawer calls, note the state before the dose in plain words. Tired. Flat. Edgy. Eat or do not eat as your day already holds, but keep your eyes open either way. At three, note again. At four, note again. Mark whether thought cleared or clouded. Mark whether temper sweetened or shortened. Mark whether limbs lightened or heavied. Mark whether the second reach came sooner or later than hunger for supper. One day of honest marks teaches more than my pages, because the marks are yours. You will see the lift go up a finger and the afternoon go down a hand. You will see the rescue arrive small and the bill arrive large. What is seen once in your own hours cannot be unseen.

The Body That Runs Its Own Supply Line

You will answer me, and I want you to answer, because your fear here is honest. Even if the afternoon sags, surely the body needs sugar for energy. Surely the brain runs on it. Surely without sweet coming in, the lights go out.

Hear the reassurance inside the correction, because this fear was installed, not born.

Your body runs its own glucose supply line from the food you already eat. Bread. Potatoes. Rice. Vegetables. Dairy. Fruit. Meat and fish where you eat them. The liver builds what the blood needs and holds it level through the day, the way a good kitchen keeps the house fed without hourly parcels. Glucose is not a parcel you must post one spoon at a time. Your body makes the level and keeps it. That is why the child runs till lunch without rescue, why the labourer worked till supper on plain meals, why you yourself think clearest far from the last dose. The supply was never the packet. The packet was shouting over the kitchen.

Ask what real fuel feels like, so the counterfeit cannot pass again.

Real fuel satisfies and then quietly holds. A real meal eaten seated, chewed, noticed to the close, leaves a clean finish and hours that do not ask for rescue. Hunger comes honestly before it and quiet lasts after it. The mouth feels fresh. The head feels usable. The temper holds. No second reach in an hour. No headache behind the eyes. No stale thirst. That quiet that lasts is the signature of true supply. You have felt it after suppers that closed well, after breakfasts that held to lunch without thought. Trust those hours. They were not accidents. They were your design showing.

Ask what the counterfeit does differently, every time.

The counterfeit excites and then empties. Rush, then sag. Bright minute, then fog. It never closes. It opens. It leaves the mouth stale and the head cloudy and the temper short, and it calls that emptiness need for itself. That is how you tell them apart without a laboratory. One closes and holds. The other opens and bills. BAD SUGAR, whatever its flavour, behaves the second way in the afternoon, daily, predictably. The behaviour is the confession.

I lived the confusion for years and called it low energy. I carried bars for energy the way a man carries a bucket for a leaking boat, never asking where the water came in. When doses thinned in a week of plain meals through illness, something curious happened that I did not arrange. Mornings held. Afternoons, though dull with illness, did not swing. The headache behind the eyes did not come. I had expected collapse without my fuel. What came was levelness without my robber. My liver had been on duty all along. I had been paying a thief to do the guard's job.

Which foods does the steady day stand on. Look without flinching.

The days that hold are built on meals, not on hits. Morning food that is eaten seated. Midday food that closes. Fruit in its skin. Vegetables with colour. Plain dairy. Grains and potatoes and real dishes cooked at home. Each closes and holds for hours. None spikes and bills. None calls a second reach in sixty minutes. None leaves you foggy, irritable and drained by five. When this book says sugar, it means BAD SUGAR, the built doses that spike, never the fruit or the meal that holds. Your meals stay. Your fruit stays. Your strength stays, because your strength never came from the packet.

Ask the trap question that closes this room.

If your body already makes its supply from your meals, what did the drawer add today except the swing. Name one hour it made stronger. Name one task it made clearer. Name one evening it made lighter. You cannot name the hour, because the drawer never made an hour. It borrowed the next hour to gild the minute. Seen once, the fuel stands exposed as the thief. The body was the supplier all along.

The Rescue That Was The Robbery

Put the pieces together and the picture turns back to front.

Morning. A dose spikes. The body surges to meet it. The surge overshoots. Mid-morning dips. You read the dip as need and dose again. Afternoon spikes. The body surges again. The surge overshoots again. The headache gathers. You read the headache as proof the day is hard and dose again. Evening sags under the day of swings. You read the sag as proof life drains you and you soothe it with the bowl that guarantees tomorrow opens low.

Each rescue was the next robbery. Each lift laid the next low. The friend who arrives at three with the hose set the fire at ten. You thanked him daily because you never saw him strike the match.

Ask who whispered the fuel story while the robbery ran.

That small gnaw of emptiness between doses is the Sugar Mite complaining as its feed thins. It is tiny. It is already dying once the daily spiking stops. It has no strength to hurt you. But while you believe its complaint means your body needs fuel, you answer a dying creature with a dose that revives it for another day. The voice that translates its gnaw into "you need energy" is older and louder. It speaks in your mother's tired voice and the advertisement's bright voice and your own weary voice at two. It says treat and fuel and rescue. It says you cannot cope without. Seen once for what it is, a con learned word by word over years, the voice loses its vote. The gnaw stays small. The story around it falls.

I know this is hard to accept while the drawer still feels like a friend. I found it hard when a wiser head first showed me. Keep your mind open a little longer, because your own afternoons will prove it faster than argument. Watch one day through. Watch the rush go small and the bill go large. Watch the clear hours gather far from doses. Watch the headache follow the spike, never the meal. Your eyes are better witnesses than your craving. The craving fires before the bite and calls itself knowledge. Knowledge arrives after the watch and answers back.

Do not change because the dip frightens you. Change because the fuel was never fuel. The dip is only the swing after the spike, and swings settle when spikes stop. No doom is required here. No bravery is required to notice an afternoon. What is required is honesty about which hours were ever strengthened. None were. The lift stole every afternoon it touched, and called the theft help.

You stand now where taste stood last time. Taste kept its bill and lost its purchase once hunger and rest and company took back their credit. Fuel keeps its rush and loses its office once the hours after take back their testimony. What good was there at all in the middle promise. A minute of brightness that billed three hours of fog. That is not supply. That is the loop feeding and calling the feeding strength.

4. NOTICE THE AFTERNOON.
Watch what the fix does to the hours after it.

Carry that watch into the rooms ahead, where the last promise still waits to speak. You will tell me, if neither flavour nor strength survives, perhaps something else remains. Perhaps the sweet pays you in another coin. We will hear that coin next, and weigh it the same way, hour by hour. Fuel has spoken now. The afternoon answered.

SUMMARY

- The drawer does not carry you to six. It borrows the afternoon to gild the minute.
- A quick sweet gives a short lift which is inevitably followed by a fast drop. The shape you feel daily is the loop, not your character.
- You named the ride yourself. The roller coaster climbs and drops as one machine, and it is only fun to people who know they can get off.
- The lift IS the body surging to meet the spike, and the same surge can leave a real after-dip. The dip is your regulatory swing, not disease and not proof you are built different.
- Evenings of fog, irritability and drainage collect the day of spikes into one bill. The state is the cost of the ride, not the cost of living.
- Your body runs its own supply line from your real meals. The packet never supplied strength. The kitchen was on duty all along.
- Real fuel closes and holds for hours. The counterfeit excites and empties in the hour. Behaviour tells them apart.
- Each rescue laid the next robbery. The dose causes the sag it claims to cure.
- Watch the hours after the fix. The afternoon testifies against the minute.
