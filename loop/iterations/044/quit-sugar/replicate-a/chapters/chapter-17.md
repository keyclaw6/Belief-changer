Chapter 17
THE LIST IN YOUR POCKET

IN THIS CHAPTER: the cleared table, the pocket list, the dead enemy.

*You have not lost a friend at that table. You have buried an enemy.*

THE CLEARED TABLE

The kitchen is clear and the morning light is on the bare wood. Plates washed. Cups up. The envelope list from Saturday still pinned to the fridge with the week's meals crossed through. You tear off a clean corner and copy something small onto it and fold it and put it in your pocket. Not because you need to study it. Because you like knowing it is there.

Compare it with the table as it was. Night. One plate ringed. Wrappers pushed aside for morning-you to find. The chair still out where you left it in a hurry. That table accused you without a word, and you cleared it fast before the house woke, as if tidiness could undo the evening. This table accuses nothing. Same wood. Same fridge hum. Same light switch under your hand. Only the evening behind it different — a meal eaten and closed, plates washed at once, no second shift after everyone slept. You stand at it a moment longer than the errand requires, not to admire yourself, but because there is nowhere else you need to be to prove anything. That ease is what carrying starts from.

We all reach this morning in the same way. We came to this book eating sweet doses every day and calling them just a treat. We called the lift fuel and the payment reward and the numbness comfort. We ate standing at breakroom plates and in dark kitchens where no one could hear, and we called that choice. It was never choice. It was the Bad Sugar trap doing its quiet work, dose calling to dose, each fix borrowing steadiness from the hours after it and sending us back for another borrow.

Look at what you see now that you could not see then.

You see that the afternoon heaviness was never a gap in you that sweet could fill. It was the dip after the sweet. You see that the reward never paid you. It charged you a second price after twenty minutes and called the charge yours. You see that the comfort never soothed anything. It muted the evening and left the worry exactly where it found it, with a film on the teeth added. The rescue was the perpetrator. That one seeing undoes the whole con.

If the old thought knocks this morning, let it knock. It will knock. A picture of icing. A smell of vanilla from somewhere. A voice saying once a week is fine, special occasions, you have been good. Hear it for what it is. It is an echo of years of mornings, not a message from the body. Your body runs its own supply line from the food you already eat. It does not ask for a sweet hit to light it. Ask the thought its one honest question:

Did that dose ever once carry you past the afternoon without calling for another? Did it ever close an evening clean?

There is only one honest answer and you carry it now. There is nothing to give up.

Carry that seeing into the ordinary hours and watch how they confirm it without any effort from you.

This afternoon at three the kettle will boil as it always boiled. You will stretch while the water heats and pour tea and carry it back to the desk. The work will move. The light will change. At dusk you will look up and notice you forgot the entire ceremony of drawer and coins and debate, and the forgetting will please you. It is not carelessness. It is the seeing holding. The break was always the rest. The biscuit beside it only stole the rest and sold it back as rescue.

Saturday the shop will stand exactly as it stood. The bright wall will shine. Vanilla and cardboard on the air. Ten years of evenings taught us to read that brightness as Friday and deserved. We read it now as manufacture, built to a measured liking peak and carried to every till so no day could pass without an offer. Walk past it the way you pass the wrong door in a corridor. Take your tomatoes and your rice and your apples. Pay. Come home to the lit kitchen and cook what your house eats and close the meal clean. Fruit will speak at its own volume again. An orange eats loud and clean. You had only forgotten because the shout drowned flavour.

And the party will come, with the table laughing in the middle of the family and the pudding set down to a small cheer. Sit in that brightness with your roast and your tea and your fruit. Notice how the talk thins while the chewing gets louder around the spoon. Notice the heaviness arriving after the plates are cleared while the evening is still young. Then lift your eyes to what was bright all along without it. You will find you danced through the course and remembered the figs late, because the evening outshone the dish. Pity the eaters at that table; never envy them. They are still inside it. You eat beside them, free, with nothing missing that was ever precious.

THE POCKET LIST

I promised you at the start that leaving would be easy, and I promised that you would only ever have to do one thing. Here is the whole of it, small enough to carry. Photograph it if you wish. Keep it in your pocket until the pocket wears through. You will find you stopped unfolding it long before that.

Read it once slowly at this clear table. Each line is a spoken order you already obeyed on the way here. They are not rules to hold with effort. They are what a free reader does without thinking, written down so the mind never mistakes noise for hunger again.

Keep it folded where you keep coins and tickets — back pocket, purse flap, phone case. Photograph it too, so it rides with the shopping pictures and the children's faces, one swipe down when the knock comes loud in a queue or at a table where you cannot unfold paper politely. When the knock comes — icing picture, vanilla smell, the old once-a-week voice — you do not argue with it and you do not test yourself against it. You take out the list, you read the lines you already lived, you put it back, and you turn back to the meal, the work, the people before you. Thirty seconds. No debate reopened. No question relitigated.

At first you will unfold it often, then only to show a friend where you keep it, then only when you find it while looking for something else and smile before you put it back. The paper softens at the folds. The ink pales at the crease. The photograph sinks down the roll under newer pictures. That fading is not neglect. It is the weight going out of it. What needed writing to hold now holds itself, and what you carried to remember you now carry because you like knowing it is there. The table stays clear in the morning because the evening stayed clear, and the evening stays clear because the seeing stayed clear, and the list in your pocket is only the seeing made small enough to touch.

If you have turned to these last pages first, hoping to carry the list without the walk, put the list back in your pocket unread and start at the first page. The list without the seeing is only words. The seeing makes the list weightless. Read with an open mind and let each page do its work before the last ordinary meal.

MOURNING OR REJOICING

Two houses in one street hear the same news on the same morning. In the first house a dear friend has died in the night. The curtains stay drawn. The table is laid with one place missing and the missing place aches. In the second house a cruel enemy has died in the night, an enemy who stole money from the drawer for years and told lies about everyone in the house. The curtains are thrown wide. The same table is laid with one place missing and the missing place sings.

Same absence. Opposite emotion. The feeling never lived in the empty chair. It lived in what the missing one did while alive.

Hold that picture beside your own table tonight. Count what the dead enemy took while it lived, and count it coldly, the way you would count a thief's takings once the thief is caught.

It took money by the week and health by the year and teeth that never grew back. It took steady afternoons and turned them into foggy, irritable hours that needed another dose to face. It took evenings and narrowed them to the cupboard. It took the taste of real food and dulled it under a filter of comparison, so apples went quiet and only the shout registered. It took our pride and told us the failure was character, when the failure belonged to the wrong method we were never warned against. It took nothing in return that was real. The lift always needed repeating by four. The love-talk always needed eating where no one could see.

For years the evening dessert sat in the middle of us like a friend. Warm light. Laughter leaning in. The spoon lifted as if love were edible. We believed the brightness came from the dish. We have watched it closely now and we know where the brightness lived. It lived in the people, the leisure, the story told wrong, the music low, the chair holding us. The dose was only sneaking a ride on the evening and charging us the night after.

The empty dessert plate is not an absence at the table. It is proof the host is finally home.

So do not mourn that plate. Rejoice at it. When BAD SUGAR crosses your mind at a party, in an aisle, at three o'clock beside a kettle going cool, answer at once with the correct description of what happened and turn back to the meal, the work, the people before you. Each answering makes the next knock quieter. The thought will come seldomer. The answer will come gladder.

That answer is the last line of this book and the first line of everything after it. Keep it for the rest of your days. Whenever the old picture rises, think it with joy and mean it.

Go and enjoy your life.
