Chapter 14
FIVE YEARS OFF, THREE DAYS BACK

You have walked with me through every room of this argument, and you carry no exception now. No birthday unexamined. No Friday unopened. No drawer of folded sheets left unread. The talk that once spoke in your own accent has been heard plainly for what it was, and the shelf of Monday restarts stands behind you, not above you.

There is one thing left to hear before your own vow, and it is not another argument from me.

I asked a woman who stepped clear of BAD SUGAR five years ago to write down exactly what happened afterwards, in plain order, without polishing and without preaching. She keeps no clinic. She sells nothing. She works the front desk at a dental practice in Leeds, she is forty-one, she has two boys, and she ate sweet things every day for nineteen years. What follows is her account, printed as she sent it, cut only where she repeated herself. Read it slowly. Then we take your last ordinary meal together.

Her account follows in her own words.

THE BIRTHDAY VOW

I took my vow on my thirty-eighth birthday, at half past seven in the evening, sitting at my own table with gravy still on my plate.

That matters to me, so I set it down first. I did not do it at midnight. I did not do it standing in a kitchen full of shame with wrappers up my sleeves. I did it after an ordinary dinner I had cooked myself. Roast chicken. Potatoes roasted hard the way my husband likes them. Greens. Raspberries and plain yoghurt after, because the boys wanted theirs with honey and I put the honey jug back in the cupboard without a speech and ate mine plain. The table was loud. My youngest had gravy on his cuff. My mother rang during pudding and sang one verse down the phone while we all laughed.

Nobody at that table knew what I had decided, and that was how I wanted it. I had told no one I was reading a book about BAD SUGAR. I had kept eating exactly as normal while I read, the biscuits with morning tea, the chocolate from the drawer at work, the pudding when pudding came. That had felt strange at first, reading about the loop with a bar in my bag, and then it felt honest, because I was looking at the thing as it was rather than through a week of hunger and white-knuckling.

When the plates were cleared and the boys were fighting over whose turn it was to choose the programme, I stood at the sink with my hands in hot water and I said it to myself, silently, the way you say the truest things. That this dose was finished. That the evening needed no apology and no encore. That I would eat my real meals with appetite and close them clean, and BAD SUGAR would not be coming with me.

I did not cry. I felt a small click, like a latch finding its plate. I dried my hands, sat between my boys on the sofa, and watched a quiz show I cannot now name. At half past ten I ate nothing, because supper had closed the day, and I went to bed.

THE FIRST WEEK

The first two days were so ordinary I kept checking I had done it right.

I ate breakfast because I was hungry. Toast and eggs on Tuesday, porridge with sliced apple on Wednesday. Lunch at my desk at half past twelve, soup and bread and an orange, and I noticed the orange the way you notice a person who has been speaking quietly in a loud room for years. Sweet, but not shouting. On Tuesday afternoon at the old hour I stood up, walked to the drawer where the bars lived, opened it, looked at them, and closed it again. Not with a clench. With puzzlement, the way you open the wrong door in a corridor. The bars were still there on Friday. I put them in the food bank tin the following Monday, unopened, and thought no more about them.

Day three brought the murmur I had been warned might come, and I set it down exactly so no one reading this invents a worse version.

I woke well. By eleven my head felt dull behind the eyes, the way it used to feel before a cold arrived. At two o'clock there was a hollow ache under my ribs, a gnawing emptiness that lunch had not removed. I recognised it at once, the little physical complaint unfed, and I did something I had never done in nineteen years of sweet eating. I welcomed it. I poured tea, I carried it to the window, I watched rain move across the car park, and I thought, there it is, the feeder going hungry, and I am eating my meals and it is starving without me. The ache stayed perhaps an hour. It returned after supper, fainter. I went to bed at half past nine and slept like a stone dropped in a pond.

Nights four and five brought the dreams, and I include them because no one warns you and they are comic once you know.

On the fourth night I dreamed the office had installed a vending machine in my bedroom and I could not get past it to reach my alarm. On the fifth I dreamed my youngest had filled my handbag with wrapped toffees and they kept multiplying whenever I threw them away until the bag weighed more than the boy. I woke laughing both times, my mouth dry, the sheets kicked off. By daylight the dreams thinned to nothing. They have never come back.

What did come back all that week was tiredness with a texture I knew well. At four o'clock I wanted to lie down for ten minutes. The first two afternoons I did exactly that on my break, coat over me, timer set, face in the crook of my arm. On the third afternoon I did not need it. I stood at the window in thin November sun with my tea going warm in my hand. I tell you the coat and the timer because someone needs to hear that the tired days are days, not a sentence. Mine lasted three afternoons. Then the afternoons stood up on their own.

ABOUT THREE WEEKS IN

About three weeks into it, something curious happened to me, and I give you my dates so you hear it as one woman's journal and not as anyone's promise to you.

On the Tuesday of the third week I poured tea at three, carried a file to the back office, worked steady through a backlog of appointment letters, and at twenty to six I looked up and realised I had forgotten the entire afternoon ceremony. No drawer. No coins for the machine. No debate with myself that I always lost and then called character. The hour had passed while I was busy with names and phone numbers, and my mouth had never raised its hand.

I sat there with my pen still in my fingers and nearly laughed out loud, because the old afternoon roller coaster had simply not arrived at the station, and I had not stood on the platform waiting for it.

The same thing happened on the Thursday, and then the Friday. I do not mean I felt noble. I mean I felt nothing in particular, which was the novelty. The letters got written. The tea went cold and I drank it cold. A colleague left a paper plate of iced buns by the kettle at four and I looked at them the way you look at someone else's post, addressed elsewhere, of no interest, and went back to my screen. That plate would once have decided my evening. That Friday it did not even decide my minute.

My evenings that week tell the rest. I cooked, we ate, the meal closed, a door gently closed because the room was finished. Before, a second hunger stitched after supper sent me back to the cupboard while the programmes played unwatched. That stitching came undone that week. I knitted half a sleeve for my sister's baby. I read sixty pages of a library novel. My hours came back to me in one piece.

If you are waiting for the catch, here it is, and it is a small one. I still thought of BAD SUGAR every day that week. The thought came at the old hours, wearing the old clothes. Biscuit o'clock. Pudding o'clock. The difference was what the thought did when it arrived. Before, the thought gave orders and I obeyed and called the obedience mine. That week the thought knocked and I answered it with the plain recognition that the voice was the old talk and its vote was spent, and the thought left. Each leaving made the next knock quieter. By the Sunday I had to reach back to remember whether the knock had come at all.

THE GRANOLA

In my sixth week I learned what my tongue had become, and I tell this story as a warning and never as an errand, because no one should go tasting on purpose to prove anything.

A rep had left a box of honey-coated granola clusters in the staff room, open, with a little plastic scoop in it. I had eaten that exact kind for years, by the handful, between tasks. That morning I walked past, took a small handful without deciding to, the way the hand remembers a handle, and put one cluster in my mouth.

It was shocking. It tasted far too sweet, sugar over everything, the grain buried under it, the honey a shout. I chewed twice and stopped. I stood there with the second cluster in my palm and did not want it. I tipped the handful back into the box, wiped my palm, drank water, and the sweetness sat on my teeth like a film.

An hour later the slump came, the old sick dip I had forgotten in six clear weeks, and I dozed at my desk after lunch with my cheek on my hand for twenty minutes. I woke thick-headed, and the first clean thought I had was that the treat I used to eat by the bag had stopped being a treat while I was away from it. My mouth had recalibrated. What I had called flavour for years had been the shout drowning the flavour.

I finished the day on soup and bread and apples. By the next morning the film was gone and the steadiness was back. I set this down precisely because someone will read it and hear permission to go testing. Hear the opposite. I did not plan it, I stopped after mouthfuls, and I count the afternoon doze as a kindness, a reminder served once. The vow I spoke at my sink was the last ordinary instance, and I have never staged another. If your tongue wants evidence, lend it mine. It tasted too sweet, it dragged the afternoon down, and I left the box for the staff room and never reached again.

THE QUIET AISLE

Some time in my fourth month I noticed the advertisements had lost their vote, and I mean the actual rectangles on the actual television.

There is a chocolate advert with a slow pour that used to put my hand in my bag before my mind had voted. It came on during a Sunday film and I felt nothing answer in my mouth. My younger boy asked for some, my husband offered apples and cheese for tomorrow's walk, the boy agreed without a war, and the film went on.

The weekly shop told the same story in plainer ink.

I shop on Saturday mornings with a list on an envelope. Tinned tomatoes. Rice. Chicken thighs. Apples. Plain yoghurt. Cheese. Tea. Soap. For nineteen years that list had a second list underneath it, unwritten, which I obeyed all the same. Bars for the drawer. Biscuits for the tin. Ice cream for Friday. Something for the nights. That Saturday in February, sleet on the windscreens outside, I walked the long aisle to fetch tomatoes and passed the whole shining wall of confectionery without slowing. A mother two trolleys down negotiating with a small girl over a multipack, the girl pulling, the mother tired, and I felt a pang I will not dress up. I had held that negotiation in this same aisle a hundred times and paid the ransom and called it love.

My trolley held tomatoes and rice and chicken and apples. I reached the end of the aisle and did not go back. At the till the little shelves of bars stood beside the gum, bright as toys, and I looked at my envelope, counted the items, and paid. Outside the sleet had turned to rain. I loaded the bags into the boot and stood a moment with rain on my face and the receipt going soft in my fist, and what I felt was not triumph. It was ordinariness. Shopping as shopping. An errand that had stopped charging me a second price.

That ordinariness widened all that first year. Mornings arrived clear and stayed clear. Meals closed clean. Fruit, eaten for years through a filter of comparison with the chocolate just gone, came forward in its own right. I had forgotten pears had perfume, and I leave that sentence standing because someone reading this has forgotten it too.

YEARS TWO TO FIVE

I will not pretend every month since has been sunlight. It has been life, which is better.

My father died in my second year. I cooked and I cried and I answered phone calls and I ate my meals because the meals held the days together. Grief did not ask for BAD SUGAR and I did not offer it. What I wanted was sleep and my mother's hand and the lit kitchen of my sister's house, and those wants were honest and I honoured them. The old talk visited once in that season, on a wet Thursday after the funeral, suggesting a bar would soften the drive home. I heard it, named it for the old voice it was, drove home through the rain with the heater loud, and made toast when I got in. The toast did not mend anything. Nothing edible could. That was the whole lesson, and it stayed learned.

Work changed. The practice moved premises. My eldest started secondary school. I danced at my cousin's wedding through the pudding course, plate untouched before me, because the band was good and the evening outshone the course. The spoon was never the brightness. The brightness was the people and the music, and it is all mine without paying the second price after. My teeth no longer fur at four o'clock the way they did in the years of the daily drip. Afternoons run level to dusk. I changed nothing else about my life to get it. Same desk. Same bus. Same envelope lists.

THREE DAYS BACK

I promised this account without polishing, so here is the unpolished page, from my third year, and the reason for this chapter's title.

It was December. My father-in-law was in hospital, my shifts had doubled, the boys were loud with tiredness, and I slept four broken hours a night for a week. On the Saturday before Christmas I wrapped presents till midnight, came downstairs for tape, saw the children's selection boxes stacked by the tree, and opened one. I ate the contents across an hour standing in the cold sitting room with the tree lights blinking, bars and coins and a foil animal I did not even like, followed by biscuits from the tin. I ate fast and secretively, the way I had eaten for nineteen years.

I woke sticky-mouthed and heavy-headed, and the old voice was waiting with its old script. You have blown it. You were always going to. Book the restart for January and enjoy the week.

Here is what clear seeing had built in me. I did not argue with the voice. I did not bargain it down to weekdays or to January. I recognised it. The slip had revived nothing. The body copes with an accidental stumble. It is the deliberate exception, nursed back into permission, that reopens the loop. I guarded the belief and the behaviour followed.

I ate an ordinary breakfast. Porridge. Apple. Tea. I told my husband what I had done, briefly, without theatre, and he said, rotten week, love, and put his hand on my neck a moment, and that was the whole confession and the whole absolution.

Day one after was loud. The gnaw knocked hard at the old hours, and the old thoughts queued with it. I gave the remaining boxes to the neighbour's teenagers that same morning, ate my meals, went to bed early.

Day two was quieter. One knock at three, one after supper, both recognised, both passing like buses I did not need.

Day three was silent. I poured tea at three, I worked, I looked up at dusk, and the steadiness was there again as if it had never left, because it never had. The belief had held. Only the noise had visited.

I have had one such December since, smaller, corrected by the second morning. I do not count them as evidence against me. I count them as the seatbelt doing its office. A stumble warns. It does not license, and it does not erase. What erases is letting the old talk back in to rename the stumble permission. I did not let it in. That is the entire difference between the woman writing this and the girl who once hid wrappers deep so the house would not hear, and it is not a difference of character. It is a difference of sight.

That is my five years, and my three days back inside them. The days get easier, not harder. The knocks get rarer and thinner. The table stays bright.

You have walked my weeks with me, the vow at the sink, the forgotten afternoons, the too-sweet mouthful, the shining aisle, the December. The days after the vow are days, not a corridor. Murmur, then quiet. Knock, then silence. Stumble, then steadiness by day three.

Yours will have its own dates and its own weather. Keep your real meals. Keep your envelope lists. Keep your evenings whole. When the old voice comes in your own accent with its small offer, answer it with its name and turn back to the meal before you. You know what it is now. You know what it is not.

The next room is your own last ordinary meal, and your own solemn vow spoken over it. Bring everything you have seen here with you to that table. I will not be there, but women like me will be standing at sinks and desks and tills all over the country, eating our roast and our apples, pouring our tea at three, closing our meals clean. Come and join us. The table is long, and there is room, and the light stays on late.
