Chapter 3
WHAT GOOD IS THERE AT ALL

It is twenty past two and you are standing in the breakroom with a paper plate in your hand.

You did not plan this plate. You came in for water. You came in to stretch your legs between emails. The box sits open on the counter where someone left it that morning. The cake inside is pale at the edges. The icing has formed a thin skin. A plastic fork lies across the top with crumbs stuck to it. Nobody is watching. Nobody offered it to you with love. You take a corner piece because it is there.

The first bite is sweet. The second bite is sweet in the same way. By the fourth bite you are chewing while reading the notice pinned above the kettle. You finish it standing. You fold the plate in half and push it deep into the bin. You walk back to your desk and the tiredness you carried in here walks back with you.

*If that cake tasted so good, why were you reading the notice while you ate it.*

IN THIS CHAPTER: the breakroom plate, the first bites and the rest of the packet, what taste is really made of, the haze that dulls the strawberry

The Bargain We Think We Are Making

Until now we have weighed two piles against each other. On one side the cost. Teeth. Mornings. Afternoons that sag. Clothes a little tighter than last year. On the other side the pleasure. The flavour. The lift of the sweet on the tongue. We say to ourselves, yes, there is harm in it, but surely there is good in it too, and surely a sensible person balances the two.

I want to set that scale aside, not because the cost is small, but because the scale itself is the wrong instrument.

A poisoned meal and a fine evening are not balanced against each other. The evening was never inside the meal. Ask a different question and the whole room changes shape. Not does the harm outweigh the good. But what good is there at all.

I know how strange that sounds when you love the sweet. You tell me dessert is the best part of every meal. You tell me I L.O.V.E sugar and you spell it out because plain letters cannot hold it. You tell me just a treat as if the smallness of the word proves the bigness of the pleasure. I hear you. I said all of it with my hand in the packet. I am not asking you to deny what your tongue felt. I am asking you to look again at where that feeling came from.

From here on in this book, when we speak of the supposed benefit, we will call it by one name only. Not pleasure in general. Not joy. Not happiness. But a treat, a fuel or a comfort. That is the entire claimed gift in one object. Three promises sold as one. In this chapter we take the first of the three and hold it to the light. The taste. The flavour. The supposed goodness on the tongue. The other two will speak in their turn. Taste speaks now.

BAD SUGAR does plenty TO you. It does nothing FOR you.

Read that again slowly, because it is the hinge this chapter turns on. Plenty has been done TO your teeth and your afternoons and your evenings by doses you barely noticed. Nothing was done FOR your tongue that the tongue could not have had without the dose. If that sentence is true, the bargain collapses. There is no bargain. There is only a bill with no purchase.

The Plate Nobody Chose

Let us stay with that breakroom plate, because it is the most honest witness we have.

Think of the cake itself. It was not very good cake. You would know good cake. Good cake is warm from the oven and eaten seated with people you like, with coffee that is hot and talk that runs over. This was not that. This was yesterday's sponge left under strip lights, cut into squares by someone you barely know, drying at the corners, icing too sweet and base too bland. If a friend had asked you that morning, do you fancy dry leftover cake at half past two, you would have laughed. You fancy good things. You have standards. You cook meals your family asks for twice.

Yet at half past two you ate it. You ate it standing. You ate it quickly. You ate it while thinking of something else.

Ask why.

Were you hungry in the honest sense. Had you missed lunch. Was your stomach growling for food. No. You had eaten lunch two hours before. Real food. A plate that closed. The hunger that points at food and then quiets when food arrives was quiet. Something else was loud. Tiredness. Boredom. The flat hour when the screen blurs and the shoulders ache and the mind wants a small event to break the stretch until home.

Ask what the cake did for that hour. Did the tiredness lift. Did the boredom clear. Did the last two hours of the working day turn bright and easy. You know they did not. You walked back to the desk with the same heaviness plus a sticky mouth. The plate went to the bin. The hour stayed.

Ask the sharper question. If taste drove that eating, why eat what does not taste good. A lover of music does not stand in a corridor listening to a broken radio. A lover of pictures does not stare at a blank wall. Only the eater of BAD SUGAR tells himself he eats for flavour and then eats flavour he would not serve to a guest.

I have stood where you stand. I tell you this as a man who ate the broken radio many times. I ate the square from the box because someone had left it. I ate the biscuit from the tin because the tin was open. I ate the end of the children's ice cream because the freezer asked. None of those moments began with desire for that exact flavour. Each began with the hour. The tired hour. The dull hour. The faintly hollow hour. The sweet was near, so the hour got a sweet name.

Pity that plate. Do not pity yourself for taking it, and do not blame the kind colleague who left it. The colleague did not set the trap. The plate is what the shelves have made normal. Sweet within reach at every hour, sweet as the answer to every dip. Seen once, the plate testifies against the taste story. Mediocre sweets are eaten for the slump, not for the tongue.

The First Bites And The Rest Of The Packet

You will answer me, and I want you to answer, because your objection is honest. But what about the good ones. Not the dry square. The real ones. The molten dessert. The thick chocolate. The fresh bakery bun still warm in the bag. Surely those prove the pleasure is real.

Let us look at those too, closely, bite by bite.

Take the packet you love most. Name it to yourself. The bar. The biscuits. The tub. Now play the last time you had it, from the first bite to the last, without skipping.

The first bite. Yes. Sweet rush. Cold or warm or crunch, exactly as remembered. Attention narrows. The mouth fills. For three seconds the world goes quiet.

The second bite. Still good. A little less bright. Attention already thinner. You notice the television again. You notice the wrapper sound.

The third and fourth. Chewing continues. The flavour is still there in theory, but you are no longer tasting it in practice. You are munching. The hand moves. The mouth moves. The mind has left the tongue and gone to the evening, the bills, tomorrow.

The rest. Eaten. All of it. Licked clean or crumbed empty. And then what. A fullness that is not satisfaction. A mouth that wants water. A faint wish you had stopped earlier. A wrapper pushed deep so the house will not count.

Ask what that sequence proves.

If BAD SUGAR gave genuine flavour pleasure, the pleasure would grow with attention, the way music grows when you listen harder, the way a fine meal grows when you sit longer. Instead the pleasure shrinks with attention. The first bite carries almost all of it. The rest carries motion. We do not savour the packet. We administer it.

Ask a second question about that first bite. What made it heavenly. Was it the cocoa. Was it the sugar crystals. Or was it the hours since the last dose.

Think of thirst. Cold water after a long walk tastes like the finest drink on earth. The same water from the same tap, drunk when you are not thirsty, tastes of nothing. Nobody claims the tap is delicious. We understand the thirst made the pleasure. The drink gets the credit the walk earned.

BAD SUGAR works the same trick in reverse. The hours since the last dose build a small emptiness. Tiredness gathers. The mouth feels flat. Then the dose arrives and the emptiness ends for a minute. Relief floods in and we call the relief flavour. The first bite feels heavenly because it ends a low the last dose created. The rest of the packet, eaten on top of relief, has nowhere to rise to, so it runs on as motion.

Isolate the variable and the trick shows. Taste without tiredness. Sweetness without the low hour. Offer the beloved bar to a mouth that has just finished a real meal it loved, seated, unhurried, satisfied to the close. How does the first square taste then. Too much. Too sweet. Clogging rather than charming. Offer the same bar at half past four after no lunch and a hard morning, and it tastes like rescue. The bar did not change. The gap changed. The gap is doing the tasting.

Ask when you last ate one and stopped glad. Just one square. Just one biscuit. You cannot name the date, because one does not complete. One starts. Anyone who ate for flavour could stop at flavour satisfied, the way a person stops at one fine peach. The eater of BAD SUGAR eats past flavour into numbness. That overshoot tells you the driver was never the tongue.

What Taste Is Really Made Of

If the sweet itself does not explain the pleasure, what does. Three honest sources have been lending their credit to the dose for years. Hunger. Rest. Company. Each is real. Each is good. Each has been letting BAD SUGAR sign its name.

Take hunger first.

Real hunger makes everything taste brighter. A plain roll after honest work tastes wonderful. Soup tastes wonderful. An apple tastes loud and wet and sharp. The body, asked clearly, answers clearly. It points at food, eats seated, notices the middle where the edge comes off, and closes at enough with a quiet that lasts.

BAD SUGAR has been arriving at the edge of that hunger and taking the bow. You come home hollow at six. Supper is twenty minutes off. A biscuit goes in while the pots steam. The hollow eases. You say the biscuit tasted marvellous. Ask what tasted marvellous. The biscuit. Or the easing of the hollow that supper would have eased better in twenty minutes and held eased for hours.

I learned this at my own table. I used to load the mouth while cooking and call it tasting. A square while the onions softened. A spoon while the rice boiled. Then supper itself tasted flat, and I blamed the recipe. The recipe was innocent. I had spent the hunger on doses and then asked the meal to please a spent mouth. When I let supper arrive to real hunger, the meal sang without help. Vegetables with colour still in them. Potatoes. Plain dairy. Fruit after. Each had flavour I had forgotten while the sweet shouted over it.

Take rest second.

The break wants a break, not a biscuit. Read that plainly. At three in the afternoon the eyes sting and the back aches and the mind slows. What the body asks for is pause. Stand. Stretch. Air at the window. Kettle on. Minutes while the water boils to breathe and look out. Tea poured hot and drunk slowly.

BAD SUGAR has been sneaking a ride on that pause. You take the biscuit with the tea and credit the biscuit for the relief the pause gave. Ask what the pause would have done alone. Every office worker knows the answer who has ever walked to the window empty-handed and felt the head clear. The legs moving. The eyes resting. The small kindness of stopping. That relief is real and it belongs to rest. The dose added nothing to it except a sticky finish and a shorter fuse to the next dip.

Take company third.

Sweet tastes sweetest among people we love, and we conclude the sweet made the sweetness. The birthday table. The cinema with friends. The kitchen where the children laugh with sticky chins. Warm light. Easy talk. Leisure earned. Of course the dessert placed in the middle of that warmth glows. Everything in the middle of warmth glows.

Ask what glowed. Set the same dessert alone under strip lights at half past two, eaten standing while reading a notice. Does it glow. You know it does not. The glow was the people. The leisure. The evening. The laughter that would have warmed plain fruit and warm bread just as well. BAD SUGAR sat in the middle of love and took love's photograph as its own.

The evening was full, and it would have been full without the dose. The people were the flavour. The dose sat in the middle of love and took love's photograph as its own.

Separate the strands once and they stay separate. Hunger pleases. Rest restores. People warm. BAD SUGAR, isolated from all three, eaten alone, untired, unhungry, unloved by occasion, reveals its own thinness. Dry crumb. Skin of icing. Sweet that cloys by the fourth bite. That thinness is the truth the libations of hunger and rest and love have been hiding.

The Haze That Dulls The Strawberry

There is a second witness, quieter than the breakroom plate, and it testifies from the other direction. Not that BAD SUGAR tastes of little alone, but that it makes everything else taste of less.

Think of the week you ate doses daily. Morning sweet in coffee. Midday biscuit. Afternoon bar. Evening bowl. Now think of the fruit in that same week. The apple in the bag. The grapes in the fridge. The orange you meant to have. How did they taste. Dull. Watery. Hard work. Nice for other people, you said, but not for me. I need something with flavour.

Ask whether the fruit changed. The orchard did not alter its work that week. The apple was the same apple. What changed was the mouth that met it.

Heavy sweetness lays a haze over the tongue. Each dose sets the bar higher, so the next thing must shout to be heard. After chocolate, an apple whispers. After icing, a strawberry tastes sour. After the daily drip of strong sweet, plain food reads as bland, not because it is bland, but because the mouth is judging everything against the shout just eaten.

Every cook knows the plain version of this. After a spoon of honey, tea without honey tastes empty for a minute. The tea did not thin. The mouth is comparing. Stretch that minute across weeks of doses and you have the eater who says real food bores him. He is not reporting on food. He is reporting on the filter.

I lived inside that filter for years and called it character. I said I had a sweet tooth, as if teeth voted. I said savoury was fine but pudding was the point. I passed the fruit bowl without seeing it. Then, in a week when doses thinned because illness kept me to soups and bread and fruit, something curious happened that I did not arrange. The apple on the third day cracked loud. Juice ran. Sweetness arrived, not the shout of icing, but a clear note with edges. A handful of berries tasted complex, sweet and sharp together, with a perfume the packet never had. Nothing had been added to the fruit. Something had been lifted from me.

The fact is the mouth recalibrates when the shouting stops.

Read that sentence as settled observation, not as promise about your week or mine. Mouths adjust. Cooks rely on it. Anyone who has cut sweet from tea knows the second week tastes different from the first. The haze thins. Plain food speaks up. This is why the heavy eater pities the apple. He tastes it through the filter and blames the apple for the filter's work.

Which foods does the loop feed on. Look at the pattern without flinching.

The packets that run the loop share a build. Fat with sugar with salt and fast starch together, melt that vanishes, crunch that gives way, sweet that hits quick and fades quick. Chocolate. Ice cream. Biscuits. Cakes. Pastries. Bright drinks. Sweetened yoghurts and cereals that drink like pudding. White bread and pizza and chips eaten as sweets, in binges that act like sweets. Each is made to be eaten fast, past fullness, without a close.

Look at the bottom of that same list, where the loop never gathers. Apples. Strawberries. Broccoli. Plain food in its natural state. Nobody raids the kitchen at midnight for raw carrots. Nobody hides broccoli wrappers deep. Nobody says one floret is too much because it is never enough. The midnight call, the standing eating, the emptied packet, all gather around the engineered build. The natural sweet never starts the chase.

It was never the strawberry.

Let that stand as plain sorting, not as rule about your table. The strawberry never taught the hand to answer cues. The strawberry never blurred the afternoon. The strawberry closes. The packet opens. When this book says sugar, it means BAD SUGAR, the built doses, never the fruit in its skin or the meal on the plate. Your meals stay. Your fruit stays. What falls away under inspection is only the packet that borrowed their credit.

Ask what this sorting proves about taste. If sweetness itself were the pleasure, the strawberry would satisfy and the packet would be optional. Instead the heavy eater passes the strawberry to reach the packet, then reports the strawberry has no flavour. The haze explains him exactly. He needs the shout because the shout deafened him. Lift the shout and the quiet foods speak again. I have watched readers laugh at this in the telling, because they remember the day plain food turned loud without warning, midweek, over soup, and they understood the cook was innocent all along.

Two Small Experiments In Seeing

I do not ask you to believe me yet. I ask you to watch twice, the way an investigator watches, without altering a single dose.

First, the plate watch. Next time the breakroom box or the tin or the drawer calls, go if you wish. Eat if you wish. But watch from the first bite as if you were taking notes for a friend. Mark the flavour of bite one on a scale only you know. Mark bite two. Mark bite five. Mark whether you are tasting at bite five or munching. Mark what you are thinking about while you munch. Mark whether the tiredness lifts or walks back with you. Write nothing down if writing embarrasses you. Notice in the moment. The noticing is the work.

Ask after. Did the flavour last. Did the hour change. Would a pause with tea have done as much for the hour. Would supper have done more for the hunger. Honest answers here teach faster than my pages, because they are yours.

Second, the fruit check. On a morning when the mouth is clean, before any dose, eat a piece of whole fruit slowly. Seated. Nothing else in the mouth. Notice the juice. Notice the edges, sweet with sharp or perfume. Notice the close, where enough arrives cleanly and the mouth feels fresh after. Set that memory beside the packet memory. One closes fresh. One runs past closing into numbness. Ask which behaved like flavour loved, and which behaved like motion endured.

Neither experiment asks you to deny pleasure. Each asks you to locate it. Was it in the dose. Or in the hunger the dose interrupted. In the rest the dose rode with. In the people the dose sat among. In the gap the dose briefly filled. Locate it rightly and the credit moves to its owners. The dose stands thinner each time it is isolated. Thin it enough times and the question changes. Not how can I keep this delight. But what delight was there at all.

3. BEGIN WITH ELATION.
There is no doom here to be brave about.

Hold that line where I put it. I set it here early on purpose, because taste feels like loss when first questioned. It is not. Questioning the flavour is not the start of gloom. It is the start of meals that taste of themselves again. No misery is required to see clearly. No bravery is required to notice a dry cake eaten standing. Elation is the correct mood for an investigator who suspects the bill is false. He leans in glad. He checks the figures. He smiles as the error shows.

Carry that mood into the next room, where the second promise waits. You will tell me, if taste thins under light, perhaps energy remains. Perhaps the dose, whatever its flavour, lends strength for the afternoon. Perhaps without it the hands would shake and the mind would stall. That is the fuel story, and it deserves its own hearing, bite by bite, hour by hour. We will give it that hearing next. Taste has spoken now. What good was there at all on the tongue alone, once hunger and rest and company took back their credit.

SUMMARY

- The scale is wrong. We do not balance harm against benefit. We ask what benefit survived inspection.
- The claimed gift is one object in this book. This chapter examines taste only.
- The dose acts upon you. It does nothing for the tongue.
- The breakroom plate proves mediocre doses are eaten for the low hour, not for flavour.
- The first bite carries relief from the gap since the last dose. The rest of the packet runs on as motion past tasting.
- Hunger, rest and company lend the pleasure the dose claims. Isolated from all three, the dose tastes thin and cloys early.
- Heavy sweetness lays a haze that dulls plain food. Lift the shouting and apples and strawberries speak again.
- Which foods gather the loop answers itself. Built packets drive the chase. Whole fruit never starts it.
- Watch twice without changing a dose. The plate watch and the morning fruit check locate the credit rightly.
- Begin glad. Taste questioned is meals returned.
