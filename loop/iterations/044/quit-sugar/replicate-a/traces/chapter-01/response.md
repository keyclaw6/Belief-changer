Chapter 1
EAT NOTHING DIFFERENT UNTIL WE FINISH

If you woke this morning with pasted teeth and a thick head and a half-remembered promise to do better with sweet food, this book was written for you. I will show you how to leave BAD SUGAR behind. Easy, immediate and permanent. Without willpower and without misery. I know you find that hard to believe. Read on.

*There is a bounded thing you can leave, and leaving it changes nothing you love about eating.*

IN THIS CHAPTER: the bathroom mirror, the boxed line, the supermarket aisles, the cola can

The Mirror at Seven in the Morning

You know the mirror I mean. The bathroom light too bright. The tongue furred. The teeth edged with something sour. The head thick from last night. You stand there and you run the film back without wanting to. The biscuits after lunch because the afternoon went flat. The cola at the desk because you said you needed to keep going. The ice cream after supper because the day had been long. Then the late piece of chocolate standing at the fridge, not because you were hungry, but because the kitchen was quiet and the day was finally yours.

We have all stood there. We tell ourselves the same small stories while we brush. We say it was a bad day. We say tomorrow we will be stricter. We say we have a sweet tooth, as if teeth made decisions. We say "just a treat," as if the word treat explained why the box is empty.

Ask yourself one plain question while the toothbrush is still in your hand. If sweet food is food like any other, why did you hide any of it. Why did you eat standing up. Why did you push the wrappers deep so no one would see. Real meals are not eaten like that. No one eats a plate of roast chicken in the dark and feels ashamed of the bones.

Here is a second question, just as plain. If you enjoy this so much, why do you keep promising to cut back. People do not promise to cut back on things that are working. They do not make Monday rules about fresh bread with butter eaten at the table. They make rules about the packet that follows them from room to room.

I am not scolding you. I was the man with the drawer. The office drawer with the bars for the low hour. The car glove box with the mints that were never mints. The coat pocket with the crumpled foil. I know what it is to say "I'll start tomorrow" at midnight and mean it, and to mean nothing by midday. There is no weakness in you that I have not found in myself. We were not foolish. We were taught to read the packet as pleasure.

You have every reason to be wary of another book about sweet food. You have run the rules and the swaps and the sugar-free weeks. You have been hungry and virtuous by noon and dazed by four. You have watched the good intentions collapse into the box, the drawer, the night kitchen. You suspect the approach was wrong rather than the person. You are right. That suspicion is the only thing I ask you to bring with you.

Because there is something you stand to win here that no diet ever offered you. Think what it would mean to look at sweet food and feel no pull at all. Not a clenched refusal. Not a counted allowance. Simply no pull. Think what it would mean to finish a meal and close the kitchen and not hear that second voice start up. If that sounds too large to trust, I understand. You have nothing to lose and everything to gain.

That sentence is not decoration. You risk no hunger. You pay no fee. You sign nothing. You simply read, with the same eyes you use for everything else, and you let the argument do its work. If I am wrong, you will have lost an hour. If I am right, you will have found the door.

What We Mean When We Say BAD SUGAR

We need one clear line before we go further, because eating itself cannot be left. You must eat. Your body asks for meals. Your table holds people you love. No sane method would ask you to stop that.

This book leaves all of that alone. It asks only for the leaving of a bounded subset. I give it a capital name so the line never moves again.

> BAD SUGAR = refined/added free sugars plus the fast junk carbs that behave like them in the loop: sweets, chocolate, biscuits, cakes, ice cream, pastries, sugary drinks and juices, sweetened yoghurts and cereals, white bread–pizza–chips binges eaten as sweets, and honey/syrup/agave/diet-sweet swaps used to keep the sweet hit alive. When this book says sugar, take it to mean BAD SUGAR. Whole fruit, vegetables, plain dairy, grains eaten as meals, and real food cooked at home are not the target and are never counted.

Read that box twice. When this book says sugar, take it to mean BAD SUGAR. An apple is not BAD SUGAR. A bowl of vegetables is not BAD SUGAR. A plate of rice eaten as dinner is not BAD SUGAR. Milk poured for a child is not BAD SUGAR. Supper cooked at home from real food is not BAD SUGAR.

What is BAD SUGAR. The packet sweets. The chocolate bar that calls from the till. The biscuits that go soft by Friday. The cake that sits under plastic in the office. The ice cream tub with the lid never quite closed. The pastries that shatter all over the bag. The cola and the bright juices and the sweetened yoghurts and the cereals that look like breakfast and act like pudding. The white bread and pizza and chips when they are eaten the way sweets are eaten, in binges, standing, secret, for the hit rather than the meal. The honey and syrup and agave and diet-sweet packets when they are used to keep the sweet hit alive under another name.

Why put them together. Because in the life of a sweet eater they behave as one thing. They arrive as a quick dose. They lift for minutes. They drop you lower than they found you. They call for another dose to lift the drop. That circle has a name in this book. It is the daily drip that keeps you topping up from morning to night.

Do you see what this line does. It takes the fear out of the project at the first step. We are not asking you to stop eating. We are not asking you to weigh your plate or to count your mouthfuls or to live on leaves. We are asking you to see one bounded group of doses for what it is, and to step out of its circle. Everything else on your table stays where it is.

"I don't keep it in the house." I hear that often. Then I open the cupboard in the story the reader tells me, and there it is behind the peanut butter. The sauce with sweet listed second. The bread that stays soft for nine days. The yoghurt with the fruit picture that is mostly syrup. This is not your fault. This is what the shelves have become. Once you hold the boxed line, you can see it clearly. Before the line, every packet argued its own exception. After the line, the exceptions end.

So keep the line where I put it. Do not stretch it to punish fruit. Do not shrink it to protect the Friday bar. Let BAD SUGAR mean what the box says, no more and no less. The rest of this book speaks inside that line.

The Aisles That Look Like Normal

Walk with me down the supermarket on a Thursday evening. The lights are kind. The trolleys roll on. The air smells of warm bread near the door. You came for milk and eggs. You leave with crunch and pops and tubs.

Watch what happens in the sweet aisle. No one hurries. Hands go out and turn packets over and put them back and take them again. A man compares two family bars as if he were choosing wine. A woman holds a cake box at arm's length and laughs with her friend and puts it in the trolley and says the children asked for it. A boy walks beside his mother adding his own tax as they go, one packet now, one for the weekend. Nobody looks strange. That is the strangest part.

At the checkout the embarrassment you carried in dissolves, because every trolley around you matches yours. The belt fills with the same bright colours. The beeps run on and on. The total climbs. You feel normal again. Of course you do. When everyone carries the same load, no one asks why it is heavy.

I want you to remember that belt, because it answers the mirror. In the bathroom you thought you were alone in the night kitchen. In the shop you see you were never alone. We learned this together. We learned it from full shelves and kind lights and packets built to be opened with one hand while the other hand works. We learned it from birthdays and office plates and petrol stations at midnight. We learned it so well that abnormal looks normal.

Does that look like choice to you. A whole town pushing the same trolley down the same aisle for the same low hour. Or does it look like something taught so early and so often that we stopped seeing it.

I am not angry at the people on that belt. I am warm toward every one of them, because I was the man ahead of them counting coins with a bar already half gone in my pocket. My anger is for the con, not the conned. There is a difference between a person and a trap. The person deserves kindness. The trap deserves daylight.

Call it by its plain name from here on. It is the Bad Sugar trap. A trap is not a character flaw. A trap is a set of jaws that looks like a doorway. Mice are not weak for entering it. They were invited by the smell. Once you see the jaws, you do not need to be stronger than the steel. You need only stop walking through that doorway.

You may ask why I speak with such certainty when you have failed before. The answer is simple. You never failed. The rules failed. The Monday restarts and the cheat days and the swaps that kept the sweet hit alive under another name, those were built to snap back. A method that leaves the belief intact and attacks the mouth will always lose to the mouth. All you have to do is follow all the instructions.

That sentence will return in this book whenever the path looks steep. It is not a scold. It is a contract. I do the seeing. You do the following. I show you the doorway and the jaws. You walk on the side where the jaws are not.

The Teaspoons in the Can

Take one ordinary can of cola from the fridge. Hold it cold in your hand. Now count what is inside it in the unit your body understands.

The fact is that the world's public-health authority sets a ceiling for free sugars at less than ten in a hundred of daily energy, which for a person of healthy weight eating about two thousand calories is about fifty grams, about twelve level teaspoons, with a further move toward five in a hundred noted as bringing additional benefit. That ceiling is not my opinion. It is the line drawn for whole populations because current intakes run far above it.

Now look at the can again. One can alone can carry around nine or ten teaspoons. One small bottle of sweetened juice can carry more. A bakery bun with icing can carry as much again. By mid-afternoon, without any feast and without any greed, a normal day of doses can put you above the whole day's ceiling before supper is even thought of.

The fact is that on average adults in the United States take in seventeen teaspoons of added sugar a day, with sweetened drinks and bakery products named as the top carriers. That figure is an average across many lives, not a sentence on you. Some take less. Many take far more without ever feeling they ate much at all, because the doses arrive one by one. A spoon in coffee. A fix at eleven. A hit at three. A shot with the evening film. None of them felt like excess at the time. Added together they exceed the ceiling twice over and more.

Stand that can next to the mirror. In the morning you saw the fur on your tongue and felt the thickness in your head and told yourself it was age or sleep. Count the teaspoons and ask again. Could a body take that daily drip, day after day, year after year, and show nothing. Could teeth bathe in that syrup and stay silent. Could blood and liver and pressure carry that load and never signal.

"I need a pick-me-up." I hear you. We all said it at two in the afternoon when the screen blurred and the room went warm. "Sugary somethings to get me through." That was my sentence for years. I believed the dose was fuel for the slump. I did not ask what made the slump. I did not ask why the pick-me-up needed picking up again at four, then again at nine. I only knew the minute after the dose felt brighter than the minute before. That minute fooled me completely.

Keep the can on the counter while you read this chapter. Do not pour it away in a brave gesture. Do not swear off anything. Eat exactly as you do now while we look together. I ask only that you count honestly when you lift a dose. How many teaspoons rode in with it. How many ceilings have you crossed this week without a single feast to blame.

What the Dose Does to Teeth and the Quiet Load After

The fact is that tooth decay is the most common noncommunicable condition on earth, affecting two and a half thousand million people, and that free sugars in foods and drinks are its most common dietary risk factor. Plaque forms on the surface of the tooth and turns the free sugars contained in foods and drinks into acids that destroy the tooth over time.

Read that slowly, with your morning mouth still in mind. The same sweet dose that lifts the minute also feeds acid against the enamel. The tooth gets no vote. It does not care whether you called the dose "just a treat" or "special occasions" or "I've managed to be good this week." It sits in the syrup all the same. Brushing and fluoride matter, and dentists do work that I do not do, but the mechanism underneath them is plain. Sweet liquid and soft crumb sit, plaque works, acid forms, surface thins.

I have sat in the dentist's chair with the bib on and the light in my eyes and listened to the small scraping sounds and told myself it was age. It was not age. It was the daily drip, year after year. The fact is that many of us carry that damage quietly while we tell ourselves no harm is being done because we feel fine between doses.

Now widen the picture from the tooth to the trunk. The lift you feel is not extra energy. It is deferred load. Higher intakes of added sugar are linked with higher risk around heart and stroke through the routes the reviews name again and again, overload at the liver, pressure in the vessels, low-grade inflammation, and the bypassing of the controls that tell you that you are full. Sweetened drinks and bakery pieces lead that intake because they deliver a fast load with almost nothing to slow it. The body copes. Then it copes a little less well. Then the numbers at the check-up begin to move.

I state these consequences flatly because you are owed the truth, not because I want you pale. Look at what they share. Teeth, liver, pressure, appetite. Each is a place where the body keeps score while the mind keeps excuses. "Once a week is fine." Count the week. "Special occasions." Count the occasions. When the daily drip is counted honestly, the special stops looking special.

And here is the heaviest part to carry, so hear it from someone who carried it. To keep taking that load every single day for the rest of your life for something you do not even need would be a strange bargain if it were set before you clearly. No one would sign it with open eyes. We signed it with closed eyes, one small dose at a time, because each dose arrived as pleasure and left as need.

Do you feel the chill as you read that. Good. Let it come fully. Then let me take it away again, because I do not want you to change from fear. Fear makes people clench and count and white-knuckle through the week and then collapse into the box. I am not here to frighten you into a cage. I am here to show you that the cage was never locked. The tooth and the liver and the pressure tell you the trap is real. They do not tell you to be afraid. They tell you there is something solid to walk away from.

Not From Fear

If fear were enough, you would have stopped long ago. Every sweet eater knows a scare. Every sweet eater has read a label at midnight and felt sick and sworn the oath and broken it by Friday. That cycle is not proof that you are broken. It is proof that fear never opened that door for anyone. It only tightens the loop for a week.

So set fear down here. I do not ask you to picture the worst or to borrow trouble from the future. I ask you to picture the ordinary. The clear morning when the tongue is clean and the head is light and breakfast tastes of itself. The quiet aisle where the trolley rolls past the bright packets without a pause. The meal closed, the plate empty, the kitchen dark, no second voice starting up. Those are not dreams. They are what remains when the daily drip stops arriving.

You may say this sounds too simple for a habit so old. I know this is hard to accept. It was hard for me too. I had failed so often that I trusted failure more than promise. I thought freedom, if it came, would come after months of counting and sweating. Then I saw the boxed line and the belt and the can, and I understood that I had been fighting the wrong thing. I had been fighting my mouth. The work was in the seeing.

That is why this book proceeds the way it does. We look first. We demolish nothing by force. We name what is there. We count what is there. We let each claimed benefit speak and then we look at where the benefit really came from. By the time we finish, there will be nothing to fight, because there will be nothing you still believe you are missing.

Eat what you love; you will never miss BAD SUGAR.

Read that promise as plainly as it is written. Your meals stay. Your appetite stays. Your table stays. Real food cooked at home, fruit in its season, plain dairy, grains eaten as meals, vegetables with their colour still in them, all stay. What leaves is the packet that pretended to be food while it behaved like a dose. When the pretending ends, the missing ends with it.

You may wonder whether such a change can hold. Ask a different question. If there is truly nothing in BAD SUGAR that does anything for you, what would there be to hold. A door seen as a doorway needs no guard. All you have to do is follow all the instructions.

Those instructions are small and they are spoken plainly. The first stands at the gate of everything else.

1. KEEP AN OPEN MIND.
You have nothing to judge yet.

Bring your doubt with you if you like. Bring your list of reasons this cannot work for you. Bring the memory of every collapsed Monday. I do not ask you to believe me yet. I ask only that you do not close the book on yourself before the argument is heard. Judge at the end if you must judge. At the start, observe.

SUMMARY

- BAD SUGAR is a bounded set of sweet doses, not all eating, and the line around it does not move.
- The supermarket belt shows the habit as taught and shared, not as personal choice or personal fault.
- A normal day of doses can cross the public ceiling without any feast, and the count is the proof.
- The same dose that lifts the minute feeds acid at the tooth and adds quiet load at liver, pressure and appetite control.
- Fear tightens the loop; this book does not ask you to act from it.
- Leaving BAD SUGAR leaves meals, appetite and tables intact.
