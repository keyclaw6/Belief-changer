Chapter 3

WHAT GOOD AT ALL

IN THIS CHAPTER: the back patio at dusk, the recliner, the ashtray filling stick by stick, the lighter, the carton within reach, and the music playing.

*The cigarette never did a single thing for you, and the scales stand empty.*

You are on the back patio as the light goes.

The recliner holds you. The ashtray sits at your elbow. The lighter lies beside it. The carton stands within reach on the small table, so there is no need to go indoors. Music plays from the kitchen doorway, low enough to talk over. You light one stick and set the pack down and lean back, and this is the ceremony you have performed a thousand times. The first draw. The lean. The gaze across the fence while the smoke moves off. The sense that the day has closed its till and this is your hour.

I know that hour well. I guarded it for years. No one was allowed to hurry it or spoil it with questions. It felt considered. It felt earned. It felt like the one part of the day that belonged to me alone, arranged exactly as I liked it, with every object in its place. The weight of the lighter in the palm. The crackle of the cellophane when the carton opened. The first thin ribbon rising past the bulb. I believed all of it was mine, and I believed the hour would be thinner without it.

Keep every object in its place now, because I want to sit beside you in that hour and audit it with you, stick by stick, while the music plays. You have seen one jaw of the nicotine trap already, the night errand and the false papers. Tonight we weigh the other jaw, the story that the trap gives something worth the errand.

THE VOICES AROUND THE ASHTRAY

Before we touch a single stick, let me speak your own defence for you, so you know I hear it.

If I asked you why you smoke, you would not give me one reason. You would give me a handful, each true to how it feels from inside.

"If anything stressful happens in my life, a cigarette takes care of it."

"Cigarettes were my reward for almost everything. Teaching a class. Finishing a story. Finishing a paragraph. Driving to the grocery store."

"The moment I light up I'm in heaven, but I feel shitty afterwards."

"You often get offered a cigarette and in that kind of environment it's hard to turn down. You don't want to be the only one not smoking and because you've been drinking, you don't think about the risks, you think one won't hurt."

"I know I can never have just one. I learned that the hard way, thinking after five months I could control myself and just have one."

Those are not my lines. They are the lines smokers speak when they speak honestly, and I print them here because I want you to hear your own voice among them. The pressured worker digging for the pack at lunch. The finisher paying each small done thing with a light. The lover of the light who already hates being a smoker. The drink-lit friend in the smoking area who calls this the last night on earth. The long quitter who found the chain back in a single offered stick.

I hear every one of those voices. I honour the life inside each of them.

And I answer none of them yet.

We leave each voice standing exactly where it spoke, while we settle a prior question that decides how every one of those voices must be heard. That question is the whole of this chapter. Get this question right on the patio, and the patio reads like daylight. Get it wrong, and we argue for ever about shadows.

THE WRONG TRIAL

We smokers have held the wrong trial for years.

The trial in our heads runs like this. On one side of the scales, the harm. The cough in the morning. The money burned. The stairs that wind us. The smell in the curtains and the coat. The plans cut short. On the other side of the scales, the help. The calm. The break. The reward. The company on the patio. And we weigh them against each other and ask the honest citizen's question. Does the harm outweigh the good.

The fact is that question is rigged, and it kept us trapped for decades.

A trial of harm against benefit assumes there is benefit to weigh. It grants the cigarette its whole case before the first witness speaks. Once you grant that, you have already lost, because no scare ever kills a felt benefit. Show a smoker a diseased lung and he nods and lights another, and not because he is stupid. He lights another because somewhere under the fright he believes the thing that scars his lung also steadies his hand, rewards his work, keeps him company on the patio. Take away half his years but leave him his friend, and he clings to the friend. That is what we all did. We counted the cost in full and paid it gladly, because we believed we were buying something.

So I refuse that trial. I do not ask you to weigh help against harm tonight. I ask you to put the harm off the table entirely, set it to one side with the lighter and the carton, and look at the other pan of the scales alone. Let the cough, the cost, the smell, the stairs stand outside the room for one hour. They will keep. They are not going anywhere. Tonight the cigarette gets the kindest hearing any accused ever got. Nothing it did to you counts against it. We ask only what it did for you.

What good is there at all.

Ask it plainly, with the harm removed, and answer honestly. Name one real thing the cigarette does for you that the evening, the music, the recliner, the company, your own body at rest would not have done without it. Just one. Not what it costs. Not what it risks. What it gives. If a friend charged you for an hour he never improved, you would not call the charge small and pay it gladly. You would ask what the payment bought. Ask it here with the same plain fairness, because fairness is all I ask of you tonight.

Does that sound too stark. I know this is hard to accept, and I welcome the protest, because the protest shows you still believe there must be something on the pan. Very well. Keep the protest beside you while we watch. The patio will judge between us without raising its voice.

Do you want to find the answer in argument or in your own eyes. Of course you want your own eyes, because argument can be cheated and eyes cannot. Then watch with me. The next stick you light on that patio is our witness. We will follow it from the flick to the butt and ask at each step the only question that matters. What did it do for you.

ONE STICK, WATCHED

Light it now in the mind's eye, slowly, the way you light when no one hurries you.

The lighter flicks. The tip glows. The first draw pulls. The smoke goes down and out. The shoulders drop a fraction. The gaze settles on the fence. The music continues from the kitchen. The recliner creaks once under your shift. The ash lengthens. You tap it into the ashtray. You draw again. The stick shortens. The filter warms. The taste thickens. The last draws bite. You press the butt into the ashtray beside its brothers and reach, without deciding, toward the pack for the audit to continue.

Now rewind that small film and separate what belonged to the stick from what belonged to the evening, because we have credited the stick with the whole patio for years. We sat in comfort and called the comfort smoking, the way a man might sit in a warm room and thank the draft under the door for the warmth.

The recliner held you before the flick and after the butt. Did the stick build the chair. The music played through the draw and through the pause between draws. Did the stick write the song. The dusk cooled your face whether you inhaled or let the stick burn in the ashtray. The pause from work was granted by the hour, not by the paper tube. Your lungs drew breath all day without asking the filter's leave. Which part of the comfort required burning leaves.

Watch closer still, because the eye misses the trick when it looks at the hour whole.

That small drop of the shoulders on the first draw. We call it calm. Look at when it arrives. It arrives within seconds of the draw, the way a debt eases the moment a coin touches the palm. It does not build like rest. It lands like a topped level. Then watch how long it stays. It stays minutes. Before the stick is out, the faint gnaw has already begun to return, the sense that something will soon be missing again. If the stick gave calm, why does the calm need topping before the butt is cold. A meal feeds you for hours. A rest restores you for hours. A friend's company warms you after he has gone home. What kind of gift must be unwrapped twenty times a day to keep you normal.

Ask yourself, and answer honestly. When the stick burned down to the filter, what had been added to the evening that the recliner, the dusk, the music, your own seated body had not already supplied. And when the ashtray took the butt, with its grey roll and its sour end, what exactly had you received that you did not own before the flick.

You know the answer, and the answer empties the pan. Nothing was added. Something was taken, used, and billed.

That is the shape we never joined while we lived inside it. We sat in a good hour and paid a toll to sit there, then thanked the toll-keeper for the hour. We believed the evening needed the smoke the way the lungs need air, when the fact is the other way around. The evening carried the smoke. The smoke carried nothing.

Watch a second stick to be sure the first did not cheat you, because the patio audit works stick by stick and the ashtray keeps the score.

You light again. The ceremony repeats exactly. Flick, glow, draw, lean, gaze, tap, draw, press. The music has moved to its next song without your help. The recliner has not improved. The dusk has deepened on its own. The ashtray now holds two butts, both sour, both grey, both warm at the crushed end. What does the ashtray own now that it did not own before, except the leavings. What do you own now that you did not own before the pair burned down. The fingers smell. The mouth tastes of ash. The carton stands one stick lighter and the evening stands not one minute richer. If the first stick gave, the second should show the gain doubled. Show me where the doubling sits. In the chair. In the song. In your chest. Point with the lighter if you can. You cannot, because the audit runs the same both times. The hour gives. The stick bills.

THE PACKAGE ON YOUR LAP

Lift the pack from the small table now and hold it while the music plays, because the pack confesses once you know how to read it.

The gold or red or white box. The neat row of white sticks. The filter that browns as you use it. The lighter that answers every time. It presents itself as a small luxury, a personal kit, a pleasure with its own furniture. The recliner, the ashtray, the carton at hand, the music on — the whole ceremony exists to tell you this is taste, this is choice, this is yours.

The fact is the cigarette is not the product. It is the package. The product is nicotine.

Sit with that sentence on the patio and let the objects translate it. The firms that make the packs learned long ago that the stick is only a dispenser, a paper vehicle for a dose. The flavour talk, the brand story, the colour of the box, the length, the talk of mildness or strength — all of it is wrapping around a measured hit. You did not fall in love with burning paper. Paper never built a ceremony. The dose built the ceremony, and the ceremony hides the dose. The ashtray on your small table never fills with pleasure enjoyed. It fills with doses spent, twenty small vehicles burned to carry one chemical back and forth across the same short gap.

Why does that change the audit. Because it shows why the pan stands empty and yet the hand keeps reaching. A man who believes he smokes for taste should smoke less when the taste turns, yet we smoked more as the taste thickened into ash. A man who smokes for company should need no stick alone on the patio, yet the loneliest sticks burned here beside the recliner with no witness but the fence. Taste and company never drove the hand. The falling level drove the hand, and the wrapping taught the mouth to call the drive desire.

Why does that change the audit. Because it shows why the pan stands empty and yet the hand keeps reaching.

You reach for the wrapping and you queue for the dose. You choose the brand and the box and the lighter, and about the going back you choose nothing. The level falls. The thought arrives. The hand moves to the pack on the small table without a vote being taken. Each stick delivers its hit in seconds and lets it fade in minutes, so the hour that felt like one long pleasure was in truth a chain of small top-ups, each quieting for a moment the want the last one had guaranteed. We called the quieting help. It was topping up wearing the clothes of help.

Ventriloquize your objection and I will meet it where you sit, because I sat there too. "But it must do something for me. I feel it do something." You feel something, and I do not deny the feeling for a second. You feel the level rise. You feel the gnaw pause. What you do not feel is a gift arriving. You feel a debt pausing. Would you call the man who knocks your hat off and hands it back your helper. Would you pay him by the hour for the rest of your days and defend him as your comfort. That is the credit error this whole book exists to correct. We gave the evening's credit to the stick, and the stick kept the books.

Name the compressed lie plainly so you can hear it whenever it speaks again. Every claimed benefit — the calm, the break, the reward, the heaven, the company — arrives in your mouth as one object. You call it a genuine pleasure or crutch.

Say it slowly on the patio and hear how much work those five words do. A genuine pleasure would enrich the hour and leave you fuller after. A crutch would hold you up where you would fall without it. This object does neither. It borrows the recliner's comfort and sells it back as its own. It borrows the music's ease and sells it back as its own. It borrows the pause between tasks and sells it back as its own. It is not pleasure and not support. It is the story that the topping up is pleasure and support, told so often by so many voices that we mistook the telling for feeling.

Once that story is seen, the audit closes by itself. Look at the ashtray. Look at the carton. Look at the butt pressed out with its sour end. Point to one thing the hour gained from the burning that the hour did not already own. You cannot, because there is nothing to point to. The pan is empty. The ceremony staged an engine of thought and dreams, and the engine ran on the music, the dusk, the chair, your own body at rest — everything except the stick. We smokers believed we lit the engine. We only lit a paper toll beside the engine and watched its smoke rise while the engine ran on its own.

It is doing plenty TO you. It is doing nothing FOR you.

Read that verdict beside the ashtray and let the objects confirm it. TO you: the cough that waits for morning, the money stacked in the carton, the smell worked into the recliner's cloth, the hour chopped into twenty small errands of flick and tap and press. FOR you: nothing the dusk did not already give, nothing the music did not already play, nothing the chair did not already hold. The prepositions carry the whole case. Plenty TO. Nothing FOR. That is not a slogan. That is the audit totalled in the patio's own coin. Hold the butt up to the light before you press it out and ask what part of it was ever yours. The glow. The ash. The sour end. None of it stayed with you except the smell on the fingers, and the evening never needed any of it to be whole.

Do you want the proof to stay as words, or do you want it as something you can carry into every room where the voices speak. Of course you want something you can carry, because words fade by morning and a true question does not. Then carry the question, not the answer. The answer fades when the music stops. The question works wherever you stand.

3. ASK ONLY WHAT IT DOES FOR YOU
Harm against benefit is the wrong trial.

When the pressured hour comes, when the finished job asks for its pellet, when the light calls itself heaven, when the drink-lit corner offers its stick, ask only what it does for you. Not what it costs. Not what it risks. What it gives. Demand one real thing, and watch the pan stand empty. That single question keeps the scales honest when the voices rise. Bring me that question answered honestly from your own patio, and the rest follows.

SUMMARY

- The harm-against-benefit trial grants the cigarette its case before the argument starts, so it was set aside in favour of a harder question.
- The voices of stress, reward, heaven, company, and control were printed as you speak them and left standing untouched while the question was settled.
- One patio stick watched from flick to butt added nothing the recliner, the dusk, and the music had not already supplied.
- The pack on the small table is wrapping around a dose, which explains the reaching without putting a single benefit on the pan.
- Every claimed benefit compresses to one object that fails both its names, neither pleasure nor support.
- Beside the ashtray the total reads plain: plenty done to the evening, nothing done for it.
