Chapter 7

TEN SECONDS UP, THEN THE CLOCK

IN THIS CHAPTER: the office desk at three, the clock with its loud second hand, the pack beside the keyboard, the stairwell with its iron rail, and the landing window.

*The lift that arrives in ten seconds and leaves in minutes never gave you anything.*

You know the timed minute from the back step. You watched the flick and the first draws soften the chest for seconds while the butt burned on toward sourness and hiding. Keep that watched minute beside you, because this hour shows you why the minute behaved that way.

Three o'clock in the office holds the whole device in one hour. The morning sticks sit behind you. The evening sticks sit ahead. The pack lies beside the keyboard with its cellophane torn at the corner. The clock hangs above the door with its second hand sweeping loud enough to hear when the room goes quiet. The stairwell waits past the toilets with its one flight down to the yard door and its iron rail cold under the palm. Bring me that hour watched honestly from your own desk, and the rest follows.

THE HOUR THAT OWNS YOU

Watch the hour the way you watched the minute, because the hour keeps books the minute cannot hide.

At ten past three you light from the pack beside the keyboard. The smoke goes warm across the tongue. The shoulders drop a fraction. The jaw unclenches. The screen looks kinder for a blink. You press the butt out and turn back to the mail. The quiet holds for a while. Then the chair starts to press. The fingers start to drum. The eyes leave the screen for the pack and back again. By twenty past the hand has checked the pack twice without lighting. By half past, with the next deadline named aloud in the meeting room, the gnaw has grown teeth and the hand is digging again.

We all lived that hour. We smokers of desks and workshops and cabs all learned its shape. Morning light. Mid-morning light. Lunch light at the wall. Afternoon light at the car. Tea light at the step. Evening light at the television. Night light at the bed window. The day became a row of small bells and we believed the bells rang for pleasure. The fact is the bells rang for the level, and the level wore the mask of pleasure to collect the credit.

See the master the hour obeys, because the hour never ran loose.

Nicotine keeps time the way a strict master keeps time. He owns the hour and he rings you like a bell. He feeds you a dose that lifts you for seconds, then he lets it fall and calls you back with a tug low in the body. At the top of the hour the tug is a whisper. At the half hour, with worry aboard, the whisper turns to a whine. You answer by crossing to the door, by palming the lighter, by finding the corner where lighting draws no comment. You call that answering your own wish. The master calls it attendance.

I knew that master. I obeyed his clock for years while I told myself I smoked when I chose. We told ourselves we lit for the taste and the pause and the company of the hands. We lit when the coffee came. We lit when the phone rang. We lit when the work closed. We lit when the talk paused. Look closer at the lighting and the choosing falls away. The lighting follows the falling level the way the cough follows the night. Morning stick. Midday stick. Dusk stick. The gaps between them taught us where our pleasures supposedly lived. The gaps were the teacher, and the teacher measured doses and not delights.

This is the nicotine trap.

Say that plainly at the desk and hear how much work those three words do. A trap that must be fed every hour never gave a gift. A master that rings you to the door never served your pleasure. The hour owns you while you call the obedience yours.

Ask what the obedience ever added past the pause of its own calling. Did the count make the letter truer. Did the cracked window in rain make the drive kinder. Did the timed break make the meeting shorter. You know the answer from your own diary. The watch added nothing to the work or the meal or the company. It taxed each one while the day did the living.

TEN SECONDS UP

Now time the lift itself, because timing tells you what the lift belongs to and timing cannot be argued with once seen.

Sit with the clock where you can see the second hand. Light in your usual way. Watch the hand while you draw. The first draw pulls warm. The second pulls deeper. Within ten seconds of the smoke reaching the lungs the sharp edge peaks and the chest loosens its fraction. Name that speed honestly from your own chair. It does not arrive as a slow afternoon that builds. It arrives as a topped level, fast up and fast gone. Before the ash has grown a fingernail, the brightest part has already passed. By the middle of the stick the draw tastes thinner and hotter. The tongue finds the dry edge turning sour. The throat finds the catch turning rough. The chest holds less loosening with each pull.

I speak your protest in your own words here, so you know I hear it before we test it.

“But I feel it working. I feel the agitation drop. I HAD to have that one or I would have snapped at them in there.”

I lived inside that sentence for years. It feels true in the hands because the first half is true in the body. The draw does bring a rush of softening. The snapping does thin for minutes. The hands do steady. This hour honours the first half fully while it tests what the first half proves. The softening is real as a feeling. The question is what the feeling belongs to.

Watch what the speed proves, because the speed accuses without my voice raised.

A kind word warms you for hours. A finished job pleases you when you meet it again at night. A meal holds you till morning. What kind of calm must be topped twenty times in a day to keep the day calm. What kind of help leaves before the stick is out. What kind of pleasure needs the clock to survive. You know the answer, and the answer holds without argument. What arrives in ten seconds and leaves in minutes never solved the pressure that filled the hour. It paused a debt that the last dose created, and the pause was misread as aid.

Light the picture from the other side to be sure the first did not cheat you, because the hour keeps its own score and the score repeats.

We smoked through the morning at our usual pace. Each stick delivered its dose and let it fade. By three o'clock, with the mail piled and the meeting voice through the wall, the level had fallen again and the body called for its top-up in the language we learned to call hunger for relief. Light feet. Bright ache. A sense of due. We called that the afternoon calling for its pause. The fact is the afternoon supplied the piled mail and the voices and the close air, and the falling level supplied the hunger on top of them. Then the next dose paused the hunger for minutes and we thanked the dose for giving calm. We felt a debt pause and we called the pause help.

Think of the child you were before the first pack, because the start remembers what the middle forgot. The boy who ran the yard at eight with scraped knees knew the lift of the bell without a paper tube carrying his name. The girl who leaned on the rail after washing up and felt the cool on her forearms knew the softening before she knew the brand. Did your nature change since. Did your nerves forget how to quiet at a pause until smoke touched them. Did your chest lose the power to steady at a kind word until fire fed it. You know the answer. The pause is older than the lighting and larger than the lighting, and the lighting has lived off the pause the way a lodger claims the house.

THE HOLLOW THAT GOT A NAME

There is a feeling you have named as yourself, and it needs naming truly before the hour can close.

Between sticks the body talks. Not loudly. Not as pain that lays you down. As a hollow. As a faint gnaw under the ribs. As hands a fraction too quick on the keys. As eyes too quick to the pack. As a temper shortened by a notch. As attention thinned so the paragraph must be read twice. Under worry the hollow sharpens. At the half hour, with the deadline named, the hollow turns to a whine that wears your voice and says light now or snap.

I know that whine. I answered it ten thousand times with flame while I told myself I was answering my own character. We smokers of tight afternoons all learned to read that whine as proof we were edgy people who needed a crutch to cope. We said we were smokers because we were highly strung. We said the stick took the edge off a nature that would grate without it. We believed the hollow was us calling for our aid.

Watch the hollow closer still, because the hollow hides the split that ends the fear.

The hollow arrives when the dose falls. The hollow lifts for minutes when the dose lands. The hollow returns when the dose fades again. Morning hollow. Midday hollow. Dusk hollow. The same shape at the same intervals, wearing different afternoons the way a bell wears different weather. A nature does not arrive by the clock and leave by the lighter. A hunger for air after stairs does not vanish for ten minutes because smoke touched it. What arrives with the fall and leaves with the topping belongs to the fall and the topping, not to your character.

What you feel is an empty, slightly edgy, slightly restless feeling.

Say that sentence at the desk and hear how much work those plain words do. Empty names the hollow under the ribs. Slightly edgy names the shortened temper. Slightly restless names the drumming fingers and the twice-read paragraph. The words give the whine its true size. Not a flaw in you. Not a verdict on your nerves. A small complaint sounding large because a lifetime of voices taught you to hear it as yourself calling for aid.

That complaint is the Ash Mite.

Hear that naming warmly, because the naming takes the weight off you without mocking what you felt. A tiny ash-dwelling feeder wails when its dose runs low. It has no malice and no grandeur. It starves within days when unfed. Its wail borrows your throat the way a gull borrows a roof. You fed it every hour and called the feeding your pleasure. You heard its wail every half hour and called the wail your nature.

That story is the Big Fog.

Hear that naming warmly too, because the naming takes the shame off you without calling you foolish. A lifetime weather of talk and pictures and friendly hands taught you that smoke relaxes and steadies and rewards. The weather fed the wail a meaning. The meaning fed the hand the lighter. The hand fed the feeder its fix. Three parts turning like wheels inside one hour. The feeder wails. The weather translates. The hand obeys the clock.

Now hold the short peak beside the hour, because the short peak proves the wail is not a new self.

For most of us the sharpest edge sits in the first few days without a stick, then it loosens across a few weeks. It may begin within hours of the last dose. The head feels light. The temper runs short. The hands look for work. The sleep runs thin for nights. Then the edge dulls and the days open wider between wails. That is the feeder dying unfed while the weather clears. It never meant you had lost the power to cope. It meant the complaint was sounding before it went quiet.

Ask yourself, and answer honestly. Has the hollow ever stayed kind for hours the way a meal stays kind. Has the topping ever built on the last topping the way a second chorus builds on a song. Has the edginess ever failed to return by the next bell after being supposedly cured. You know the answer, and the answer breaks the character story at its hinge. What must be topped by the clock never cured the character. It rented the character out and signed the margin.

THE LIFEGUARD

There is a picture that holds the whole inversion in one frame, and this hour needs it before the stairwell, because the picture stops the gratitude that keeps the hand digging.

Picture a swimmer in late water. He plays near the ropes while the light holds. A strong arm closes around his neck from behind and pushes his head under. The chest burns. The limbs thrash. The water fills the nose. Then the arm hauls him up by the hair for a gasp. Air slams back. The chest loosens its fraction. The light looks kinder for a blink. The arm holds him smiling at the surface while the swimmer coughs and thanks the arm for the air.

You see the fraud at once from the beach. No one thanks that arm. No one calls that gasp kindness. No one says the swimmer needs the arm to breathe. The arm created the drowning and sold back the gasp as rescue. Thanking the arm for calm would be madness while the arm still holds the neck.

Thanking the stick for calm is thanking that arm for air.

Hold that water beside your desk hour and watch the credit change hands. The last dose pushed the head under by guaranteeing the fall. The fall filled the chest with the hollow. The next dose hauled the head up for ten seconds and sold the gasp as aid. We smokers coughed at the surface and thanked the arm for the air while the arm kept its grip for the next dunking. Morning dunking. Midday dunking. Dusk dunking. The gasps felt like kindness because the dunkings taught us where kindness supposedly lived. The dunkings were the teacher, and the teacher measured doses and not delights.

I welcome the anger that picture raises, because the anger belongs on the arm and never on the swimmer. No one calls the swimmer weak for thrashing. No one calls him foolish for gasping. He was conned by strength from behind while he played. We were conned the same way at the school wall and the park bench and the cold bus shelter where the first sticks tasted foul and the head swam. We coughed through the foulness and kept at it until the cough learned manners. Then the arm taught us to call the gasp pleasure and the dunking our nature. We believed because the gasp felt true in the chest while the hand that pushed stayed out of sight.

Ask what the gasp ever gave past the pause of its own dunking. Did the gasp make the water kinder. Did the gasp teach the swimmer to swim. Did the gasp stay in the lungs past minutes. You know the answer from your own hour. The gasp paused the burning the arm lit. Nothing added to the swimmer that the swimmer did not already hold before the arm closed. That is not a slogan. That is the desk hour totalled in its own coin.

The hour that rings never gave. It only billed.

THE PACK ON THE DESK

Now look down at the pack beside the keyboard, because the pack tells you what the hour has been moving through your hands.

The gold pack lies with its torn mouth where the fingers dig. The foil shows the bright inner mouth. The sticks stand white in their row with their filters firm at the lip. The lighter lies beside with its thumb wheel worn smooth from years of flicks. The glass dish holds the morning butts with their brown stains creeping up the filters and their stale tails still threading in the close air. The fingers carry the smell into the keys. The tongue carries the sour film into the tea. The throat clears once, then again.

Read that pack the way you read any tool once you know its job, because the pack never hid its job once the hour showed it.

The cigarette never stood as a thing of flavour and leaf the way the pictures taught. The pack and the paper and the filter stand as layers around a dose. The product inside the package is the dose itself, measured to lift you in seconds and leave you in minutes so the clock can ring again. The dealer who made the pack knew the lift and the fall the way a timekeeper knows the bell. He built the paper tube to carry the dose to the lungs fast. He built the filter to brown while the dose passes. He built the pack to hold a day of doses within reach of the hand. He sells the layers. He moves the dose.

I speak that flatly from your own desk, without dressing it as a charge from books, because the desk proves it without my voice raised. The pack sits where the hand can find it blind. The count runs in the head before the drive. Enough for the day. Enough for the evening in. Shop shut at ten. Kiosk shut at eleven. Garage open through the night at the far junction with its hard lights. We ran that count ten thousand mornings and called it habit. The fact is the count was guard duty, and the guard watched a paper box the way a nurse watches a drip. The non-smoker in the same office runs no count. The tea pours. The mail opens. The day opens. Nothing missing. Nothing guarded. Our day opened with stock-taking before speech, and we called the stock-taking ours.

Ask what the layers ever did past carrying the dose. Did the gold quiet the nerves. Did the filter sweeten the tea. Did the lighter warm the letter. You know the answer. The layers carried. The dose topped. The fall returned. The hand dug again. The pack never gave the pause. The pause belonged to the debt lifting for minutes while the desk did the living.

Weigh the pack in the palm at half past and feel how much of the hour lives in that small weight. The card box gives a fraction under the thumb. The sticks shift inside with a dry whisper. Four left for the afternoon. Two for the evening in. One held back for the morning kettle. The mind runs the small arithmetic while the screen glows. Corner shop shut at six. Garage open late at the far junction. The lift shared at the yard door at five. We smokers ran that arithmetic ten thousand afternoons and called it planning. The fact is the arithmetic was guard duty, and the guard watched paper the way a nurse watches a drip. The neighbour at the next desk runs no arithmetic. The drawer holds pens. The mug holds tea. The hour holds work. Nothing counted. Nothing weighed. Our palm weighed doses before speech, and we called the weighing ours. We said the counting calmed us.

Carry that weighed pack through the kitchen talk and watch who moves the hand. The kettle boils. The talk runs on football and rotas and the broken lift. A mate slides the pack across and nods toward the yard door. The hand lifts before the mind votes. The lighter finds the thumb. The door sighs. The yard air meets the face with its bins and bricks and washing lines beyond the wall. The talk continues without the words while the dose tops the hollow. We called that company. The fact is the company stayed at the kettle while the dose called the feet to the yard. The talk gave the warmth. The yard gave the pause. The stick billed for both while the kettle did the kindness. We said the yard break helped us cope.

Do not change from fear of that pack. I do not want you stopping because the pack frightens you. The method takes care of fear the way daylight takes care of a locked door once seen. Keep your eyes on the clockwork while the fear thins. The seeing does the work the dread never did.

ONE FLIGHT

There is one flight past the toilets that every smoker in this building knows, and it closes the hour the way the dish closed the minute.

Push back the chair at half past with the gnaw whining. Pocket the pack from habit. Walk the corridor with its humming strip light and its notice board. Push the stairwell door with its wired glass. The air runs cooler here. The iron rail runs cold under the palm. The steps drop one flight to the yard door with its wired window showing the yard bricks and the bins. The sound of the office thins behind the door.

Climb down and climb back, because the flight keeps what the desk hides.

You climb one flight with the pack in the pocket. The thighs burn on the rise. The chest tightens. The air comes short at the top. You pause at the rail with the palm cold on the iron. The throat clears. The chest holds a faint heaviness where the loosening stood at ten past. You tell the yard window that the years have marked the wind. You hear the clock through the door ringing the next half hour while you stand catching the short air.

I stood on that landing for years with the same short air and the same story about age and weather. We smokers of stairs and hills and platforms all learned that story. We said the stairs had grown steeper. We said the years had shortened the wind. We said the stick had nothing to do with the rail gripped white. The story felt kind because it spared the light the blame. Keep the story beside you while the landing speaks, because the landing speaks without my voice raised.

On the half-landing I stopped with my hand on the iron rail and felt my breath come back and knew the cigarette never helped me breathe.

Hold that landing minute beside the desk hour, because the two together end the character defence. The debt creates the hollow. The hollow wears your voice. The dose pauses the hollow for ten seconds. The clock rings the hollow back. The flight shows the billing in the chest while the clock shows the billing in the hour. One device. Two faces. The same hands turning the wheels.

Time the flight the way you timed the lift, because the flight is the exercise this hour owns.

Watch the second hand while the chest loosens after the next dose. Count the seconds the loosening holds before the fingers drum again. Watch the landing air after one flight while the chest heaves. Count the breaths before the air runs full again without flame. Ask which master the counts obey. Did the loosening lengthen when you drew deeper. Did the stairs grow kinder in the smoking years than in the clear school years before the first pack, when the legs ran bright on sleep and food and curiosity alone. Did the hollow ever skip the half hour because the afternoon pleased you. You know the answer, and the answer holds without argument. The counts obey the fall and the topping, never your worth.

You have timed the minute. You have timed the hour. The pack on the desk and the rail in the stairwell told one story in two rooms. Bring me that watched hour answered honestly from your own rooms, and the rest follows.

7. TIME THE RELIEF AND WATCH IT LEAVE
Ten seconds up, then the clock starts again.

When the next hollow calls, when the hand finds the pack blind, when the first draw softens the chest, watch the seconds run while you keep your eyes open. Demand one real thing the draw adds past the pause of its own debt. Keep the desk. Keep the pause. Keep the tea. Bring me that watched hour answered honestly from your own rooms, and the rest follows.

SUMMARY

- The lift arrives in seconds and leaves in minutes because it tops a falling dose, not because the hour turned kind from smoke.
- The hour rings like a bell because the dose fades by the clock, and the hand obeys the fall while calling the obedience pleasure.
- The hollow between sticks is a small feeder wail inside a lifetime weather story, not proof the edginess is who you are.
- The sharpest edge sits in the first days without a stick and loosens across weeks, which shows the wail dying rather than a self lost.
- The pack holds a day of doses inside paper layers, and each draw moves the dose while the desk does the living.
- The swimmer thanks no arm that dunked him, and the smoker owes no thanks to the dose that paupered the air it rents back.
- The stairwell shows in the chest what the clock shows in the hour, and both point one way once seen.
