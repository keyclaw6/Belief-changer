Chapter 8

THE DEALER'S WORKBENCH

IN THIS CHAPTER: the kitchen table in morning light, the gold pack with its torn mouth, the browning filter, the lighter with its worn wheel, and the garage cans.

*The gold pack never sold you safer smoking, it fed you doses dressed as choice.*

Put the pack on the table and look at it with me while the kettle steams behind us, because this table tells you who built the clock you timed.

The kitchen holds its morning shape. The oilcloth carries a ring from the mug. The breadboard leans at the backsplash with crumbs still along its edge. The window shows the yard wall and the washing line with one shirt lifting in the draft. The table itself stands bare except for what your hands laid down without thinking. The gold pack with its cellophane torn at the corner. The foil mouth pulled back. The white sticks standing in their row with their filters firm at the lip. The lighter beside with its thumb wheel worn smooth from years of flicks. The glass dish from last night holding three butts with their brown stains creeping up the filters and their stale tails still threading in the close air. Your fingers carry the smell into the keys of the day. Your tongue carries the sour film into the tea. Your throat clears once, then again. You know this table from ten thousand mornings, and you believed the table held your choice laid out for the day.

We all laid that table. We smokers of kitchens and desks and cabs all ran the same small count before speech. How many for the morning. How many for the work. How many for the evening in. Enough for the day. Enough for the shop shut at ten. Enough for the kiosk shut at eleven. Enough for the garage open through the night at the far junction with its hard lights. We called that planning. We said we liked the brand and the taste and the pack in the pocket. We said the gold meant smoother and the filter meant cleaner and the lighter meant ours. The fact is the count was guard duty, and the guard watched a paper box the way a nurse watches a drip. The neighbour at the next table runs no count. The drawer holds pens. The mug holds tea. The hour holds work. Nothing counted. Nothing weighed. Our palm weighed doses before speech, and we called the weighing ours.

I stood at that table for years with the same count and the same story about liking, while the table kept books I would not read. We told ourselves we bought flavour and leaf and a pause that belonged to us. We told ourselves the men who made the pack sold tobacco the way a baker sells bread. Look closer at the objects while the tea cools, because the objects confess once the clock has shown you the loop.

THE PACK ON THE TABLE

Lay the objects out one by one, because each one carries its part of the work and none of them hides once seen.

The gold pack lies with its torn mouth where the fingers dig. The card gives a fraction under the thumb. The sticks shift inside with a dry whisper. The gold carries a crest and a fine line and a word like lights or gold or smooth in small letters along the edge. The foil shows the bright inner mouth. The sticks stand white in their row. The filter sits firm at the lip with its cork pattern and its neat ring. The lighter lies beside with its flint wheel thinned from years of thumbs. The dish holds the morning butts with their filters gone soft and brown from lip to middle and their ash gone grey at the foot. The fingers smell of the dish after touching it. The tongue finds the dry edge turning sour by the middle of the stick. The throat finds the catch turning rough. You know these marks from your own hands, and you trusted the marks to mean milder.

We trusted the gold the way we trusted a sign over a shop. We said gold meant gentle. We said the filter took the harm while leaving the pleasure. We said the brown on the used filter proved the filter had caught what would have entered the chest. We said the lighter proved the moment was ours because the hand chose the minute. We believed the pack sold choice in twenty grades, and we chose our grade the way we chose our tea. The fact is the grades sold the same work in different coats, and the coat taught the hand where safety supposedly lived.

Ask what the coat ever did past carrying, because the table answers without my voice raised.

Did the gold quiet the nerves. Did the cork pattern sweeten the tea. Did the lighter warm the letter. Did the crest shorten the stairwell breath. You know the answer from your own diary. The coat carried. The dose topped. The fall returned. The hand dug again. The table never gave the pause. The pause belonged to the debt lifting for minutes while the kitchen did the living.

Hold the weighed pack in the palm at half past and feel how much of the day lives in that small weight, because the weight accuses without my voice raised. Four left for the afternoon. Two for the evening in. One held back for the morning kettle. The mind runs the small arithmetic while the kettle sings. Corner shop shut at six. Garage open late at the far junction. The shared light at the yard door at five. We ran that arithmetic ten thousand afternoons and called it ours. The fact is the arithmetic was attendance, and the attendance watched paper the way a nurse watches a drip. The non-smoker at the same table runs no arithmetic. The tea pours. The mail opens. The day opens. Nothing missing. Nothing guarded. Our day opened with stock-taking before speech, and we called the stock-taking character.

WHAT THE LAYERS CARRY

Read that pack the way you read any tool once you know its job, because the pack never hid its job once the hour showed it.

The cigarette never stood as a thing of flavour and leaf the way the pictures taught. The pack and the paper and the filter stand as layers around a dose. The product inside the package is the dose itself, measured to lift you in seconds and leave you in minutes so the clock can ring again. The men who built this pack knew the lift and the fall the way a timekeeper knows the bell. They built the paper tube to carry the dose to the lungs fast. They built the filter to brown while the dose passes. They built the pack to hold a day of doses within reach of the hand. They sell the layers. They move the dose.

I speak that flatly from your own table, without dressing it as a charge from books, because the table proves it without my voice raised. The pack sits where the hand can find it blind. The count runs in the head before the drive. The foil mouth stays open for speed. The sticks stand ready for the next topping. The lighter waits for the thumb. The dish fills by the hour. No baker watches his bread get weighed in the palm before speech. No tea seller counts his tea with dread at midnight. Only the dose seller needs the hand to guard a day of hits the way a nurse guards a drip. We smokers guarded paper and called the guarding habit. The fact is the guarding watched the level, and the level wore the mask of liking to collect the credit.

Think of the workbench where that pack was drawn, because the bench explains the table without my voice raised. On the bench lie papers of different thickness. Filters of different length. Holes punched in different rings. Tipping in different shades. Gold in different grades. The hands at the bench do not ask which leaf pleases your afternoon. They ask which paper draws fastest to the lungs. Which filter browns most kindly while letting the dose through. Which pack holds a full day within reach without frightening the buyer. Which word along the edge keeps the buyer loyal while the level falls. The bench builds the vehicle. The table pays for the ride. The lungs pay the toll. We believed the bench served our taste. The bench served the topping.

This is the nicotine trap.

Say that plainly at the table and hear how much work those three words do. A trap that must be fed every hour never gave a gift. A bench that measures the fall never served your pleasure. The table holds you while you call the attendance yours.

Ask what the layers ever added past the carry, because the carry indicts without my voice raised. Did the paper calm the row with your mate. Did the filter settle the bill on the mat. Did the gold make the child laugh louder at tea. You know the answer, and the answer holds without argument. What carries a dose never cured the day it rode. It billed the day for the ride while the day did the kindness.

THE MILDER BARGAIN

Now take the gold word at its word, because the gold word is where the bargain lives and where the bargain breaks.

I speak your protest in your own words here, so you know I hear it before we test it.

“But mine are lights. I moved to gold years ago. They feel smoother on the throat, and the morning cough sits lighter since I switched.”

I lived inside that sentence for years. It feels true in the throat because the first half is true as a feeling. The draw does feel cooler on the tongue. The throat does catch less on the first pull. The head does swim less at the first light. This table honours the first half fully while it tests what the first half proves. The smoothness is real as a feeling. The question is what the feeling belongs to.

Watch the filter while you draw, because the filter tells you what smoothness belongs to and the telling cannot be argued with once seen. Light a gold stick in your usual way at this table. Draw as you always draw. Watch the first inch burn. The smoke comes cooler. The tongue finds less bite. The chest loosens its fraction for seconds. Now look at the butt in the dish from the same pack yesterday. The filter stands brown from lip to middle. The stain creeps where the lips held. The white has gone the colour of tea. You believed the brown proved the filter had kept the harm from you. The fact is the brown proves the dose passed, and the hand learned to pull what the paper promised to withhold.

The men at the bench knew the hand would learn. They punched tiny holes around the filter ring so machine air would thin the draw. They printed lower numbers from machines that puffed without hunger. They knew the human hand covers those holes without knowing. They knew the human chest pulls deeper when the draw runs thin. They knew the human day adds one more stick when each stick gives less. Your hand learned without lessons. The thumb slides over the ring. The fingers close a fraction tighter. The draw runs a fraction longer. The chest holds a fraction deeper. The day runs one stick longer to keep the level where the level demands. Not every hand the same way, and not every day the same count, but the hand finds its way back to the level while calling the finding liking. You know this learning from your own table. The gold pack empties as fast as the red ever did. The dish fills as brown as ever. The stairwell catches as short as ever. What was sold as milder taught the lungs to work harder for the same topping.

Ask yourself, and answer honestly. Has the gold ever let you skip the half-hour call because the morning pleased you. Has the filter ever kept the dish from browning by night. Has the smoother throat ever kept the palm from weighing four for the afternoon and two for the evening and one for the kettle. You know the answer, and the answer breaks the bargain at its hinge. What must be topped by the clock never turned mild by the coat. It taught the hand to top harder while the coat took the credit.

That tutored doubt is the weather you breathed, because the doubt kept the hand loyal while the bench kept the dose. A lifetime of talk and pictures and friendly hands taught you that smoke relaxes and steadies and rewards, and that gold relaxes more kindly. The weather fed the hollow a meaning. The meaning fed the hand the lighter. The hand fed the level its fix. Three parts turning like wheels inside one kitchen. The level falls. The weather translates. The hand obeys.

That story is the Big Fog.

Hear that naming warmly, because the naming takes the shame off you without calling you foolish. A lifetime weather of talk taught you to hear a falling level as yourself calling for aid, and to hear gold as kindness. You heard because every voice you trusted spoke the same weather. You obeyed because the softening felt true in the chest while the bench stayed out of sight.

THE HOLE IN THE MASK

Picture the bargain from the bench side to be sure the table did not cheat you, because one picture holds the whole fraud in a single frame.

Picture a work crew handed masks against dust in a closed room. The dust hangs thick. The masks sit tight. Breathing runs hot and short. Then a man at the bench offers a kinder mask with a neat hole punched at the cheek. The air comes cooler through the hole. The tongue finds less bite. The chest thanks the hole for kindness. The crew wear the kinder masks longer. They breathe deeper through the hole to fill the chest. They stay longer in the dust because the dust feels milder. By night thebacks of the kinder masks show brown rings around the holes where the dust poured through. The lungs hold more dust than the tight masks ever let in, while the crew praise the kindness that let it pass.

You see the fraud at once from the doorway. No one calls that hole kindness. No one says the crew breathed cleaner because breathing felt cooler. No one says the brown ring proves the mask kept the dust from them. The hole made the poison cooler to pull, and the cooler pull taught deeper breathing. Praising the hole for protection would be madness while the dust still hangs.

The gold stick with its ring of holes works that hole in paper. The cooler draw is the cooler hole. The deeper pull is the deeper breath through the dust. The brown on the used filter is the brown ring around the mask hole, showing what passed while the tongue praised smoothness. We smokers wore the kinder mask and praised the bench for kindness while the bench widened the way in. Morning mask. Midday mask. Dusk mask. The masks felt kinder because the holes taught us where kindness supposedly lived. The holes were the teacher, and the teacher measured doses and not delights.

I welcome the anger that picture raises, because the anger belongs on the bench and never on the wearer. No one calls the crew weak for breathing. No one calls them foolish for trusting the kinder mask when every voice on the site praised it. They breathed as taught while the dust hung. We breathed as taught at the school wall and the park bench and the cold bus shelter where the first sticks tasted foul and the head swam. We coughed through the foulness and kept at it until the cough learned manners. Then the bench taught us to call the thinner pull protection and the topping pleasure. We believed because the smoothness felt true on the tongue while the holes stayed out of sight.

Hold that mask beside your gold pack and watch the credit change hands. The bench punched the thin draw. The thin draw taught the deeper pull. The deeper pull moved more of what the filter claimed to catch. The dish kept the brown receipt. We paid for the hole while praising the kindness. That is not a slogan. That is the table totalled in its own coin.

It is doing plenty TO you. It is doing nothing FOR you.

Say those two sentences at the table and hear how much work those plain words do. TO names the dust moved, the deeper pull taught, the level rung, the count guarded, the stairwell shortened. FOR asks for one real gift past the pause of its own debt, and the table stays silent. The silence ends the bargain without my voice raised.

THE CANS IN THE GARAGE

There is a flash from a garage down the road that this table needs before the blame lands, because the flash lets your own care accuse without my voice raised.

A mate keeps cans of butts hidden in his garage so his dog will not nose cancer from the bin, while his own chest queues behind the dog for the same smoke.

Hold that garage a breath, because one breath does the work. The cans stand in a row on the cold concrete with their lids pressed tight. The labels show beans and paint. The dog sniffs at the door and is called away kindly. The man Febreezes the coat before coming inside, for the dog and the child and the curtains. The care is real. The hands are kind. The same hands lift the next stick to lips that no can protects. The same chest takes what the cans hide from the dog. No lecture follows. The picture does not need my voice. Your own care for the small breathing at your feet tells you what the table has been moving through your own breathing while you called the moving yours.

We all lived that split. We smokers of dogs and children and clean curtains all learned to hide the tails while keeping the topping. We put the dish on the high shelf. We shut the kitchen door. We washed the hands before lifting the baby. We said we smoked because we liked it, while our own hands proved we knew what the liking moved. The hands protected every lung in the house except the one that paid for the pack. You know that split from your own sink, and you never needed my blame to feel it. The split was the bench billing you while your own kindness kept the receipt.

Now widen the indictment to the bench that built the split, because the bench built more than paper and holes.

The dealer who made the pack built the dose and the doubt together. He built the dose to lift in seconds and fall in minutes so the hand must return. He built the doubt to keep the hand from reading the return as design. He printed gold to teach milder. He printed numbers from machines that never hungered to teach safer. He paid for pictures of porches and rivers and laughing talk to teach company. He paid for pictures of desks and pauses to teach calm. He kept one flat line running through every school gate in the land that grown hands choose and young eyes admire, and said no more while the eyes learned. The dose rang the clock. The doubt translated the ringing as your nature, your taste, your need for a pause. Two hands turning one wheel. One hand measured the fall. One hand named the fall yours.

I knew those hands. I paid those hands for years while I blamed my own. We blamed the thumb for sliding over the ring. We blamed the chest for wanting the top. We blamed the temper for shortening by half past. We said we lacked the spine that non-smokers held. We said the count proved we were the kind who needed a prop. We said the garage cans proved we cared for others more than ourselves. The fact is the blame belonged on the bench from the first topping, and the bench counted on the mirror to take it. A conned hand is not a weak hand. A tutored doubt is not a verdict on your worth.

Look again at the lighter beside the pack, because the lighter belongs to the bench work as surely as the paper does and you never bought it apart from the dose. The wheel wears smooth where the thumb finds it blind in the kitchen half-light. The flint throws the same small flame through rain and wind at the yard door. The metal warms in the pocket beside the coins. You believed the lighter proved the moment was yours because your thumb chose the minute. The fact is the lighter keeps the topping within reach of the hollow, and the bench needs it there the way a till needs its drawer. No baker leaves his lighter on your table. No tea seller leaves his flame in your coat. Only the dispenser-maker leaves his fire in your palm and calls the loan your habit. We carried his fire and called the carrying ours, while the dish filled with his brown receipts.

See the foil mouth and the crest while the flame stands beside them, because the foil and the crest do bench work you never named. The foil holds the row upright for speed, so the next stick meets the hand without a pause for thought. The crest lends the air of a banknote, so the hand trusts the dose the way it trusts stamped paper. The cellophane crackles to tell the fingers something fresh waits inside. You trusted the crackle to mean care taken for you. The fact is the care was taken for the return of the hand, and the hand returned ten thousand mornings while calling the return liking. We the conned kept loyal to a paper coat while the bench kept the level, and the loyalty felt like taste because every picture named it taste.

Count what the table takes in a week, because the week shows the meter running while the day hides it. Twenty sticks a day stand as twenty hits moved through one chest. One hundred and forty hits in a week move through the same rooms where the tea pours and the talk runs. The fingers smell of the dish at night. The tongue wakes with the sour film. The throat clears on the stairs. The palm weighs the next pack before the shop shuts. You called that paying for pleasure. The fact is the hand pays the meter the company hung on your day, coin by coin, to keep the level where the bench set it. Obeying the clock never bought an hour. It rented each hour back from the fall the last dose laid, while the kitchen did the living that got billed as smoke.

Do not change from fear of that bench. I do not want you stopping because the brown frightens you or the mask sickens you. Fear never kept a hand loyal to seeing. Keep your eyes on the workmanship while the fright thins. The method takes care of fear the way daylight takes care of a locked door once seen. The seeing does the work the dread never did.

8. BLAME THE DEALER, NEVER YOURSELF
You were conned, not weak.

Carry that weighed pack through one more morning and watch who moves the hand now that the bench stands in view. The kettle boils. The mug waits. The window shows the wall and the line. The pack lies with its torn mouth. The lighter waits with its worn wheel. The hand lifts. The thumb finds the wheel. The filter meets the lip. The first draw pulls warm. The chest loosens its fraction. The clock starts its quiet count toward the next hollow. Watch the count without blame on the mirror, because the mirror never built the clock. Demand one real thing the draw adds past the pause of its own debt. Keep the kitchen. Keep the pause. Keep the tea. Bring me that watched table answered honestly from your own rooms, and the rest follows.

SUMMARY

- The pack on the table works as hardware for moving doses, with paper, filter, and gold acting as layers that carry the hit to the lungs.
- The product moved through those layers is the dose itself, built to lift fast and fade fast so the hand returns on time.
- Gold and lights feel smoother on the tongue, while the hand covers the holes, pulls deeper, and keeps the day on the same level.
- The brown on the used filter shows what passed through, not what stayed out of the chest.
- The garage cans show hands that protect every breathing in the house, which tells what the pack moves through the one chest that pays.
- The bench built the falling level and the story that named the fall as nature, so blame sits with the maker rather than the mirror.
