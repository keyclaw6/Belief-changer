### The Evening You Tell Yourself You Need It

You left the last kitchen seeing it plainly.

The taste you loved was not BAD SUGAR's. The company threw the party. BAD SUGAR was only sneaking a ride.

That was a tender one to lose. Taste felt like the last honest reason.

And as that reason falls, what comes up now? Listen.

"It's how I cope. It's how I connect. Without it I'd be tense. Without it I'd be awkward."

Do you hear that weary voice? Is it louder than the connoisseur? Does it sound less like flavour and more like need?

We speak now to P-03, who keeps a drawer of sugary somethings to get me through. We speak to P-04, who rides the roller coaster and reaches for a fix at 3pm to feel steady again. If you have ever said, "I need something right now" before you even knew what you wanted, this chapter is yours. If you have ever taken the biscuit because the room was doing it, or because the day was doing you in, this chapter is yours.

I said the same things. I told myself BAD SUGAR helped me cope. I told myself it helped me belong. I believed a sweet after a hard call made me kinder. I believed a table with cake made people warmer. I was not lying. I was misreading.

Shall we test those two beliefs against your own life, without a lecture and without shame?

We will not borrow willpower. We will not tell you to be stronger. We will look.

Have you ever told yourself, "I deserve this, I've managed to be good this week"? Have you ever stood in a break room where every hand held a biscuit and felt you would be odd without one? Have you ever finished a sweet and felt calmer for a few minutes, then more on edge than before?

You have. We all have. That pattern is not proof that BAD SUGAR helps. It is the clue that it doesn't.

### Does It Calm You — Or Does It Borrow Calm?

Let us start with cope.

Picture the stressful evening. The day has bitten. Children need attention, messages need answers, your chest is tight. You say, quite kindly to yourself, "Treat when you feel you deserve one." You open the cupboard. You eat. For a moment, the noise drops. Your shoulders lower. You breathe.

Does BAD SUGAR calm you?

Ask the simplest honest questions.

Do you feel calmer *before* you reach, or after? How long does the calm last? What do you feel an hour later when the wrapper is empty?

If it were a genuine calm, would you need another hit so soon? Would calm need topping up every hour like a phone battery?

The fact is, the calm is brief. And the unease that follows is not new stress from life. It is the come-down after the dose.

Look at what animal studies let us say plainly, and where they stop. In rats given a binge pattern, not steady nibbling, a binge can trigger dopamine in the nucleus accumbens, part of the reward circuitry. After that, a dip has been measured — lower accumbens dopamine with a rise in an opposing signal. That dip showed most clearly when the animals were given a drug that blocks the body's own opioid system. It is animal work. The size of the rise is smaller than with drugs of abuse. It is not proof of a human withdrawal diagnosis. But it shows there is a real measured low after the high in that model. 

Do you recognise that sequence? Lift. Dip. Need.

In the same work, cues — the wrapper, the smell, the corner where you always snack — can make those circuits fire again even without a bite. That gives a plausible picture for why a packet rustle can call you from the next room. It does not prove you are physically dependent. It gives language for why a cue feels like a command.

So when you feel that faint agitation on a tense evening, is it life that needs soothing? Or is it the dip after the last dose, dressed up as life?

We were taught to read every tightness as "I need something sweet." We were not taught that the sweet creates tightness, then sells it back as relief.

Does a thing that calms you leave you tighter an hour later?

Does calm need to be re-bought with another dose?

Does a genuine pleasure or treat borrow tomorrow's steadiness to rent you ten minutes tonight?

You know the answer. We all do when we watch the clock instead of the craving.

Take the isolate-the-variable test.

On the next tense evening, keep everything the same — the same hard day, the same kitchen, the same ten minutes of quiet — but change one variable. Have a glass of water. Step outside for two minutes. Speak to one person honestly. No dose. Watch what happens to the tightness.

Does the tightness pass anyway? Does your breathing still slow? Do you still finish the evening?

You do. The calm belonged to the pause, the breath, the human moment. BAD SUGAR was only sneaking a ride on your pause.

And if the calm belonged to the pause, what did BAD SUGAR do FOR you?

Nothing. It did plenty TO you. It spiked. It crashed. It taught your brain to read a normal tight evening as a sugar cue.

That is the ledger again. Doing TO you vs doing FOR you. See it with cope in the left column. On the TO side: the spike, the crash, the next craving, the lesson to reach again. On the FOR side: what? Ten minutes of borrowed quiet that the quiet would have given you for free?

Hold that. We are not finished.

### It's The Other Way Around

We need to say this flatly, because the whole trap hangs on it.

It doesn't relieve tension. It creates it.

It doesn't soothe stress. It adds a second stress and then removes it.

It doesn't steady you. It makes you unsteady and then briefly steadies the unsteadiness it made.

It's the other way around.

We spent years thinking: I am tense, therefore I need a sweet. The truth, seen from the right side, is: I had a sweet, therefore I am tense until the next one.

Do you feel how that flips the story? Can you feel the kindness in the flip? You are not a tense person. You are a person whose baseline was made tense by repeated hits.

Ask it as a question whose honest answer closes the loop.

Have you ever felt that jittery, slightly hollow, slightly restless edge before a dose and called it stress, then watched the edge vanish after the dose and called that proof that sugar works?

Have you ever felt that edge on a holiday when you ate less BAD SUGAR, and found yourself *less* tense, not more?

Have you ever watched a child who never learned the sugar fix get through the same stressful afternoon — tired, yes, grumpy, yes — but without needing a sweet to do it?

If BAD SUGAR were a genuine cope, why would the people who don't use it cope?

If BAD SUGAR truly calmed you, why would you feel worse after its calm fades?

If it gave you energy to get through, why would you need sugary somethings to get me through, again and again, forever?

The roller coaster tells the same tale. You board for the lift. The lift is real for a moment. Then you spend the ride gripping the bar. You tell yourself you needed the ride to feel better. You forgot you were fine before you queued.

We were all on that ride. We thought we were choosing relief. We were choosing the cause of the feeling we wanted relieved.

Small dip. Brief relief. Guaranteed next dip. That is not coping. That is a loop.

And the loop is small. This is not a mighty force compelling you. In that animal model, the dependency described by the team who did the work was mild but well-defined. Mild. Well-defined. Real, but small. Not a storm to survive. A faint, slightly restless tide to outwait. It peaks for days, not lifetimes. Many self-described yo-yoers put that cycle in plain words: quick lift, fast drop. Lived description, not diagnosis. No glucose panic. No disease. Just the pattern we felt and finally named.

If the physical part is faint, why does it feel so strong?

Because the belief is loud.

We will meet that belief by name in the next chapter. For now, hear only this: the agitation you call stress after a dose is not proof you need a dose. It is proof the last dose is wearing off.

It causes the aggravation. It does not relieve it.

The relief you feel from a sweet is not BAD SUGAR doing something FOR you. It is BAD SUGAR stopping, for a moment, what it was doing TO you.

See that and the whole cope story loses its floor.

### The Room Where Everyone Is Grazing

Now the second half of the lie: connect.

You know the scene. Late afternoon meeting. Break room. Birthday cakes. Someone says, "Go on, just one won't hurt." Everyone holds a plate. You hold a plate because they hold a plate. It feels like belonging.

Or the party. Warm light. People laughing. A table of biscuits, chocolate, pastries. You tell yourself dessert IS the point of the day. Without the sweet, you would be outside the circle.

Does BAD SUGAR connect you?

We will do the same isolate-the-variable test with people.

Keep the room. Keep the laughter. Keep the ritual. Change one variable. Remove BAD SUGAR and change nothing else.

Would the room still be warm? Would the friends still be friends? Would the birthday still be a birthday?

Have you ever been at a gathering where you had no BAD SUGAR — because you were full, or ill, or simply not hungry — and still felt entirely with people? Have you ever laughed for an hour and realized you had not touched the table at all?

Have you ever watched someone who doesn't eat BAD SUGAR enjoy the same party, eyes bright, fully included, missing nothing but the fix?

Have you ever noticed that after the cake is eaten, the connection you remember is the talk, the hug, the joke — not the sugar?

We were taught to glue ritual to dose. The glue was not real. The ritual was.

I remember standing at a work birthday, plate in hand because everyone had a plate, smiling while calculating whether taking one would be rude and whether not taking one would be ruder. That is not belonging. That is a trap negotiating with you in the language of manners.

Take BAD SUGAR out of that scene and what is left?

People. Timing. Music. Light. The relief of pausing together. The pleasure of being known. That is connection.

What did BAD SUGAR add to the conversation? Did it introduce you? Did it make the joke funnier? Did it hold your friend's hand?

No. It sat on the table and took credit.

Have you ever thought a party was better *because* of the cake, when later you could not recall the cake at all but could recall who stood beside you?

Have you ever felt *less* connected after the grazing — slightly foggy, slightly regretful, slightly distant — and called that social?

If BAD SUGAR connected you, why would you ever feel lonely while eating it alone at night, secret and quiet, out of sight of everyone?

We both know the answer. Connection is people and ritual. BAD SUGAR was only sneaking a ride on your belonging.

There is another honest distinction here. Sweetness as taste is not the enemy. Ripe fruit at that same table can be complete on its own. Plain dairy can be complete. They need no support. BAD SUGAR — the refined, added, processed carbs we mean when we say sugar in this book — is different. It rarely arrives alone. It arrives with butter, salt, chocolate, cream, crunch, fizz. It borrows the gathering's warmth just as it borrows fat's flavour. Two borrowings, same trick.

Do you see the ledger again?

Doing TO you vs doing FOR you, but now in the social column.

TO you: the nag to take, the self-talk about "once a week is fine," the split attention between people and plate, the next-day story that you were good or bad.

FOR you: what? People were already there. Laughter was already happening. Ritual was already holding you.

Nothing. Empty column. Same as before.

### The Tight Shoes You Forgot You Were Wearing

I promised you one picture that does the whole argument in a single body-memory. Here it is.

Imagine you wear shoes a size too small from morning until night. You lace them tight. You walk. You work. You stand in that break room, smiling with tight shoes pinching.

What do you feel by five o'clock? A dull ache. Restlessness. Irritability. You cannot name it. You only know you want relief.

Now you untie the laces and slip the shoes off. What do you feel? Relief. Sighing. "Ah."

Do you call that pleasure? Would you say, "I love tight shoes, they relax me"? Would you say, "Tight shoes help me cope with my day and connect me to my colleagues"?

You would not. You would say, "The relief is real. The shoes are the problem."

That is the entire Sugar Trap in one foot.

We lace the shoes with each dose. The lace is BAD SUGAR. The pinch is the dip — that faint, slightly restless edge, the spike then crash, the cue firing when you see the tin. The relief you feel when you unwrap the chocolate is not BAD SUGAR giving you something. It is BAD SUGAR briefly letting you take off the shoes it put on you.

You boarded the roller coaster to feel lift. You wore tight shoes to feel relief.

You never rose above the baseline of a person who never started. You only relieved, for a moment, the discomfort BAD SUGAR created, then guaranteed it would return.

Ask the honest question that collapses the last excuse.

If BAD SUGAR truly helped you cope, would you need to cope more after it?

If it truly helped you connect, would you need to slip away alone to eat it secretly, unseen?

If it were a genuine pleasure or treat, would it need to create discomfort first to be felt as pleasure at all?

The fact is, non-users cope. Non-users connect. They do it without tight shoes. They have bad days, hard evenings, awkward rooms — and they get through them without needing to untie anything, because they never laced it.

You can too. You already know how. You cope with every other stress that has no fix — a late train, a child's cry — without a dose. You connect with people over a walk, a joke, a hand on a shoulder, with no plate at all. Those capacities are already inside you.

We were not weak-willed. We were pinched and told the pinch was life.

See it. Name it. It is the other way around.

### What Was Really Holding You

Let us land this without hedging, because this benefit was the last foothold. After this, every item on the FOR side has been examined and found empty. Taste fell. Energy fell. Treat fell. Now cope and connect fall.

You told yourself BAD SUGAR relaxes you, steadies you, carries you through, and makes you belong.

Your own life contradicts you.

The calm after a dose is brief and must be bought again. That is not calm. That is a loop.

The room where everyone grazes is warm because of people and ritual, not because of the table. When you remove BAD SUGAR and keep people, warmth remains. When you keep BAD SUGAR and remove people, what is left? A wrapper alone at night. That is not connection.

The rescue you feel is the rescuer-as-perpetrator. Grateful to the one who pushed you in for pulling you out.

We were not foolish. We were conned on phoney information, young, before we could choose. The trap taught us to read a learned dip as life and to read a borrowed pause as help.

So let us give the credit back to its owners, plainly, with no softening.

The pause gave you the calm. The breath gave you the calm. The friend gave you the belonging. The music, the light, the joke, the day itself gave you the belonging.

What did BAD SUGAR give you?

It gave you tight shoes, then charged you for taking them off.

It did TO you what you thought it did FOR you.

Let that settle in two short beats. No second thesis.

It caused the tension. It took credit for the relief.

If that is true, what will you miss? If the tension was the price of the dose, and the company was there without it, what does stopping take from a tense evening or a friendly room?

Nothing.

You do not need a replacement ritual. You do not need a stronger excuse. You need clear seeing. And you have it.

I know the voice that will come later, because it came to me in the same room. It will say, "But I need something right now." When it comes, answer it with what you now know.

Do you need something? Or do you feel the faint, slightly restless pinch of the shoes and misread it as need?

Is the room missing sugar? Or are you fully in the room and the sugar is missing you?

Is this cope? Or is this taking off tight shoes you no longer need to wear?

You already know.

Think of the next ordinary evening. Hard day. Kitchen light. Same tightness.

This time, do not lace the shoes. Make tea. Sit. Speak. Breathe. Let the evening be an evening, not a cue.

And think of the next ordinary gathering. People you like. Table full or not. Keep everything. Change nothing else in your life. Stand there without a plate if you want to. Stand there with fruit if you want to. Notice what is left when BAD SUGAR is not sneaking a ride.

People. That's what is left. Always people.

And that is not loss. That is relief.
