Chapter 12
ORDINARY DAYS

*You eat when hungry, stop when satisfied, and walk past old doses like they belong to someone else.*

### THE FIRST MORNING

You wake to a quiet house.

No foil on the pillow. No sour mouth that needs brushing before you can speak. No drawer calling from the kitchen while the kettle warms. The tongue lies clearer. The belly lies level. Hunger talks plain, the way hunger talked when you were small.

We know that hunted morning well. We woke furry and foggy. We told ourselves we needed sugary somethings to get me through the day ahead. We opened the cupboard before the kettle boiled and told ourselves it was hunger. It was never hunger. That old hunt was the Sugar Trap talking, not hunger.

Sit at the table this morning and let hunger lead.

Pour tea. Cut bread. Butter it. Slice apple. Eat slowly because the belly asks, not because the clock orders. Note how bread closes. Note how apple sounds loud after years of brown noise. Note how a mouth that told truth all night keeps telling truth at seven.

You will hear a small voice, half curious, half wary: “Is this all? Do I watch myself all day now?”

I hear you. I stood in my own kitchen the first morning with palms flat on wood and waited for the pull to shout. It did not shout. It muttered from far off, thin, twitchy, like a radio left on in another room. I smiled at it because I knew what it was and what it was not.

Ask that mutter straight.

When did morning ever need brown stuff to start — or did mornings start for years before the first dose taught you to doubt them? When did bread ever leave you hunted — or did hunt always follow the sweet dose, never the plain meal? When did hunger ever lie to you the way the wrapper lied — or did hunger always point to food and then go quiet when food arrived?

Only one answer fits mornings you have lived. Mornings never needed sweetening. Hunger never needed tutoring. The dose opened what the meal closed.

You eat and stop the way a small child drops a peach mid-bite to run off.

If you live with diabetes or take medicine that touches blood sugar, follow your clinician first and use this book for belief only.

That is the whole morning job. Nothing to tidy. Nothing to count. Nothing to carry. Breakfast closes hunger. Hunger returns at lunch. Lunch will close it again. The body steadies to a level hum when brown noise stops.

Was the lift ever energy — or was it a spike that borrowed from the afternoon? You know now. It was borrowing. This morning borrows nothing. Tea tastes of tea. Water tastes of water. Bread tastes of wheat and butter and salt. The mouth reports without story, and the report is good.

We spent years saying, treat when you feel you deserve one, as if deserved closed hunger. It never closed hunger. It opened hunt. This table closes hunger because hunger was the only thing talking.

Leave the drawer shut by not thinking about it. Wash the plate. Wipe the table. Put the bread away. Walk to the window with tea and look out at the street waking. Buses pass. A neighbour laughs. Milk bottles clink. The hour you once filled with rustle and chew fills itself with ordinary doings, and ordinary doings hold.

The fact is you are not watching yourself. You are eating.

### THE AISLE AND THE TILL

Midday you walk the shop.

Bright packs shout from low shelves. Cartoon faces grin. Till-height bars lean toward the hand. Sweet smell pumps from the bakery corner. Hands around you reach, drop bars in baskets, reach again.

We walked that aisle hunted. We read labels we did not believe. We promised small and bought family-size. We told ourselves, I've managed to be good this week, so one small bar harms nothing, while the hand already held two. We paid and ate half before the car door shut.

Walk it today with new eyes.

Push the trolley slow. Look at the packs. Small, brown, bright, tuned to a bliss-point by people who never met you yet counted your hand. See the price tickets. See the hours priced beside them — money, time, fog, furry teeth, evening belly-talk. It does plenty TO you. It does nothing FOR you.

You will see a mother drop chocolate in for a tired child. You will see a man at the till add two bars to petrol. You will see a girl weighing family-size against small and choosing family-size because the small never holds.

Does that look like enjoyment to you? Ask plain.

When did that trolley ever roll out level — or did it always roll out hunted, with wrappers already empty before supper? When did that till treat ever stay small — or did the sliver always carry the binge once schedule met dose? When did that bright pack ever pay back its price — or did it always bill after, in fog and thirst and second reach?

That is the pattern shops taught you. The pack never paid. It billed.

Let pity rise, not hunger. Those hands are not indulging. They are feeding a loop they did not choose with eyes open. You were conned the same way. You know the hand moving before decision, the cupboard half-empty at ten at night, the mouth saying enough while the hand keeps moving. Warmth belongs to the person. Harshness belongs to the con that set the shelf at hand height.

Say nothing. Buy what hunger asked for. Bread. Soup. Greens from the market. Fruit that smells of itself. Cheese. Fish. Rice. Food that closes hunger and then lets you leave the table. No lecture at the till. No sermon to the mother. No warning to the man with petrol. Visible quiet does more than talk. Talk drags you back into argument and argument reopens what sight closed. Keep your mouth for tea and apples.

That twitch at the old corner of the aisle is the Nibbler dying, not calling.

Note it and walk on. Mouth waters at wrapper rustle. Hand lifts half an inch toward old height. Head says sweet o’clock though belly sits quiet. That is all. A bell ringing though dinner never comes. Learned firing, not orders. Let it ring unfed and it dulls by the next aisle. By cheese it has gone. By apples you have forgotten to listen for it.

Pay. Pack. Take the bags. Feel how light the hands are when they carry food, not hunt.

Walk out into daylight with the bags in both hands. No second bag to hide. No receipt to blur. No eating in the car to blunt the drive home. The car smells of bread and greens. The head stays bright behind the eyes. The belly stays quiet under the ribs.

Drive home the ordinary way. Traffic lights. Radio low. A bus pulls out. A child crosses with a satchel. Your hands stay on the wheel. Nothing in you bargains for one for the road. Nothing in you rehearses the wrapper in the glove box. The road is only road. That plainness is the proof. Freedom does not need a ceremony between shop and house. It rides quiet in the passenger seat with the bags.

Pull up. Carry the bags up the path. The door clicks. The kitchen waits as you left it, plate washed, table wiped, morning crumbs gone. Set the bags on the counter and unpack without hurry. Bread in the bin. Greens in the drawer. Apples in the bowl where they catch light. Soup on the shelf. Cheese wrapped. Fish cold. Rice back in its jar. Your hands know where things live. Nothing is rearranged to avoid anything. Nothing is hidden on a high shelf. The drawer that once hid bars now holds pens and string and batteries, and you open it for string without a second thought.

Wipe the counter. Rinse a glass. Put the kettle on because you fancy tea, not because you need a ritual to get through. Look at the table, clear in midday light. This is change-nothing-else lived, not as an idea but as lino under shoes, tap running, bags folded and put behind the bin for next time.

And then, while the tea brews, hunger talks.

Not the twitch. Not the head-hint from the aisle. Plain hunger from a breakfast well closed hours ago, a hollow ease under the ribs that points to food. Sit down to it. Cut bread again. Pour soup hot. Eat hungry and notice. Salt steadies. Warm spreads. Chewing slows on its own. The belly closes quiet, the way a door closes when it is shut, not slammed. Stop when satisfied. Leave the last spoon because you are satisfied, not because a rule says leave it. Push the bowl an inch away. Drink tea.

Wash up. Put the board away. The kitchen resets to clear again. No fog gathers behind the eyes. No thirst wakes behind sweet. No second reach writes itself on the afternoon. The house ticks on — clock, fridge hum, pigeons on the sill — and you are in it, not watching it.

The fact is the shop lost its orders, and the kitchen never picked them up.

### THE THREE O'CLOCK HOUR

Three comes with its old bell.

Desk warm. Eyes dry. Work thick. Drawer where bars lived now holds pens and string. The hour that once meant ten-minute buzz and four o’clock fog rings thin. Mouth hints sweet. Head hints lift. Hand remembers the walk to the machine.

We lived that hour yo-yo. We took a sweet top-up at three, buzzed till half past, fogged by four, reached again at half past four, called it short term lift then fast drop and still called the lift help. We said we needed it to function. We said it helped concentration. It never helped. It lent and took back with interest.

Sit through three today and watch what three does without a dose.

Drink water. Stand. Open the window. Roll shoulders. Eat nothing because hunger has not talked since lunch closed clean. Let the thin want talk past you. It says, I-need-to-eat-something-RIGHT-NOW. It means, feed me though belly sits quiet. Hear it as echo, not need.

You know this feeling. That empty, twitchy, slightly shaky, need-something-sweet-now feeling once passed for orders. It passes now for what it is — a tiny creature unfed, grumbling as it thins.

Ask it straight, the way you learned to ask everything.

When did the three o’clock dose ever carry you to five level — or did it always drop you by four and sell you the second dose as rescue? When did the buzz ever build work — or did work always wait while fog thickened and hands reached? When did the drawer ever close hunger — or did hunger always live at meal times while the drawer lived between them?

Afternoons you have lived answer without hesitation. The dose never carried. It dropped and billed the drop as need.

It is the other way around. The low made the lift look like help. The scene gave what the dose claimed.

Do not push the thought away. You cannot not think a thought by ordering it gone. Let it come and give it the answer you own. When the sweet thought crosses at the desk, think at once with a grin that reaches the chest:

BRILLIANT! I'M FREE!

Say it under breath at three. Say it while water cools the mouth. Say it while eyes clear without brown help. Each time the thought comes it brings less pull and more grin, until one evening you notice the thought has not come for days and you laugh at the quiet.

I tell you from my own desk. I kept pens where bars slept. First day the hand opened at three from habit and closed again while I laughed at old wiring. Second day the mouth watered at wrapper rustle two desks down and dried by the time the kettle boiled. Third day three passed without ringing and I looked up at half past four surprised that work had moved. No props held me. No counting held me. Sight held me.

Change nothing else in this hour. Keep work, chair, window, tea. Keep the walk to stretch legs. Keep meals at meal times. The baseline returns on its own when doses stop shouting over it. Hunger points to food. Thirst points to water. Tired points to air and rest and night sleep, not to brown stuff that borrows from evening.

Short verdict for three: the bell rings, dinner never comes, you keep typing.

### THE EVENING MEAL AND THE SOFA

Evening is the kindest test because evening was the dearest lie.

Supper steams. Soup, bread, market vegetables cooked plain, fruit after. Hunger talks after the day. You sit. You eat. You stop when satisfied. The belly closes quiet. Tea tastes wonderful? No — tea tastes clean and loud because the mouth tells truth.

We ate evenings hunted. We closed supper and opened hunt within the hour. We told ourselves food is love and celebrating with chocolate proved love. We carried bowls to the sofa and ate while scrolling, while answering messages, while planning tomorrow, then blamed the day for the fog that followed the dose.

Eat this evening with eyes open.

Serve supper hungry. Taste salt, oil, bread crust, green bite, apple skin. Note how ordinary food eaten hungry closes, while brown stuff eaten full opens. Note how company gives warmth, lamplight gives soft, laughter gives lift — the dimmed lights and shared laughter once billed to chocolate in hand gave everything while the dose sneaked a ride.

That is the whole strongest lie undone without a fight. The room sang. The people sang. The story on the screen sang. The dose rode along and billed the night after. Take the dose out of that room in memory and the room still sings. Put the dose alone at a bare table at ten and nothing sings. Furry teeth. Thirst behind. Sour note under ribs. Head slow behind eyes. Who gave and who took? The room gave. The dose took.

After supper, sit where hunt used to sit.

Kettle clicks. Neighbour laughs through the wall. Dishes wait. Hands lie still. No cupboard opening. No second opening to check the first opening. At nine the old hour rings faint — sweet o’clock — and you hear it as a bell with no dinner behind it. You grin in lamplight and think the thought you own.

BRILLIANT! I'M FREE!

Do not preach to the house. If others take dessert, let them. If a partner offers from love, thank love and keep tea. Say, no thanks, I am happy with this, and mean food, not lack. No sermon on bliss-point. No warning on teeth. No tale of your morning tongue. The book changed your belief; argument will not change theirs while the con still talks. Live the sofa quiet and let quiet do the talking. If they ask, tell plain what you saw — lift makes low, room gives glow — and leave it. If they scoff, smile. You scoffed once.

Keep everything else exactly where it was. Same sofa. Same cup. Same walk to the sink. Same bedtime. Same shop tomorrow. Freedom is not a new life built from props. It is the old life lived with old doses gone. Eat what hunger asks. Stop when satisfied. Drink water when thirsty. Sleep when tired. No replacement system holds you. The body holds you.

Ask the sofa straight when memory glows.

When did the box ever close an evening — or did evenings always close best before the box opened? When did the second biscuit ever taste better than the first — or duller, more driven, more hurried? When did the night ever need sweetening to feel kind — or did kind always live in rest, warmth, people, while sweet billed after?

Evenings you have lived give a single reply. Nothing was given. Much was taken.

Short verdict for evening: the room sings, the wrapper rustles elsewhere, you stay.

### IF THE HAND TAKES A DOSE

Hear this well because it guards the rest.

A slip does not rebuild the con unless you let belief back in. The body absorbs an accidental crumb. The mind must not steer toward it. A seatbelt exists for a jolt, not for driving wild. If the hand takes from habit — a biscuit at a meeting, a bite offered and taken before thought wakes — do not wail. Do not count days. Do not call it proof.

We all fumbled from habit in early days. I took a wrapped mint at a till from old wiring, chewed half, tasted cloy and coat, spat the thought out with the mint. No trap rebuilt that day because belief did not return. The tail reported — furry, too-sweet, thirst waking — and sight grew warmer for the lesson.

If it happens to you, do this.

Spit or swallow and stop. Note the tail without scolding. Tongue coats. Thirst stands. Belly talks though supper closed. That is not pleasure returning. That is the old bill presenting itself for one dose. Ask what it did FOR you that people, meals, rest had not already done cleaner. None stands. It did plenty TO you in one small brown square. Let the rumble strip warn, not crash. Slow. Steer back to centre by sight, not by vow of sorrow. The next hunger points to food. Food closes it. The echo thins again within hours.

Hear the two doors that lie after a slip and do not open them.

“I got away with it, so one each week harms nothing.” When did one ever stay one — or did the sliver always carry the binge once timing woke? “I blew it, so I may as well start dating again.” When did a jolt ever mean the belt failed — or did the belt hold precisely because you did not steer toward the ditch? Roads you have driven show the difference plain. A deliberate date rebuilds. An accidental jolt observed rebuilds nothing.

You guard belief, not behaviour with panic. Belief is the giant. The tiny echo starves when belief stays down. Both ropes you once felt — have it, you need it, and you cannot stop, you will fail — belong to the same hands. Cut the middle by sight and both fall slack. There is no outside to fear. Only habit pacing where a door stands open.

Do you feel the weight off shoulders when dating stops? You do. That is arithmetic, not courage. Arithmetic needs eyes only.

So keep the line clean around BAD SUGAR. No healthy level is sought inside it. Natural sugars inside whole fruit, plain vegetables, plain meals belong to hunger and satisfaction and stay. Brown stuff, sweet drinks, biscuits, cakes, desserts, sweetened cereals and grazing doses of starchy snack-carbs eaten as sweet hits do not cross. An accidental blip passes. A deliberate one is the con talking with a calendar. See it as script, not reasoning, and it loses voice.

You are not on watch. You are at table, in aisle, at desk, on sofa, living hours that waiting always stole. A passing thought is a dead enemy, not an order.

**SUMMARY**
- Morning hunger points to food, and food closes hunger without brown help.
- The shop aisle shouts, yet packs bill after and give nothing during.
- Three o’clock rings thin and passes, while work moves without a top-up.
- Evening warmth lives in people, lamplight and meals, not in the wrapper.
- A thin echo for a few days thins further when left unfed and unobeyed.
- An accidental bite teaches by its tail and rebuilds nothing when belief stays down.
- A passing sweet thought gets one owned answer and then leaves quiet behind.
