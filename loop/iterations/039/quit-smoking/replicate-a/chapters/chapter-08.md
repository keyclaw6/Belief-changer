Chapter 8
THE KETTLE, THE PLATE, THE OPEN DOOR

*The morning, the meal and the break were whole all along — the dose added nothing to them.*

## THE FEET BEFORE THE FLOOR

We know that first minute blindfold.

Alarm. Eyes not open. Feet have not touched the floor and the hand has already found the pack on the chair. Lighter cold in the palm. First drag on empty lungs before the bladder, before water, before a word spoken. Chest tightens, head swims a shade, throat bites, and we call that swim waking.

I did that for years. I believed I could not start until paper started me. I believed the day had a lock and only a light held the key.

Stand in that minute with me now and watch what starts you.

Feet on cold lino. The shock of it runs up the ankles and into the calves and you are awake before you have decided to be awake. Spine lengthening after night flat, shoulders rolling back, jaw unclenching. Mouth dry from sleep, tasting of sleep, not of ash. You stand. You are standing. Who stood you up? Not paper. Legs, balance, breath.

You walk to the kitchen. Bare feet, floorboards, the small ache in the lower back that eases as you move. You fill the kettle. You hear the click, then the rising hum. You stand and wait while it builds. Steam touches the face. You draw the first full breath of the day down to the bottom of the ribs and it moves without a rattle. Cough nothing. Clear nothing. Only air in, air out, ribs wide.

That breath is yours. No paper taught it.

“I can’t start without the first one. Mornings are impossible.”

I hear you, because that was my voice. Let your own mornings answer.

If the first dose truly woke you, why did you wake enough to find it, light it and carry it before it touched you? Who walked to the chair? Who struck the lighter? Who drew? Waking came first. Paper arrived second and charged for the walk.

If the first dose truly cleared the head, why did the head clear more with water, with piss, with the kettle hum and the cold floor underfoot than with the bite that left the mouth sour and the chest a shade tight? Watch it tomorrow with clear eyes. Piss long. Rinse the mouth. Drink water slow and feel it go down cold. Splash the face. The fog thins from the edges. The eyes come on. None of that came from a pack.

Watch the taste. On a smoking morning the mouth wakes dry and we cover dry with ash and call the cover flavour. Tongue furred, lips dry, throat already rasping from last night’s last dose, and we lay smoke over it and say, ah, that’s better. Better than what? Better than the dry we never washed away. On a clear morning the mouth wakes dry and water tastes sweet and tea tastes of tea and the tongue wakes with the day. The first sip of tea spreads. Bitter, hot, alive. The second sip deeper. Which mouth is more awake? The washed mouth or the ashed mouth?

Watch the hands. On a smoking morning the hands shake a fraction until the first dose and we read the shake as need for paper. On a clear morning the hands are still for the kettle, steady for the cup, sure for the tap. The shake was not morning. The shake was night without a dose catching up, the small empty nip grown loud by sleep. Feed it and it quiets for minutes. Wash, move, breathe and it quiets because morning itself carries you forward.

Watch the chest. That first drag on empty lungs never filled them. It narrowed them. Head swim is not waking. Head swim is a brief lack of clear air called a lift. A non-smoker never needs to swim to stand. He stands, he breathes, he moves. We stood, we smoked, we coughed, and we told ourselves the cough was the engine starting.

The first dose never woke the morning. The morning woke you and paper rode on its back.

Stand it again slower, because this minute holds the whole book.

Feet to lino. Cold to skin. Spine to length. Kettle to hum. Steam to face. Water to mouth. Breath to bottom ribs. Piss, wash, swallow, breathe. Five small acts, each older than any pack, each doing its own waking work. Paper stood beside them and took the credit for all five, the way a man standing on a station platform takes credit for the train arriving.

Ask it over your own floor.

Who woke before the light?
What cleared before the bite?
Which breath went deeper — the smoked one that tightened, or the clear one that widened?

The morning did not need lighting. It needed living.

## THE PLATE STILL WARM

Then the meal.

We all know the table point. Last mouthful down. Plate still warm. The taste lingering that the cook worked for — fat, salt, lemon, gravy, the crisp edge, the soft middle. Belly full, shoulders down, talk easy. And before the plate is cleared the hand is in the pocket. We light over the plate. We dull the tongue we just fed and call the dulling the finish.

That old pellet after the paragraph, after the drive, after the plate — we paid every finished thing with a light.

You fear the meal will fall flat without the full stop. You say it in your own words and I will not mock it, because I said it:

“After food I must have one. Otherwise the meal isn’t finished.”

So finish a meal in your head with me and stay to the taste.

You eat. You chew slow at the end. You put the fork down. The mouth is full of the meal, warm and particular. You sit. You do not rush. You breathe through the nose. The flavour stays five minutes, ten minutes, changing as it fades, the way a note stays in a room after the string is struck. Garlic first, then lemon behind it, then fat smooth over both. You drink water. The water lifts the flavour again. You talk. You laugh with the mouth still tasting. You pick the last crisp edge with fingers and it cracks. You lick salt from the thumb.

That lingering is the finish. No paper wrote it. Hunger satisfied wrote it. Cook wrote it. Your own tongue wrote it.

Now put paper into that picture and see what it does. The first drag burns the tongue, dries the palate, lays ash over lemon and gravy. The lingering note is gone in one draw. The nose fills with smoke instead of food. The mouth that held garlic and lemon now holds sour. The throat that was easy now works a shade harder. The company that was warm now waits while you step to the door or waves smoke from the plate. What exactly did the finish finish? It finished the taste.

I ate my best meals as a smoker with a dulled tongue and told myself I loved food. I told friends about restaurants while tasting only the first three mouthfuls clearly and the rest through a veil. I taste more now in one dinner than I tasted in a month of lit finishes. Not because food changed. Because the veil stopped dropping over the plate.

Ask it plain over your own plate.

If the dose completed the meal, why does food taste stronger, longer and cleaner when no dose comes after it?
If the dose was the pleasure of eating, why do you remember the meal and forget the dose, except for the sour mouth and the need to air the room after?
If the dose helped digestion or settling, why does the belly sit heavier, the chest tighter, the mouth sourer after the light than before it?

Look at the hands again. After the meal the hands want to rest. Fork down, cup held, thumb along the warm ceramic. We trained the hands to want a stick between fingers at that moment and called the training hunger. It was not hunger. Belly was full. It was timetable. The last dose had faded during the meal and the small empty signal had begun to stir under the fullness. We lit to quiet the stir and told ourselves we lit to honour the cook.

The party was work. The flavour was food. Paper sneaked a ride on hunger satisfied and called the ride dessert.

Leave the plate to linger and the lingering does its own work. Five minutes becomes ten. Talk stretches. Belly settles by itself, the way bellies do. Tongue stays clean. Nose stays open to coffee, to peel, to the sweet that comes next tasting of itself and not of ash. Nothing is missing. The full stop was already written by the last mouthful. Paper only blotted it.

## THE DOORWAY AT ELEVEN

Then the break.

Ten forty. Screen ache. Shoulders up round the ears. Eyes dry from looking short too long. The mind offers its old translation on cue: step out, get air, take paper, clear the head. We stood in that doorway a thousand times, shoulder to the brick, dose between fingers, watching the street and calling the watching rest.

Take that doorway with me now with empty hands.

You push the door. Cold air hits the face. You hear traffic, a gull, heels on pavement, a shutter half up down the road. You roll the shoulders. You fill the chest to the bottom. The ache behind the eyes thins because the eyes look far for a minute and the legs move and blood moves. You lean nothing on nothing. You stand five minutes and look at nothing useful. Thoughts settle because you stopped feeding them paper for five minutes.

That pause was air. The rest was distance. The clear head was blood moving while the eyes looked far. Paper stood in the doorway with you and took the credit for the street.

What stirs underneath is an empty, slightly restless, slightly edgy little tug.

That is all. A faint emptiness under the ribs. Hands a fraction too idle. Skin a shade too tight. Not pain. Not collapse. The same small signal you learned to read as now-you-need-it.

Left alone it frets, peaks, passes. Attention goes to the bus going past, to the cold on the ears, to the next job waiting inside. For minutes you forget it entirely. Then it flickers back fainter. That wave is typical — as a rule sharpest in the first days without a dose and usually softening within weeks, not the same to the day for every body, bodies and days differ — and I speak of belief here, not of your body's care; if you feel ill or you worry, speak to your own clinician for your own care.

The tug never stopped you typing, driving, carrying shopping, laughing at the joke. That stairs-breath pinch on the second flight told on the lungs, not on the need. A real need interrupts act. This only colours while you get on with everything. You still answer, still carry, still climb, still laugh while it nips. That is the signature of a small signal carrying a large false story.

Try it over this doorway.

If the break needed paper, why does the air clear the head when paper stays inside? Why do the shoulders drop with movement and distance and cold on the skin, none of which came from a pack?
If paper rested you, why did every break end with the same restlessness scheduling the next break within the hour? What rest leaves you needing rest again before the hour turns?
If paper helped thinking, why do the best thoughts in that five minutes arrive when you look far and breathe deep, not when you draw and cough?

Watch the hands again, because the hands tell truth here. With a dose the hands hold one thing and do nothing else. Fingers pinched, wrist cocked, ash watched. Without, the hands hang, then move. In pockets for warmth, out for cold, thumb rubbing the ear against the chill, palm flat on the brick feeling its rough cold. They are not idle. They are unheld, and unheld is not empty. Empty-handed is not missing. It is open.

Watch the nose. With a dose the nose holds smoke. Without, the nose holds street. Diesel, rain on stone, coffee from the shop two doors down, someone’s toast. None of it grand. All of it real, and real is what restores. The brain that stared short all morning stretches on far and wide and different. That stretch is the rest. Paper never stretched anything. It narrowed the chest while the eyes happened to look far, then charged for the looking.

Stay the five minutes out. Do not cut it short to prove anything. Let the cold do its work. Let the legs shift weight. Let the jaw unclench. The small tug will talk in the background while you live in the foreground. You will hear it the way you hear traffic — there, not orders. It frets, it peaks, it passes, it returns fainter. You do not answer it. You answer the street, the air, the next job waiting inside that will go better for far looking and deep breathing.

## BACK INSIDE WITH EMPTY HANDS

You stand five minutes and look at nothing useful. Thoughts settle because you stopped feeding them paper for five minutes.

Now come back inside with me, because the proof does not end at the door. The fear says: fine for five minutes, but the hour will flatten. The work will drag. The taste will die. The tug will grow until you cannot think. So stay the next hour with empty hands and see what a complete moment does next when you do not interrupt it.

Push the door. Warmth again. Screen glow. Chair cool under you. You sit. Hands free, nothing pinched, nothing watched, nothing timed. The cup from before is still warm enough. You lift it. Coffee tastes of coffee, not of ash over coffee. The flavour from lunch still lingers at the back of the tongue, faint now, clean — garlic gone, lemon a memory, only a smooth fullness that water lifts again when you sip. No sour over it. No veil dropping mid-morning.

You turn to the next job. Mail, figures, the paragraph that needs finishing, the call that needs making. You start. Not with a lift. With attention.

First ten minutes: the body settles to the chair. Shoulders that dropped outside stay dropped. Breath taken deep without a rattle stays deep because nothing has narrowed it. You read the same mail once and understand it. You answer in three lines. Done. The small tug flickers in the background, faint as radio from another room. Hands feel a fraction too idle for a second, then find the keys, the pen, the edge of the cup. They do not need holding. They hold what work gives them.

Second ten minutes: you hit the snag. Figure wrong, wording wrong, customer short on the phone. The old translation offers itself instant: step out, light, cope. Watch it offer and watch what happens when you do not obey. Shoulders tighten with the snag — because snags tighten shoulders — then loosen as you deal. You re-add the column. You re-write the line. You keep the voice even on the call while the ear reddens. None of it needs smoke. The snag sits on the pad, you touch it, it moves. The tug nips while you do it, then fades while you do it, because attention is on the snag, not on the nip. That is the whole secret of the hour: foreground lives, background dies.

Third ten minutes: you stand for water without deciding to stand for paper. Legs straighten. Back eases. You drink slow at the sink. Water tastes sweet after coffee. You look out the window while you swallow. Far again for twenty seconds. Eyes moisten, ease. You hear the kettle in another room, someone laughing, heels again. You go back. No coin dropped. No meter fed. The needle never entered your thinking because you never rented it.

Fourth ten minutes: the paragraph comes. You write it clean in one pass. You read it back and it holds. That glow in the chest as you lean back — whose is it? We used to sign it over to paper. Finished, therefore light, therefore glow belongs to light. Watch it unsigned. Chest easy, mouth clean, hands still on the desk, breath wide. The glow belongs to finished. Work did that. You did that. Paper would only have burned the tongue that now tastes water sweet and laid ash over the minute you now own whole.

Fifth ten minutes: the tug returns fainter. Same ajar window under the ribs, thinner now. You notice because I asked you to notice, not because it stops you. You type through it. You sip through it. You answer the mate through it and laugh at the joke through it and the laugh is full because the chest is not tight. If the tug were you, you could not laugh through you. If the tug were need, need would interrupt act. It does not interrupt. It colours faintly while you get on with everything, and colouring is not command.

Sixth ten minutes: the hour closes. Look back over it. One mail answered, one figure righted, one paragraph written, one call held even, water drunk sweet, flavour lingered clean to its natural end, breath deep all hour without tightening, hands holding only what work and cup gave them. No break needed to repair the break. No coin to repeat the coin. The fuller pleasure did not flash and die at the doorway. It persisted across the next hour without a new decision, because you made no new decision against it. You only lived and it stayed.

That is what complete does next. It continues.

Ask it over that hour, with your own work as witness.

If empty moments were flat, why did this hour hold more flavour, more air, more done work than any lit hour you remember?
If the tug were growing torment, why did it grow fainter while you worked, laughed, drank, typed — doing everything it said you could not do?
If paper gave the glow, why did the glow arrive fuller with nothing lit, and stay longer with nothing fed?

We feared the grey stretch. Unlit, the stretch fills with its own detail: steam, flavour, cold, sound, breath, done work. Lit, the stretch thins to one repeated act: sour mouth, tight chest, tug rebuilding before the hour turns, doorway again, coin again. Which hour was empty? The one with everything in it, or the one with one thing in it twenty times?

Stay another twenty minutes in your head and the shape holds. The cup empties. The tongue still clean. The chest still wide. The hands still doing. The small empty signal still fretting faint in the background, still unfed, still starving a little more each hour, still without orders once the translation is refused. The Nipper nips. The Smokescreen talks. You hear both and obey neither, because hearing is not obeying. Traffic is heard. Orders are obeyed. You have learned the difference.

This is not willpower. Willpower would be standing at the door shaking and saying I must not. There is no shaking here. There is kettle, plate, doorway, desk, cup, water, paragraph — each whole, each enough, each sharper for no veil. You do not miss paper through the hour. You forget paper through the hour, the way you forget a plaster when the arm stops itching. For minutes together paper never crosses the mind. When it flickers — smell from a coat, hand to pocket by old habit — it arrives pre-labelled: ah, the dying nip, the old story. No debate. No pang of loss. Only a brief noticing and back to the mail, the sip, the laugh.

Live that hour once in imagination and the next real hour is already changed. Not because you forced it. Because you saw who did what. Air cleared. Distance rested. Food flavoured. Work glowed. Breath breathed. Paper only stood beside each and charged. Credit returned is a lock picked. The day does not need feeding. It needs living, and living is what you just did for sixty minutes with nothing in your hands but warmth and work.

## WHAT THE DAY KEEPS

Put the three together and the day changes shape.

Morning kettle without ash. Plate with taste left to linger. Doorway with air and legs and far looking. Desk with hands doing and flavour lingering clean to its end. None of them asked for a coin. Each of them was complete before the hand moved to the pocket. Each of them is sharper when the hand does not move.

We were taught to fear empty moments. Empty morning, empty after-meal, empty break — as if the hours would lie flat and grey with nothing lit in them. Watch them lived and the fear has it backward. Lit, they were flat: same sour mouth, same tight chest, same tug rebuilding before the hour turned. Unlit, they fill with their own detail: steam, flavour, cold, sound, breath taken deep without a rattle, work finished and glowing in the chest.

It never fixed the itch. It caused it.

That is not a slogan now. It is the kettle, the plate, the doorway saying the same thing three ways. The hunger between doses was made by the dose before it. The pleasure in the dose was the hunger going quiet for minutes. The morning, the meal, the break never needed quieting. They needed living.

Paper never made the moment. The moment made paper seem alive.

So live the next one as it stands. Feet to lino. Kettle to steam. Plate left to linger. Door to cold. Desk to work. Let the small tug nip and pass while you taste and breathe and hold nothing in your hands but warmth and air. The Nipper starves unfed. The Smokescreen loses its translation when you see who did what. The nicotine trap held you by credit it never earned, and credit returned is freedom in the hand.

8. TRUST YOUR BODY TO BREATHE AND TASTE
Paper never made the moment — feet to lino, plate left to linger, door to cold air.

**SUMMARY**
- The first dose never woke the morning; waking, water and movement woke it.
- Food tastes longer and cleaner when no dose burns the tongue after it.
- The break rests through air, distance and looking far, not through paper.
- The between-dose tug is small, short-lived and not the self speaking.
- Morning, meal, break and the working hour after are complete lived without a dose, fuller for it.
- Fuller pleasure does not flash and die; it persists across the next hour with empty hands.
