Chapter 9
WHO BUILT THIS WANT

IN THIS CHAPTER
The pack in the palm, the filter to the light, the midnight pull both ways

*That hunger was never yours — it was packed for you, and both fears that hold you were packed with it.*

## THE BOX IN YOUR PALM

You have lived the kettle morning with nothing lit. You have left the plate to linger. You have stood in the cold doorway with empty hands and watched the five minutes hold. Those hours stood whole.

So why does the hand still move to the pocket on its own?

Pick up the box and look at it as if you had never seen it before. Do it now, with the hand you use.

Cardboard, square, warm from the pocket. A flip lid that opens with one thumb. Foil inside, folded neat. Cellophane outside, tight and crackling. Twenty white tubes in a row, packed straight like cartridges.

We learned to call that box company. Character. Mine.

It is none of those. It is a day's supply box.

Count your day through it. One for waking. One for the bus. Two for the morning. One for coffee. One for after food. One for the call that ran long. One for the wait. One for the drink. One for the last thing at night. The number was put in your pocket before you lived the day, and you were taught to call the counting yours.

Take one tube out and roll it between finger and thumb. Paper. Brown leaf cut fine. A brown tip that looks like cork. It looks simple. It looks almost innocent, like nothing a grown person could not put down by teatime.

In their own planning rooms, far from posters and slogans, one of their own scientists wrote it plain: the cigarette should be conceived not as a product but as a package — the product is nicotine.

Sit with that in your own terms. The paper is the wrapper. The tube is the squirt. The puff is the delivery. The box is the day's dosing kit, measured to see you through to night with none left to waste and none left to think with.

Did you ever walk into a shop at fourteen, sixteen, twenty and say, I want to rent my mornings and my meals and my breaks to a drug, twenty squirts a day, every day, paid by me, carried by me, thought about by me? Of course you did not. We were conned into the first ones by friends lighting in doorways, by smoke curled round laughed faces, by posters that made lit hands look adult and calm, and then the dosing did the rest. The first tubes were a trick. The timetable after was chemistry.

Ask it plain over your own palm.

Who chose twenty as your number — you, or the men who packed twenty?
Who chose the timing — your body, or the short fade that calls for the next squirt inside the hour?
If this hunger was your nature, why did it need a factory, a foil, a flip lid and a poster to teach it?

You did not build this want. It was built for you and sold to you as you.

That box in your palm is the nicotine trap made visible.

It never soothed the day. It scheduled the day.

## WHY TWENTY AND WHY CRISP

Stay with the box a minute longer, because the box is talking even when you are not lighting.

Why twenty? Why not six, why not three? Why does every pack in every shop in every country hold the same neat number?

Because the dose fades fast. The lift, the quiet, the taken-care-of minute lasts no longer than a bus ride, and then the small empty nip stirs again under the ribs. To hold a smoker through waking to sleep you need about that many squirts. Fewer and the smoker would notice long clear stretches and start to wonder what the tubes were for. More and the pocket would bulge and the cost would sting too soon. Twenty is not your appetite. Twenty is the arithmetic of a short fade multiplied by a long day.

Why the flip lid that opens with one thumb? Try it. Thumb up, lid back, tube out, lid shut, all while holding a cup, holding a wheel, holding a phone to the ear. No pouch to untie. No tin to unscrew. No pause in talk. The hand learns the move until the move needs no thought, and thought is the one thing the seller cannot afford. A slower box would leave a gap for the question to get in: do I even want this one? A one-thumb box closes the gap. You dose while driving. You dose while laughing. You dose while waiting for the kettle. The convenience is not kindness to you. It is maintenance of the timetable.

Why does it fit the palm, the shirt pocket, the small pocket in the bag? Because a dosing kit that stays home misses doses. A dosing kit that travels never misses. The size was drawn round your life the way a holster is drawn round a gun — not to please the eye but to keep the tool on the body. Jacket, jeans, desk drawer, car door, bedside table. Where you go, it goes. The panic you feel when the box runs low is not your nature either. It is the timetable ringing an alarm the packer set.

Why the foil and the cellophane and the crackle? Tear a fresh pack. Hear the crackle. Smell the first lift of leaf. Every tube dry, crisp, identical. No stale end to put you off. No damp tube to cough over and question. The crispness tells the nose that each squirt will land the same, so the hand keeps moving with trust. Left open to air, leaf goes stale and harsh and the smoker might pause. Sealed tight, each of the twenty promises the same minute as the last, and the day runs without a jolt. The foil does not keep you. It keeps the dose uniform so the dosing never breaks on a bad tube.

Why the silver words round the side — smooth, mild, fine, gold? Those words were not written for the leaf. They were written for the cough in the morning and the pinch on the stairs and the sour mouth you rinse before kissing. When doubt stirs, the eye drops to the pack and reads kindness. Smooth means I am cared for. Mild means I am safer than the man on the hard pack. Gold means I chose well. The words lay a hand on the shoulder while the other hand picks the pocket.

See what all of it does together. The count sets the rhythm. The lid removes the pause. The size keeps the kit on the body. The seal keeps each hit trustworthy. The silver language quiets the cough-worry that might break the rhythm. None of it changes leaf or drug. All of it keeps hourly redosing moving without thought, without question, without a clear hour long enough to see through.

That is why the seller needs the timetable and the costume together. A bare drug in a brown bag with a skull on it would make you think. A crisp white kit in cellophane with kind words, opening with one thumb, counted to your day, lets you act without thinking and call the acting yours. The kindness language is not care. It is oil on a chain that must not squeak, lest you look down and see a chain.

Who decided that your breaks should come twenty to a box?
Who decided that your hand should find the next one without a thought getting in first?
Who decided that your worry should be answered not with truth but with silver?

Not you. Never you. Men in rooms you will never enter decided, drew, sealed, counted and worded, so that a short-fading dose could pass as your day.

Look at the box again and let the anger go where it belongs. Not at your hand for reaching. Not at your chest for wanting. At the hands that packed the reaching and taught the wanting and then charged you for both. You are not weak for carrying it. You were fitted for it, the way a post is fitted for bills — not because the post wants bills, but because bills need posts.

The box never kept you company. It kept you dosed.

## THE HOLES IN THE FILTER

Now hold a tube to the light and look at the tip.

Brown tip, white wrap, a neat ring of tiny holes punched round the paper, so small you can miss them until the light finds them. Mild. Light. Smooth. The promise printed in silver says less, cleaner, kinder. Anyone with a morning cough and a stairs pinch wants to believe there is a softer version. I wanted to believe it. We all did.

Look closer. Those holes are theatre.

On a machine, with a rubber mouth drawing neat and steady, air slips in through those holes and mixes with the smoke. The readings drop. Tar down. Nicotine down. Numbers to print in a table. The machine does not press lips to paper. The machine does not hold the tube between fingers that cover the ring. The machine does not draw deeper because the taste went thin.

A mouth is not a machine.

Put your own lips and fingers on that filter and you cover half those holes without knowing. Draw a shade deeper because the taste is thin and the old nip still talks. Hold a second longer because the hit feels missing. Take one more from the box before the hour turns because the last one left the nip half-quieted. As a rule smokers pull harder on lights and take in what the machine said they missed — not to the millimetre the same in every smoker, bodies and days differ, the pattern bends person to person, but the direction holds. The reading changed. The need did not loosen.

I speak here of how the trick works, not of any softer way to smoke. This does not make the hard-hitting kind a better choice. There is no better choice in the kit. There is only the kit.

Ask it over your own hand.

If lights gave less, why did you smoke the same number, or more, and hold each deeper and longer?
If the filter shielded you, why did the cough stay, the stairs stay hard, the morning throat stay sour?
If mild was mild, why did the timetable never loosen — why the same hourly call, the same pocket pat, the same tightness when the box ran low?

That mild story is the Smokescreen talking.

See why the seller needs that costume too. The cough and the pinch and the sour mouth are information. Left plain, they would make a thinking person stop and look down at the box. Silver words and vent holes give the thinking a place to go that is not out: not I must see through this, but I will switch to silver and carry on. The fear is answered with a product instead of a question. The doubt is dosed instead of heard. The timetable survives the very signs that should break it, because the signs have been labelled as solved.

That is not care. It is packaging. The same dosing kit with a kinder costume, kept moving by kind language while the hourly call goes on underneath unchanged.

Once you see the costume, you cannot unsee it. Every silver word is a hand on your shoulder while the other hand sets the alarm for the next hour. Every vent hole is a dial turned down on a machine while your mouth turns the dial back up without asking you. You were never protected. You were measured — on a rubber mouth that never lived a morning — and then left to make up the difference with your own lungs, your own lips, your own extra tube before lunch.

Let the anger sit where it belongs. Not on the smoker in the mirror for choosing silver. We chose silver because we felt the damage and wanted it to stop while keeping the supposed friend. That is a kind motive twisted by false information. Put the blame on the wardrobe department that dressed the same drug as a nurse. You reached for less harm. They sold you the same harm with holes.

The holes did not change the drug. They changed the reading.

## THE FIRST ROPE

This is where most smokers live once the kettle and the plate have shown themselves whole.

Not in doubt about mornings. In dread at night. Two voices. Opposite directions. Both loud when the house goes quiet.

“I have tried and tried. What if I try again and come back smoking? Better not to promise than to prove I am weak.”

I know that voice. I lived inside it. Let me meet it straight, because it sounds like wisdom about you and it is information about the trap.

Picture the night minute. Bedside light on. Box half empty. You add the day: lit after food though you said you would not, lit in rain though you hate rain, lit before the call though the call went well without. The chest feels the day. The mouth tastes the day. And the mind does its old sum: look at the record, who are you to think this time holds?

That sum feels like self-knowledge. It is not. It is a rope.

The first rope says you cannot stop. It pulls toward staying because trying feels more shameful than staying. Stay and no one, not even you, sees you fail. Try and the whole street watches. So the hand learns to prefer the known sour to the unknown shame, and calls the preference realism.

All you have to do is follow all the instructions.

Hear that rope for what it is. Did you as a child sit down and decide that courage lives in paper? Did you decide that a grown person needs a tube to face a morning? Or was that rope handed to you, loop by loop, by every failed attempt the wrong method arranged and by every poster that said lit hands are steady hands? The pull feels inside. The hands holding the other end are not yours.

You are not afraid to fail because you are weak. You are afraid to fail because the trap taught you to read its own timetable as your character. Drop the idea that the dread is news about you. It is news about the trap that needs you dreading, because a person dreading a test does not sit the test.

## THE SECOND ROPE

The second voice pulls the other way and holds just as tight.

“I have smoked through my whole adult life. Breaks, nights out, work done, food finished. Who am I with empty hands? What if the days go flat and grey with nothing lit in them?”

I know that one too. It sounds like identity. It sounds like love for a version of yourself that laughs louder with a tube between fingers, thinks sharper with smoke round the head, belongs fuller on the step outside the pub.

Meet it with your own lived hours, not with talk.

Where did you meet that flat grey stranger you fear becoming? Not in life. In posters. In talk in smoking doorways. In the seller's old story that lit hands are lively hands and unlit hands are empty hands. You have already watched a kettle morning stand full with nothing lit — feet to cold lino, spine lengthening, steam to the face, water tasting sweet, breath taken down to the bottom ribs without a rattle. You have watched a plate linger — garlic first, then lemon behind, then fat smooth over both, talk stretching while the flavour stays. You have stood in the cold doorway with nothing pinched between fingers and felt shoulders drop because legs moved and eyes looked far. Was any of that grey? Was any of that missing? Which hour held more — the lit hour that thinned to sour mouth and tight chest and the next call inside the hour, or the unlit hour that held steam and flavour and cold and sound and done work?

“I am a smoker. It is who I am.”

Was that true at ten? At twelve, before the first conned puff behind the bike shed, did you feel missing? Did you sit through lessons, run for buses, laugh till sides ached, sleep deep and wake clear, and feel a tube-shaped hole in the self? Of course you did not. The self was whole. Then the dosing taught the hands a timetable and the posters taught the mind a story, and the story called the timetable identity. Identity was the last costume, put on after the silver words, for the same job: to make leaving feel like losing a self instead of dropping a kit.

Ask it plain.

If lit hands were your nature, why did the first ones make you cough and dizzy and sick, and why did you have to train the body to bear them?
If unlit life is flat, why did the kettle hum, the lingering plate and the cold street feel fuller with nothing lit than any lit hour you can name?
If paper made you you, who were you for all those years before paper, laughing and running and tasting with a clean tongue?

See the trick now whole. One rope says you cannot stop. The other says you cannot live stopped. Pull on either and you stay at the post, lighting the next tube while arguing with yourself. That is the point of both. A person arguing with himself stays still. A person still lights on time.

Did you tie either rope? Did you decide that courage lives in paper and that joy needs smoke? Or were those ropes handed to you, one by the failed attempts arranged for you, one by the seller who needs you afraid of your own clear days?

That pull both ways is the tug-of-war of fear.

Both ropes belong to the same hands. Not yours. The trap holds one end of each and lets you do the pulling. Drop the idea that the fear is information about you. It is information about a business that needs you afraid to move and afraid to arrive, so you never move.

## WHAT STAYING RENTS

It is time to look at what staying rents, because the seller never puts this on the box. I lay it flat, and I do not lay it to drive you with fright. Fright never freed anyone; it only makes the next tube feel like shelter. I lay it so you see who did this to you, and then we leave fright behind and walk out on anger and clear sight.

Stale. The mouth in the morning furred and sour, tasting of last night's last dose before water. Clothes aired at the window. Windows opened in winter. Mints chewed. Spray in the bag. The small lie about where you were for ten minutes. The kiss turned away, not from you but from the ash round you. The child asking why you smell like that, and the hot shame that follows because you love that child clean through. We told ourselves no one noticed. Everyone noticed. We noticed most, and we carried the noticing like a second weight on top of the day. The hiding in garages, the Febreeze, the stepped-outside minute that cut the film and the meal and the talk — that divided self was not your character. It was the timetable demanding a doorway.

Breathless. The second flight taken slower while the unlit friend breathes easy above. The laugh that ends in a cough. The chest a shade tight by evening. The swim in the head on the first drag called a lift, though clear heads never need to swim to stand. The body that was built to run and climb and sing taught to ration stairs, to plan hills, to sit out the last dance because the chest would pay. I speak here of what the trap rents, not of your body's care; if you feel ill or you worry, speak to your own clinician for your own care.

Chained. The box counted last thing and first thing. The pocket patted without thought. The shop run in rain for morning. The evening cut short to buy for morning. The party stepped out of. The film missed in the middle. The train seat chosen for the next platform's doorway. The desk minute watched for the hour mark. Twenty small decisions a day made by cardboard, and we called the obedience choice.

Put them together and stop looking away, because looking away is what the seller bought with silver words like smooth and mild. Mouth sour, chest tight, day timetabled by a box. Nights shortened. Money burned tube by tube. Attention rented out hourly to a nip. Years measured not in lived mornings but in packs carried, doorways stood in, plates blotted, kisses turned from.

That is what staying looks like: stale, breathless and chained.

Feel the weight of it and then let the weight change hands. You did not do this to yourself from weakness. A business that packs a drug as twenty social moments did this to you for profit, then taught you to call the doing your choice. The sour mouth is not proof you love lighting. It is proof lighting sours. The pinch on the stairs is not proof you need paper. It is proof paper took. The panic at an empty box is not proof of your nature. It is proof of a timetable packed by other men.

And here is the turn that loosens both ropes at once. Once you see the builder, the fear changes shape. Fear of failing was fear of proving weak before the street. There is no weak here. There is only a conned person seeing a kit. Fear of living unlit was fear of becoming a grey stranger. There is no grey stranger. There is only a kettle morning, a lingering plate, a cold doorway person who was taught to pay a coin for what was already his. The ropes fall because the hands holding them are seen, and seen hands cannot pull in the dark.

You are not a smoker by nature who must fear life unlit. You are a clear morning, a lingering plate, an open doorway person who was taught to pay for what was already yours. The builder built the want. The builder built the fears that guard the want. Seen together, builder and ropes and box stand as one thing — a con, not a character — and a con, once seen, has no hold on a thinking person who keeps his eyes open.

9. SEE THE SELLER BEHIND THE SMOKE
That box in your hand was packed to keep you lighting.

**SUMMARY**
- The want did not start in me; the first tubes were conned, then dosing set the timetable.
- The box works as a day's dosing kit, counted and timed to the short fade.
- Flip lid, pocket size, foil seal and silver words keep hourly use moving without thought.
- Filter holes and mild promise change the machine reading, not the hourly need.
- Both night fears pull opposite ways yet belong to the same trap hands.
- Staying keeps mouth sour, chest tight and days timetabled by cardboard.
- Seeing the builder moves blame from my character to the maker.
