Chapter 8
THE KETTLE, THE PLATE, THE OPEN DOOR

**IN THIS CHAPTER**
The cold lino by the bed, the kettle steam in the kitchen, the plate after the meal, the doorway air at eleven, empty hands

*The morning, the meal and the break were whole all along — the dose added nothing to them.*

## THE FEET BEFORE THE FLOOR

We know that first minute blindfold.

Alarm. Eyes not open. Feet have not touched the floor and the hand has already found the pack on the chair. Lighter. First drag on empty lungs before the bladder, before water, before a word spoken. Chest tightens, head swims a shade, and we call that swim waking.

I did that for years. I believed I could not start until paper started me.

Stand in that minute with me now and watch what starts you.

Feet on cold lino. Spine lengthening after night flat. Mouth dry from sleep, tasting of sleep, not of ash. You stand. You walk to the kitchen. You fill the kettle. You hear the click, then the rising hum. Steam touches the face. You draw the first full breath of the day down to the bottom of the ribs and it moves without a rattle.

That breath is yours. No paper taught it.

“I can’t start without the first one. Mornings are impossible.”

I hear you, because that was my voice. Let your own mornings answer.

If the first dose truly woke you, why did you wake enough to find it, light it and carry it before it touched you? Who walked to the chair? Who struck the lighter? Waking came first. Paper arrived second and charged for the walk.

If the first dose truly cleared the head, why did the head clear more with water, with piss, with the kettle hum and the cold floor underfoot than with the bite that left the mouth sour and the chest a shade tight?

Watch the taste. On a smoking morning the mouth wakes dry and we cover dry with ash and call the cover flavour. On a clear morning the mouth wakes dry and water tastes sweet and tea tastes of tea and the tongue wakes with the day. Which mouth is more awake?

The first dose never woke the morning. The morning woke you and paper rode on its back.

## THE PLATE STILL WARM

Then the meal.

We all know the table point. Last mouthful down. Plate still warm. The taste lingering that the cook worked for — fat, salt, lemon, gravy. And before the plate is cleared the hand is in the pocket. We light over the plate. We dull the tongue we just fed and call the dulling the finish.

That old pellet after the paragraph, after the drive, after the plate — we paid every finished thing with a light.

You fear the meal will fall flat without the full stop. So finish a meal in your head with me and stay to the taste.

You eat. You chew slow at the end. You put the fork down. The mouth is full of the meal, warm and particular. You sit. You breathe through the nose. The flavour stays five minutes, ten minutes, changing as it fades, the way a note stays in a room after the string is struck. You drink water. The water lifts the flavour again. You talk. You laugh with the mouth still tasting.

Now put paper into that picture and see what it does. The first drag burns the tongue, dries the palate, lays ash over lemon and gravy. The lingering note is gone in one draw. The nose fills with smoke instead of food. What exactly did the finish finish? It finished the taste.

Ask it plain over your own plate.

If the dose completed the meal, why does food taste stronger, longer and cleaner when no dose comes after it?
If the dose was the pleasure of eating, why do you remember the meal and forget the dose, except for the sour mouth and the need to Febreeze the room after?

I ate my best meals as a smoker with a dulled tongue and told myself I loved food. I taste more now in one dinner than I tasted in a month of lit finishes.

The party was work. The flavour was food. Paper sneaked a ride on hunger satisfied and called the ride dessert.

## THE DOORWAY AT ELEVEN

Then the break.

Ten forty. Screen ache. Shoulders up round the ears. The mind offers its old translation on cue: step out, get air, take paper, clear the head. We stood in that doorway a thousand times, shoulder to the brick, dose between fingers, watching the street and calling the watching rest.

Take that doorway with me now with empty hands.

You push the door. Cold air hits the face. You hear traffic, a gull, heels on pavement. You roll the shoulders. You fill the chest to the bottom. The ache behind the eyes thins because the eyes look far for a minute and the legs move and blood moves. You stand five minutes and look at nothing useful. Thoughts settle because you stopped feeding them paper for five minutes.

What stirs underneath is an empty, slightly restless, slightly edgy little tug.

That is all. A faint emptiness under the ribs. Hands a fraction too idle. Skin a shade too tight. Not pain. Not collapse. The same small signal you learned to read as now-you-need-it.

Left alone it frets, peaks, passes. Attention goes to the bus going past, to the cold on the ears, to the next job waiting inside. For minutes you forget it entirely. Then it flickers back fainter. That wave is typical — as a rule sharpest in the first days without a dose and usually softening within weeks, not the same to the day for every body, bodies and days differ — and I speak of belief here, not of your body's care; if you feel ill or you worry, speak to your own clinician for your own care.

The tug never stopped you typing, driving, carrying shopping, laughing at the joke. That stairs-breath pinch on the second flight told on the lungs, not on the need. A real need interrupts act. This only colours while you get on with everything.

Try it over this doorway.

If the break needed paper, why does the air clear the head when paper stays inside? Why do the shoulders drop with movement and distance and cold on the skin, none of which came from a pack?
If paper rested you, why did every break end with the same restlessness scheduling the next break within the hour?

The pause was air. The rest was distance. The clear head was blood moving while the eyes looked far. Paper stood in the doorway with you and took the credit for the street.

## WHAT THE DAY KEEPS

Put the three together and the day changes shape.

Morning kettle without ash. Plate with taste left to linger. Doorway with air and legs and far looking. None of them asked for a coin. Each of them was complete before the hand moved to the pocket. Each of them is sharper when the hand does not move.

We were taught to fear empty moments. Empty morning, empty after-meal, empty break — as if the hours would lie flat and grey with nothing lit in them. Watch them lived and the fear has it backward. Lit, they were flat: same sour mouth, same tight chest, same tug rebuilding before the hour turned. Unlit, they fill with their own detail: steam, flavour, cold, sound, breath taken deep without a rattle.

It never fixed the itch. It caused it.

That is not a slogan now. It is the kettle, the plate, the doorway saying the same thing three ways. The hunger between doses was made by the dose before it. The pleasure in the dose was the hunger going quiet for minutes. The morning, the meal, the break never needed quieting. They needed living.

Paper never made the moment. The moment made paper seem alive.

So live the next one as it stands. Feet to lino. Kettle to steam. Plate left to linger. Door to cold. Let the small tug nip and pass while you taste and breathe and hold nothing in your hands but warmth and air. The Nipper starves unfed. The Smokescreen loses its translation when you see who did what. The nicotine trap held you by credit it never earned, and credit returned is a lock picked.

8. TRUST YOUR BODY TO BREATHE AND TASTE
Your body knows freedom without being taught.

**SUMMARY**
- The first dose never woke the morning; waking, water and movement woke it.
- Food tastes longer and cleaner when no dose burns the tongue after it.
- The break rests through air, distance and looking far, not through paper.
- The between-dose tug is small, short-lived and not the self speaking.
- Morning, meal and break are complete lived without a dose, fuller for it.
