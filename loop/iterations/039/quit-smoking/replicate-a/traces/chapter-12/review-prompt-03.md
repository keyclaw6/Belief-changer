### The draft chapter

Chapter 12
YOUR LAST CIGARETTE

*You are a non-smoker the second you stub it, because the want died before the tug did.*

### ARE YOU READY TO BE DONE

There is nowhere left to bargain. That is why this page feels different from the first page you read. Not braced. Not argued at. Quiet. Attentive. A person at a door he has already decided to walk through.

Let me ask you straight, as one who walked out to one still inside.

Do you feel ready? Are you champing at the bit to stub the last one and walk out? Do you see clearly that there is nothing out there to miss and everything in here to leave?

I ask because I will not ask you to muster courage or clench anything. If you still feel a pull toward keeping one small exception, do not force yourself forward. Put this chapter down, go back to the start and read again with open eyes until the pull is gone. All you have to do is follow all the instructions.

But if you are ready — if the deals look like the con they were and the last tube looks like paper and weed — then we finish it now. Tonight. On this page. Not after this pack, not on Monday. Now.

You have heard the doubts already. I hear them with you because we all heard them.

“What if I am not ready enough?”

“What if I need one more week to be sure?”

“What if this last one is different?”

Ask them plain and answer honest. What would another week teach you that ten thousand tubes did not? Would the throat feel different on the ten-thousand-and-first scratch? Would the tray look different at midnight? There is only one honest answer, and you know it. No new fact is coming. The seeing is done. What is left is the doing.

We all stacked tomorrows for years. I stacked them. We said after the box, after the holiday, when work calms. Each tomorrow cost money burned, breath shortened, evenings timetabled by pocket and doorway, the sour mouth and the morning shop-run, the small divided self that says one thing in daylight and does another in the yard. If leaf gave anything real, why would you need to quit tomorrow? And if it gives nothing, why pay another thirty, another hundred, another thousand doses to arrive at Monday?

So I gate you here, not on a schedule but on seeing. Do you see that the tube seasoned nothing and ashed everything it touched? Then you are ready. Remember, you have nothing to lose and everything to gain.

### THE FAREWELL TABLE

We all built the last one up in our heads for years. The final smoke at sunset, the deep nostalgic drag, the slow goodbye to an old friend. Forget that picture. It was painted for you.

Your last cigarette is not a farewell to a friend. It is a look, with clear eyes, at what you are leaving.

Take it to an ordinary table. Kitchen table is best. No drinks around you, no mates watching, no music to dress it. Just you, the tray, and one ordinary tube from your current pack. This is ceremonial, not extra. Do not buy a fresh pack for it. Do not smoke a chain to get ready. Smoke as normal until this one, then stop on it.

Light it as you always did. Then do something you have never done in all your years of smoking. Pay full attention to it.

Watch the paper burn. Look at the yellow stain spreading on the filter where your lips sit. Look at the grey ash lengthening and threatening your cuff. Taste what is actually on your tongue — not heaven, not calm, not company. Dry paper. Hot scratch at the back of the throat. Sour underneath. Smell your fingers after two puffs. Smell the air in the small room around you. Notice how quickly the shoulders drop for a minute and how the head feels nothing except a brief quiet of want, while the bills, the argument, the tiredness you carried to the table sit exactly where you left them.

We smoked ten thousand of these without looking. We lit on the step, in the car with the window cracked, in the garage with the door cracked, blowing thin smoke into rain while we listened for footsteps in the hall. We Febreezed the hell out of ourselves and chewed mints till the jaw ached and told ourselves the tube was seasoning the moment. It was ashing it.

Look now. Is this pleasure? Is this a crutch that held you? Or is it a dose — a squirt of nicotine through a paper tube that quiets for minutes the hollow pull the last squirt left?

Ask yourself while it burns, and answer honest:

Did this tube add one thing to the table, or did it take taste off the tongue and leave the room hazed?
If this is a friend, why did you have to hide it from the people you love?
If this calms you, why are you already counting when you can have the next one?

There is only one honest answer, and you already know it. It took from your breath, your pocket, your evening, and it moved not one paper on the table.

Smoke it to the end, not to enjoy it but to see it. See the stain, the ash, the sour mouth, the faint swim that passes in seconds. This is what the dispenser sold you as reward, as pause, as little parties. Ninety seconds of scratch and sour for an hour of being timed for the next one.

You saw it. It ends here.

That seeing is its own settling in this room — not a mood, but the plain sight of the tube with its paint washed off. Keep looking until the prettiness is gone and only the paper, the stain, the ash and the sour remain. No laboratory tasting. No extra chain. One ordinary tube, watched all the way down, tells the truth that ten thousand hurried tubes hid.

Notice the fingers. Notice the yellow at the nails that soap never quite lifted. Notice the cuff. Notice the small burn hole you stopped seeing years ago. Notice the tray already dotted with mashed ends, each one lit with the same hope and each one ending in the same sour mash. We told ourselves each lighting was different — the morning one, the meal one, the break one, the night one. Watched here, under a bare bulb, they are all the same: light, scratch, quiet for minutes, want again.

Notice the mouth. After two puffs the tongue furs. After four the throat scratches. After six the wine you are not drinking would sour, the food you are not eating would shorten at the edges. The body keeps a ledger even when the mind tells stories. The ledger here says dry, hot, sour, then want.

And notice the table itself. The unpaid bill with the red edge. The phone face-down with the unsent message. The tiredness behind your eyes that no puff lifted. None of it moved. None of it softened. None of it was touched by leaf. The shoulders dropped for a minute because the last dose stopped asking, not because the bill was paid or the word was unsaid. The problem papers sat exactly where you left them, untouched by leaf.

We used to call that drop calm. We used to say a cigarette takes care of it. Watched now, you see the trick. The tube did not touch the care. It touched the want it made.

### WHAT THE TUG WILL DO AFTER YOU STUB IT

I want you to know exactly what happens in your body after you stub it, so no small sensation can fool you later.

While you smoked, you fed hourly. Dose, quiet for minutes, fade, tug again. The tug you called hunger, boredom, tension, need-a-break was the fading of the last dose asking for the next.

When you stop feeding it, that tug does not grow into something mighty. It stays small. For most smokers the fidget sharpens over the first day or two, sharpest around two or three days, then eases over a week or two as you stay without smoking — this is not a sentence on you, it fades while you stay without a dose. Not every body keeps the same clock, and if you live with illness or worry about stopping, speak to your clinician for your own care.

Hear that clearly, because the old story will try to misread it.

That tug is not you needing leaf to be yourself. It is not stress proving you cannot cope. It is not pleasure calling you back. That faint pull is only the Nipper dying unfed.

And that little creature is trivial. We fed it every forty minutes for years and called it need. Starved, it complains for days in a small voice and then dies. What kept it alive for years was never its strength. It was belief — films, hoardings, older hands lighting at every door, the timed nip misread as proof the tube helped. Kill the belief and the little creature starves with it. You are stepping out of the nicotine trap for good, and you leave it easily, immediately and permanently.

Do you see the turn? You never smoked to rise above normal. You smoked to climb briefly back toward the calm a non-smoker lives in all day, then slid straight back down to pay again. The dose made the hollow it then filled for minutes.

So after the last stub there is nothing to wait for. No staircase to climb. No days to count. You do not become a non-smoker in a week when the tug fades. You are one when the belief dies — which is now, if you mean the vow.

Hold that distinction, because it is everything. The tug is body noise. The belief is the master. Body noise fades on its own clock whether you watch it or not. The master dies the second you see there is nothing to miss and shut the door. From that second you are not waiting to be something. You are something, while noise dies in the background.

I tell you this now so you will not be fooled at midnight on day two. When the shoulders feel hollow and the hands feel empty and the mind says, “This proves I need it,” you will know better. You will say, “No. This proves the last dose wore off. This is the dying, not the needing.” You will not argue with it. You will not time it. You will let it tap and pass, the way a bell taps and passes in another street.

### THE VOW

Stub that last tube. Push it hard into the tray until the glow is out. Look at the mashed end. Smell it. That sour mash is what you used to put in your mouth and call heaven at lighting.

Now say it, out loud if you can, solemn as a promise that matters because it does:

I will never smoke another dose. Not one puff. Not one special occasion. Not tomorrow, not on holiday, not on a bad day. The trap is closed.

Say it with joy, not with a heavy heart. There is no friend in that tray. There is a dead enemy. Congratulate yourself. I congratulate you with all my heart. You have just done what millions believe is impossible and what you believed was impossible for years — you have walked out while still allowed to walk out smiling.

12. SMOKE YOUR FINAL CIGARETTE AND KNOW YOU ARE FREE
You have seen the stain, the ash and the sour while life sat untouched.

From this second you do not wait to become a non-smoker. You are one. Empty hands, clear tongue, coat that will smell of rain tomorrow. Breathe to the bottom of your lungs and notice you do not need to cough to earn the air. That breath is yours without payment.

### THE HOUR AFTER THE STUB

Stay at the table a minute. Do not rush this hour. This hour is the whole book in one room.

Look at the tray. Really look. The mashed end you just pushed out is still warm at the tip and already cold at the filter. The ash has broken and dusted the rim. The smell rising off it is sour, stale, faintly sweet in a way that turns the stomach now that you are watching. In all the years you tapped ash without seeing, you told yourself the smell lived outside you — on the coat, in the car, in the garage. Now, with the room still, you smell that it lived in you. On the fingers. On the breath. On the tongue.

Let the disgust do its work, then let the joy take its place. Not joy that you suffered and won. Joy that there is nothing to suffer. The tube gave you stain, ash and sour while the bill stayed red and the phone stayed face-down. Seeing that, the want has nowhere to stand. The door is shut because there is nothing behind it worth opening for.

Stand now. Throw the empty pack away — not as a test of courage, but as rubbish, because rubbish is what it is. Feel the soft corners of the box, the rattle of the last tubes if any are left, the cellophane that used to sound like promise. It is paper and weed in printed cardboard. It counted your doses for you and called the counting pleasure. Drop it in the bin and hear the thud. That thud is not loss. It is rubbish hitting rubbish.

Wash the tray or bin it. Run hot water. Watch the grey film lift and swirl. Scrub the brown ring that never quite came out. Dry it and put it upside down, or put it straight in the bag with the pack. You do not need a monument to ash. You need a table you can eat off without moving anything aside. Smell your hands after the soap. They smell of soap. That is what hands smell of when no tube has touched them for an hour.

Wash your hands slowly. Between the fingers. Under the nails where yellow sat. Up to the wrist where smoke sat in the hairs. Feel the water hot, then cool. This is not a ritual to keep you busy. This is what hands do when they are not holding paper. They wash. They dry. They rest open on the table without searching the pocket.

Lift them and look. Empty hands. No tube between knuckles. No ash on the cuff. No tapping, no shielding from wind, no cupping in rain. For years those hands performed a hundred small jobs for leaf — patting pockets before standing, flicking, shielding, hunting trays, holding mints, spraying coats. Watch how quiet they are now. They lie still. They do not miss the work. The work was slavery dressed as habit.

Breathe to the bottom of your lungs. Slow, through the nose. Feel the cool room air go all the way down without catching on scratch. Hold a second. Let it out through the mouth. Taste the mouth after the wash — toothpaste, water, nothing sour underneath. That taste is yours. It was yours before the first tube you ever lit, and it is yours again now that the last one is mashed. No tube cleaned it. Time and soap and no more paper did.

Sit with the kettle waiting for morning. You do not need to drink now. Just know it is there, and the cup will taste of what is in the cup, not of what came before it. For years the first sip was always rinsed through sour. Tomorrow the sip will be whole. Not because you earned it by waiting a week. Because you stopped ashing it tonight.

This is what KNOW YOU ARE FREE means while the little creature is still dying. It does not mean the tug is already gone. It means you no longer read the tug as orders. The tug may tap tonight. It may tap tomorrow night. It taps as a dying complaint, not as proof you left something behind. Identity is conferred by dead belief, not by faded tug. The belief died at the tray when you saw stain, ash and sour with life untouched. The tug will fade on its own clock. You do not wait for the clock to tell you who you are. You tell the clock who you are while it ticks down.

We used to think becoming a non-smoker was something the body granted after days counted. We counted mornings, we counted cravings, we counted how long since the last one, as if each counted day built the new self brick by brick. That counting kept leaf alive as something still missed and still measured. I tell you flat: you do not build the new self by counting. You reveal it by seeing. The seeing is done. The self is here. Empty hands, clear tongue, washed tray, binned pack. No staircase. No tally. No waiting room.

If the mind says, “But I still feel the tug, so how can I be done?” answer it with what you saw. “I feel the tug because the last dose wore off. I am done because I saw the dose gives nothing to keep.” The tug is noise. The seeing is verdict. Noise does not overturn verdict.

If the mind says, “Should I test myself by keeping a pack in the drawer to prove I can leave it?” answer it cold. “A person who has walked out does not keep rubbish in the drawer to prove it. I binned it because it is rubbish.” Keeping it would be keeping the question open. The question is closed.

If the mind says, “I feel oddly flat, not elated — did it work?” answer it warm toward yourself. “Flat is the room after noise stops. Elation comes when the quiet proves itself hour by hour. I am not flat because something is missing. I am quiet because nothing is shouting.” Give the quiet an hour and you will feel the weight off the shoulders that you carried to every doorway.

Walk to the door and back. Feel the pocket without the box. For years the pocket-pat came before standing — keys, phone, box, lights. The hand went before the mind decided. Now the hand finds keys, phone, nothing else, and the mind does not lurch. That absence is not emptiness. It is pocket as pocket should be. No timetable inside it. No hourly hire.

Walk to the window. Open it a crack. Smell the night. Rain, bins, next door cooking, cold stone. For years the window was a place to blow thin smoke through while listening for footsteps. Now it is a place to breathe through. The difference is not small. It is the difference between using the night as a hiding place and meeting it with nothing in the mouth.

Come back to the table. Look once more at the mashed end, now cold. That is the whole story in one object. Paper, stain, ash, sour. No calm in it. No company in it. No pause in it except the pause it stole to feed itself. You watched it burn with open eyes. You will never need to watch it again, because you will never need to prove again what was proved here.

This hour asks one thing of you: solemn joy. Solemn, because you spoke a vow that matters. Joy, because the vow took nothing and returned everything — taste that stays to the end, breath that goes to the bottom, hands that rest, a table with no tray on it, a pocket with no timetable in it. Solemn joy is not loud. It sits upright. It looks at the bin and nods. It washes its hands and breathes. It knows.

### TWO DOORS THAT STAY SHUT

Staying shut needs only one thing guarded: the belief. Not your behaviour with white knuckles. The belief. Two thoughts will knock in the next days and weeks. Both will sound like you. Neither is you. Both are the old script, pre-labelled.

The first knock comes on a bad day. Argument, bills, phone down hard, hand already moving to the old pocket before the mind catches up. A voice says, very kind: just this once, this is real stress, a cigarette takes care of it.

Hear it with clear eyes. Did leaf ever move one paper on that desk? Did it ever pay one bill or unsay one word? It quieted for minutes its own tug and left the problem untouched. You do not need a dose to cope. You need air, hands open, and the problem itself. The tug will pass whether you feed it or not — fed, it guarantees another; unfed, it starves a little more.

The second knock comes on a good day. Patio, drinks, laugh, a hand extending a pack: go on, one will not hurt. Or a quiet voice after months clear: I have proved I can leave it, I can prove I can take one.

Hear that too. Months clear never made a single dose safe. One after months scratches, sours the mouth for an hour, and by evening the hourly nip is back tapping the shoulder it had left alone — doorway, tray, mint, shop-run, divided evenings rebuilt within weeks. One never stays one because one re-teaches the belief that the dose gives something. It gives nothing. A stumble, if it ever happens, is a rumble strip, not a crash. Put it out, see the scratch for what it was, shut the belief again. It warns. It never licenses another on purpose.

So we close both doors now, before they knock.

Whenever the thought of a tube crosses your mind, do not argue with it and do not try to not think it. Greet it with the shout that turns the old trigger into a small hit of joy. Say it the first time at this table and feel how true it is. Mean it from the belly:

YIPPEE! I'M FREE!

Say it and taste toast tasting of toast in the morning without the hand-search. Say it when you watch other smokers huddle in the rain outside a doorway and feel pity, not envy — they are still timed, still soured, still chained to pocket and tray. You are not being deprived. They are.

That shout is your lifelong script. The thought arrives, the shout answers, the moment proves itself whole. The tube was only ever sneaking a ride on kettle, food, pause, company, night air. The kettle, the food, the pause, the company, the night remain. The rider is gone.

Stand now. Wash your hands once more. Lift your head. Walk into the next ordinary hour as a non-smoker who is happy to be one, champing at the bit for the days ahead.
