Chapter 12
YOUR LAST CIGARETTE

**IN THIS CHAPTER**
The kitchen table after midnight, the yellow stain and grey ash, the stale end in the tray, the empty pack, the kettle waiting for morning.

*This last tube gives you nothing to keep, and the second you stub it you are already a non-smoker.*

### ARE YOU READY TO BE DONE

The patio is settled. The glow was faces and talk, not leaf. Cutting down is settled. Fewer only meant thinking more. The special one is settled. One rebuilds the whole house. Tomorrow is settled. Tomorrow only hires another week of pocket and doorway for no new fact.

So there is nowhere left to bargain. That is why you feel different reading this page from the way you felt when you opened this book. Not braced. Not argued at. Quiet. Attentive. A man at a door he has already decided to walk through.

Let me ask you straight, as one escapee to another.

Do you feel ready? Are you champing at the bit to stub the last one and walk out? Do you see clearly that there is nothing out there to miss and everything in here to leave?

I ask because I will not ask you to muster courage or clench anything. If you still feel a pull toward keeping one small exception, do not force yourself forward. Put this chapter down, go back to the start and read again with open eyes until the pull is gone. All you have to do is follow all the instructions.

But if you are ready — if the deals look like the con they were and the last tube looks like paper and weed — then we finish it now. Tonight. On this page. Not after this pack, not on Monday. Now.

And you know you have nothing to lose and everything to gain.

### THE FAREWELL TABLE

We all built the last one up in our heads for years. The final smoke at sunset, the deep nostalgic drag, the slow goodbye to an old friend. Forget that picture. It was painted for you.

Your last cigarette is not a farewell to a friend. It is a look, with clear eyes, at what you are leaving.

Take it to an ordinary table. Kitchen table is best. No drinks around you, no mates watching, no music to dress it. Just you, the tray, and one ordinary tube from your current pack. This is ceremonial, not extra. Do not buy a fresh pack for it. Do not smoke a chain to get ready. Smoke as normal until this one, then stop on it.

Light it as you always did. Then do something you have never done in all your years of smoking. Pay full attention to it.

Watch the paper burn. Look at the yellow stain spreading on the filter where your lips sit. Look at the grey ash lengthening and threatening your cuff. Taste what is actually on your tongue — not heaven, not calm, not company. Dry paper. Hot scratch at the back of the throat. Sour underneath. Smell your fingers after two puffs. Smell the air in the small room around you. Notice how quickly the shoulders drop for a minute and how the head feels nothing except a brief quiet of want, while the bills, the argument, the tiredness you carried to the table sit exactly where you left them.

We smoked ten thousand of these without looking. We lit on the step, in the car with the window cracked, in the garage with the door cracked, blowing thin smoke into rain while we listened for footsteps in the hall. We Febreezed the hell out of ourselves and chewed mints till the jaw ached and told ourselves the tube was seasoning the moment. It was ashing it.

Look now. Is this pleasure? Is this a crutch that held you? Or is it a dose — a squirt of nicotine through a paper tube that quiets for minutes the empty tug the last squirt left?

Ask yourself while it burns, and answer honest:

Did this tube add one thing to the table, or did it take clean taste and clean air off it?
If this is a friend, why did you have to hide it from the people you love?
If this calms you, why are you already counting when you can have the next one?

There is only one honest answer, and you already know it. It does plenty TO you. It does nothing FOR you.

Smoke it to the end, not to enjoy it but to see it. See the stain, the ash, the sour mouth, the faint swim that passes in seconds. This is what the dispenser sold you as reward, as pause, as little parties. Ninety seconds of scratch and sour for an hour of being timed for the next one.

That seeing is your relief in this room — not a mood, but the plain sight of the tube with its paint washed off.

### WHAT THE TUG WILL DO AFTER YOU STUB IT

I want you to know exactly what happens in your body after you stub it, so no small sensation can fool you later.

While you smoked, each dose peaked in the brain within seconds and faded within minutes. That rapid fade is why you fed hourly. Redose, quiet for minutes, fade, tug again. The tug you called hunger, boredom, tension, need-a-break was the fading of the last dose asking for the next.

When you stop feeding it, that tug does not grow into something mighty. It is an empty, slightly restless, slightly edgy little tug, already dying. For most smokers the restlessness sharpens over the first day or two, sharpest around two or three days, then eases over a week or two — this is not a sentence on you, it fades as you stay smoke-free. Not every body keeps the same clock, and if you live with illness or worry about stopping, speak to your clinician for your own care.

Hear that clearly, because the Smokescreen will try to misread it.

That tug is not you needing leaf to be yourself. It is not stress proving you cannot cope. It is not pleasure calling you back. That tug is only the Nipper dying unfed.

And the Nipper is trivial. We fed it every forty minutes for years and called it need. Starved, it complains for days in a small voice and then dies. What kept it alive for years was never its strength. It was belief — films, hoardings, older hands lighting at every door, the timed nip misread as proof the tube helped. Kill the belief and the little creature starves with it. You are stepping out of the nicotine trap for good, and you do it easily, immediately and permanently.

Do you see the inversion? You never smoked to rise above normal. You smoked to climb briefly back toward the calm a non-smoker lives in all day, then slid straight back down to pay again. The tube never fixed the itch. It caused it.

So after the last stub there is nothing to wait for. No staircase to climb. No days to count. You do not become free in a week when the tug fades. You are free when the belief dies — which is now, if you mean the vow.

### THE VOW

Stub that last tube. Push it hard into the tray until the glow is out. Look at the mashed end. Smell it. That sour mash is what you used to put in your mouth and call heaven at lighting.

Now say it, out loud if you can, solemn as a promise that matters because it does:

I will never smoke another dose. Not one puff. Not one special occasion. Not tomorrow, not on holiday, not on a bad day. The trap is closed.

Say it with joy, not with a heavy heart. There is no friend in that tray. There is a dead enemy. Congratulate yourself. I congratulate you with all my heart. You have just done what millions believe is impossible and what you believed was impossible for years — you have walked out while still allowed to walk out smiling.

12. SMOKE YOUR FINAL CIGARETTE AND KNOW YOU ARE FREE
Close the trap with joy, not sadness.

From this second you do not wait to become a non-smoker. You are one. Empty hands, clear tongue, coat that will smell of rain tomorrow. Breathe to the bottom of your lungs and notice you do not need to cough to earn the air. That breath is yours without payment.

### TWO DOORS THAT NEVER OPEN AGAIN

Freedom, once conferred, needs only one thing guarded: the belief. Not your behaviour with white knuckles. The belief. Two thoughts will knock in the next days and weeks. Both will sound like you. Neither is you. Both are the old script, pre-labelled.

The first knock comes on a bad day. Argument, bills, phone down hard, hand already moving to the old pocket before the mind catches up. A voice says, very kind: just this once, this is real stress, a cigarette takes care of it.

Hear it with clear eyes. Did leaf ever move one paper on that desk? Did it ever pay one bill or unsay one word? It quieted for minutes its own tug and left the problem untouched. You do not need a dose to cope. You need air, hands free, and the problem itself. The tug will pass whether you feed it or not — fed, it guarantees another; unfed, it starves a little more.

The second knock comes on a good day. Patio, drinks, laugh, a hand extending a pack: go on, one won't hurt. Or a quiet voice after months clear: I have proved I can leave it, I can prove I can take one.

Hear that too. I know I can never have just one. I learned that the hard way, thinking after months I could control myself and just have one, and one rebuilt daily smoking within weeks — doorway, tray, mint, shop-run, divided evenings. One never stays one because one re-teaches the belief that the dose gives something. It gives nothing. A stumble, if it ever happens, is a rumble strip, not a crash. Put it out, see the scratch for what it was, shut the belief again. It warns. It never licenses another on purpose.

So we close both doors now, before they knock.

Whenever the thought of a tube crosses your mind — in the morning when feet have not touched floor, at meal-end when taste lingers, on a break with air on your face, on that night patio — do not argue with it and do not try to not think it. Think this instead, and mean it from the belly:

YIPPEE! I'M FREE!

Say it the first time at this table and feel how true it is. Say it tomorrow at the kettle and taste toast tasting of toast. Say it when you watch other smokers huddle in the rain outside a doorway and feel pity, not envy — they are still timed, still soured, still chained to pocket and tray. You are not being deprived. They are.

That shout is your lifelong script. It turns every old trigger into a small hit of joy. The thought arrives, the shout answers, the moment proves itself free. Within days you will notice mornings, meals and breaks complete without a dose, fuller without the hourly interruption. Within weeks you will wonder how you ever believed leaf owned those moments. It was only ever sneaking a ride on kettle, food, pause, company, night air.

Stand now. Throw the empty pack away — not as a test of courage, but as rubbish, because rubbish is what it is. Wash the tray or bin it. Wash your hands. Lift your head. Walk into the next ordinary hour as a non-smoker who is happy to be one, champing at the bit for the days ahead.

**SUMMARY**
- The last tube showed nothing worth keeping, only stain, ash and sour taste while life around it stayed untouched.
- The restless tug after stubbing is a small dying complaint that peaks quickly and fades when unfed, not proof of need.
- Freedom started at the stub, not after days counted, because the belief died before the body settled.
- A bad day does not make a dose a cure and a good day does not make a dose safe, both thoughts are the old script talking.
- Any future thought of smoking answers itself with joy that smoking is over.
