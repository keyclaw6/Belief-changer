Chapter 3
WHAT DOES IT ACTUALLY DO FOR YOU

*Forget harm against pleasure for a minute and ask a plainer question — what did it ever actually do for you?*

## THE SCALES WE BROUGHT WITH US

We all walked in here carrying scales.

On one side we piled the harm. The cough in the morning. The short breath on the hill. The money burned. The smell in the hair and the coat. The hiding in garages and on back steps. The counting and planning and patting of pockets. The voice at night that says this cannot go on.

On the other side we piled what we called the good. It steadies me. It marks a pause. It finishes a meal. It belongs with company. It tastes good. It is mine.

And for years we stared at those scales and told ourselves the same sad story. The harm side is heavier, yes, but the good side is real, so stopping will hurt. That is why we dreaded the thought of a last dose. Not because we loved coughing. Because we believed something true would be taken from us if we stopped weighing.

I know those scales because I carried them for years. I could recite my list the way other men recite football scores. After food. With coffee. On a break. When the phone would not stop. When the kids were loud. When the pub was loud. When I wanted to think. When I did not want to think. A reason for every hour, a little party after every finished thing.

We call that being honest with ourselves. It is not honesty. It is the Smokescreen doing sums.

A con does not need to deny the harm. It only needs to keep one small credit alive. Keep the smoker saying, well, at least it does this one thing for me, and the circle holds. We will pay thirty doses a day for that one imagined thing. We will stand in rain for it. We Febreeze the hell out of ourselves for it. We will tell our children small lies for it. All to protect a credit we never tested.

So I am not going to argue harm with you here. I am not going to list what smoke puts in lungs and throats and coats and bank statements. You know that list by heart. Every smoker does. Harm never freed anyone, because every smoker who ever lit in rain already knew the harm and lit anyway.

I am going to move the scales off the table altogether.

What if the good side is empty? That is the question that ends the weighing. Not does it harm more than it helps. What help was ever there at all?

Look at our own lives for the answer. We started as non-smokers who laughed and argued and ate and paused and grieved and joked without lighting anything. Then we learned to light, and very soon we could not laugh or argue or eat or pause without lighting. Did life get richer in that change, or did the dose slip itself between us and life and then charge us for standing there?

We say we love smoking and hate being smokers in the same breath. That split sentence tells the truth our scales hide. Love does not divide a man down the middle. A circle does.

You do not have to throw one memory away to keep the other. Keep both, and let them sit side by side without scales.

Keep the flare. That first second when the match takes, the bite, the head swimming for an instant — it was felt. I am not asking you to deny what your nerves reported. Thousands of us said the same in the same words, that the moment of lighting feels like heaven and the minutes after feel shitty. That split is not imagination. It lives in the lighting second only, and it fades within pulls. Hold it there, small and brief, where it belongs.

Keep the other memory too. The stairs that tell. The purse emptied for paper. The day cut into exits, the evening planned around buying ahead, the pride dented when you smiled at your own door with spray still in the air. That is not imagination either. That is what was done to breath and to pocket and to timetable and to pride, day after day, while the flare lasted seconds.

The scales want you to weigh those two memories against each other, as if a second of flare could balance a day of keeping. Put the scales down and you do not have to weigh at all. Say what each memory proves, plain.

The flare proves a tug went quiet for a minute. Nothing more. It does not prove food was finished or company was completed or work was rewarded. It proves the Nipper was fed and went quiet, as it always does, before asking again.

The dented day proves plenty was done to you while you called it for you. Breath shortened. Money burned. Hours portioned. Pride made to hide. Those are not prices paid for a gift. They are what the circle takes to keep itself turning.

Hold both and the split sentence stops tearing you. You can remember the second for what it felt like as a sensation and remember the life for what it did as a servitude, and see both at once without choosing a side to defend. It does plenty TO you. It does nothing FOR you. Once that is seen, there is nothing left to balance. A brief quiet of its own tug on one side, and a long doing-to on the other — not two goods to weigh, but a sensation and a servitude, each in its true size.

Stay with me as an investigator while we look. Not as a defendant. Not as a judge. As one smoker with another, asking what a dose ever added that the moment did not already own.

## SAY IT OUT LOUD

Do this with me now, where you sit.

Say your reasons out loud, in your own dialect, the way you say them to yourself when no one is listening. Not the polite reasons for the doctor. The real ones.

“It calms me down.”
“It marks the end of food.”
“It gives me a break.”
“It goes with a drink and with mates.”
“I like the taste.”
“It is my little reward. My stick. My ciggies. My habit smokes.”

Good. I have heard every one of those from my own mouth. We all carry the same menu because we all learned it in the same place. The nicotine trap teaches the same lines to everyone it holds.

Now keep that list on the table and walk with me through three ordinary moments from yesterday. Not special nights. Not crises. Ordinary moments when we swore the dose mattered.

First, food. Plates pushed back. Warmth still in the belly. Taste still on the tongue. Someone reaches for talk. We reach for the pack. Tap, flick, first pull. We say that pull finishes the meal.

Ask plain. What did that pull add to the food? Did it make the food warmer, richer, better seasoned? Or did the food already do its work, and the dose only interrupted the lingering taste with hot paper and then left a sour mouth where a clean taste had been? Non-smokers at that same table finished the same food, lingered in the same warmth, talked the same talk, and wanted nothing after. They lacked nothing. What did we get that they missed?

Second, the pause. Mid-morning. Eyes tired. Shoulders tight. We step out to the door with the same faces and light and stare at nothing for six minutes and call it a break.

Ask plain. What made that a break? The standing still? The air on the face? The minutes away from the screen? Or the paper tube between the fingers? A non-smoker takes the same six minutes, stretches, looks at sky, rolls shoulders, and comes back clearer, without lighting anything. The pause was real. The legs asked for it. The eyes asked for it. Did the dose create the pause, or did it only ride along while the body did what bodies always do when given a minute?

Third, company. Laughter. Talk running easy. Someone lights. Hands move. Lighters pass. We light too and say the moment is complete because we all lit together.

Ask plain. What completed it? The joke? The friends? The hour off work? Or the shared suck on paper? Put non-smokers at that same table with the same jokes and the same hour, and they laugh just as hard, stay just as long, remember just as much in the morning. The company was the warmth. When did warmth ever need smoke to be warm?

You see what I am doing. I am not yet pulling each excuse apart thread by thread. That work comes, and it comes easily, on ground we are laying now. I am asking you to change the test.

We were taught to ask, does the harm outweigh the pleasure? That question keeps the pleasure assumed. It lets the dose keep credit it never earned.

Ask instead, what pleasure did the moment not already own before the dose arrived? Name one thing the dose brings that a non-smoker lacks in that same food, that same pause, that same laugh.

We cannot name it. No smoker can when pressed to be exact. We point to a feeling, not a thing. A drop in the shoulders. A sense of heaven at lighting. A sense that now the job is done, now I can rest. Feelings are real as feelings. The credit for them is false.

Think of how we spoke about work. “Cigarettes were my reward for…well, almost everything. Teaching a class. Finishing a story. Finishing a paragraph. Driving 500 miles. Driving to the grocery store.” I quote that because we all lived it. The finished class was good. The finished paragraph was good. The drive done was good. Then the dose stepped in front of the good and took the bow.

Who taught the class? We did. Who wrote the paragraph? We did. Who drove the miles? We did. The dose sat in the ashtray while the work was done and then claimed the applause after. That is not a reward. That is a guest who arrives after dinner and says he cooked it.

Keep your list in view. We are not finished with it. But notice how thin it already looks when you ask what was added, not what was risked.

Could a habit that adds nothing hold us for twenty years? Yes, if we believe it adds everything. Belief is the whole lock here. The Nipper only tugs. The Smokescreen tells us what the tug means. Together they make paper look like a friend.

## THE ARMS THAT WOULD NOT LET GO

Let me show you what that friendship feels like from the inside, because this picture will stay with you when the list fades.

Picture dark water. Cold closing over your head. You go down once, twice. Your chest burns. Then arms close around you from behind. Strong arms. They haul you up. You break the surface gasping. You cling. You sob with thanks into the shoulder of whoever holds you. You would love that holder for life.

Now picture this. You are still in the water ten minutes later. The arms are still around you. You try to swim to the bank and the arms hold you back. You try to stand and the arms pull you down to chest depth again. You say, let me go, I can stand now, and the grip tightens. You realize with a cold shock that the same arms that lifted you are keeping you in.

Would you call that rescuer a friend? Would you thank those arms for the lift and agree to stay in the water for life out of gratitude? Or would you see in one flash that the lift and the hold were one trick?

That is what a dose does, and I want you to feel it as lived, not as words.

We thrash in small discomforts all day. Tiredness. Hunger. Irritation. Dullness. Worry. The ordinary weather of being human. Then the dose wraps its arms around us. First pull, shoulders drop an inch, the tug goes quiet. We say, there, it takes care of it. We feel heaven for a second and call the arms our friend.

But watch what those arms do after the lift. They do not put us on the bank. They keep us in the water so we need another lift an hour later. Feed the Nipper and it quiets. Leave it unfed and it nips again — an empty, slightly restless, slightly edgy little tug that we were taught to read as need for another dose. Each quiet plants the next tug. Each tug demands another quiet. Round and round, lift and hold, lift and hold, while life waits on the bank.

Keep the rescuer where it belongs in this picture. The rescuer is not your mate or your partner or your boss. The rescuer is the dose itself. People did not do this to you. A paper tube did, with help from a story about what the tube gives. Warm to people, always. Harsh to the tube and the story. People sat with you at food and laughed with you in pauses and loved you through hard days. The dose only rode along and claimed their work.

Ask the picture three plain questions and answer them to yourself, not to me.

If those arms truly lift you out, why do we need lifting forty times a day, every day, for years, and never once arrive on dry land?

If those arms truly calm the water, why is the smoker the one patting pockets in queues, counting before weekends, waking in the night to plan the morning light, while the non-swimmer sleeps through?

If those arms are a friend, why do we hide the embrace, wash it off our hands, spray it from our coats, lie about where we were held?

There is only one honest answer, and your stomach already knows it before your mouth catches up.

We mistook being held in the water for being saved from it. The lift was real as a sensation. The saving was false. A friend puts you on the bank and lets go. This holder never lets go unless you see the hold and step out yourself.

Hold that swimmer in mind when we look at food and pauses and company again. The meal did not need holding. The pause did not need holding. The laugh did not need holding. The arms only taught us to feel held, then taught us to call held by another name — pleasure, crutch, reward, mine.

## WHAT IT DOES TO, NOT FOR

Now we can say the thing our scales would never let us say.

We were taught to speak of smoking as if it does things for us. It does something for nerves. For taste. For focus. For fun. That little word for carries the whole con. For means on our side, working for us, like food works for hunger and rest works for tiredness.

Watch one dose with full attention, as you smoke as normal, and tell me what you see.

First second, flare and bite, heady and sharp. We call that heaven. “The moment I light up I’m in heaven, but feel shitty afterwards. So whilst I love smoking I hate being a smoker.” Your own sentence, spoken by thousands of us in different rooms. I will not mock that flare. I felt it. Nerves report what nerves report.

Second pull, duller. Third, hot and papery. End, bitter and stale, yellow on fingers, ash on shoe, sour mouth, faint nausea if you attend. Ten minutes after, the faint tug starts again. The heaven lived in the lighting second only. It never spread across the day. It never stayed for the meal or the pause or the company. It flared and died while we watched.

If heaven lived in the tobacco, the tenth dose would shine like the first. It never does. We leave half-doses to go cold. We puff without tasting while talking. We light and forget and light again. A day-long gift would not need chasing every forty minutes. A lighting-second tap needs chasing, because it fades by design.

And what of the hours around that second? What does the dose do there?

It does plenty to breath, making stairs tell. It does plenty to mouth and hair and clothes, making us wash and spray and chew. It does plenty to pocket and purse, making us count and buy ahead. It does plenty to day and night, making us plan exits and stand in cold. It does plenty to pride, making straight men and women lie at their own doors.

Plenty TO. Nothing FOR.

Say that slowly to yourself, not as a slogan but as what you just watched. The flare gave no food, fixed no quarrel, finished no paragraph, held no friend closer. The work was yours. The laugh was yours. The food was yours. The pause was yours. The dose only did things to lungs and fingers and timetables while you did the living.

It does plenty TO you. It does nothing FOR you.

Let that sentence sit where the scales were. It does not argue harm against help. It removes help from the table. Harm we already knew. Help we assumed. Once help is gone, what is left to weigh?

We fed that circle for years because we read the quiet after a dose as a gift. The Nipper asked. The Smokescreen answered, this is calm, this is taste, this is company. We lit. The tug quieted. We called the quiet proof. But the quiet only proved the tug had been there, and the tug had been planted by the previous dose. Paper, tug, story. That was the whole store.

I am not asking you to feel gloomy about that. Gloom belongs to loss, and nothing was lost here. I am asking you to feel the ground shift under a heavy weight you carried. If nothing was ever given, nothing will be missed. The scales were a trick of light. Put them down and your hands are empty for better things.

Think of the closet hide again. Mints. Spray. Socks on cold stone. Grind under heel. Sniff-test. Smile at the door. Would we do all that for something that does for us? We do that for something that does to us and has taught us to call to by the name for. Choosers do not hide. Served men do not hide. Only kept men hide, and we hid daily.

So keep your list, but change its heading. Do not head it What I Get. Head it What I Was Told I Get While Something Did To Me. Read each line under that heading and hear how hollow it rings.

“It calms me.” To what? To the tug it planted an hour before?
“It finishes food.” With what? With a stale end after a clean taste?
“It gives me company.” How? By standing between me and company with something to tend?

It is the other way around. We never lit because life was missing something. Life went missing in small pieces because we kept stopping to light.

## THE ONLY QUESTION LEFT

There is a moment in every escape when argument stops and seeing starts. We are at that moment now.

Forget for a minute what you were told to fear about stopping. Forget trembling hands and bad temper and empty breaks and nights out spoiled. None of that is on the table in this room. We are not talking about stopping yet. We are talking about seeing.

You came in weighing. You said, I know it harms me, but what about what it does for me? I have put another question in front of you. What if it never did anything for you at all, and only did plenty to you while you called to by the name for?

Answer these to yourself, honestly, as one adult to another. No one hears but you.

When non-smokers eat the same food, take the same pause, laugh at the same joke, do they sit there deprived, missing a layer only smoke can give — or do they live the layer fully while we step out to tend paper?

When we light the tenth dose of the day and taste almost nothing, leave it to burn, forget it while talking, what happened to the great taste and the great moment we swore lived in it?

When the heaven second fades within pulls and leaves us planning the next heaven, does that look more like a gift received or a tug quieted for minutes so it can tug again?

You know. You have lived the answers a thousand times. The non-smoker misses nothing. The tenth dose proves the taste was never in the tube. The fading flare proves the heaven was the quieting of a tug, not the adding of a joy.

That shift back to the true owners was done not by me but by you, out of your own days. The food gave. The pause gave. The people gave. The work gave. The dose took time and breath and money and quiet pride, and called the taking by the names of the givers. Once you return each credit to its true owner, the dose stands bare. Nothing else to love. Nothing else to weigh.

I want you to feel how light that leaves the hands. We came in braced for a fight about will and loss. There is no fight here. There is only a cleared table. The thing we thought we would have to mourn was never a friend. It was arms around a swimmer who wants the bank.

Look at what those light hands do now. They do not clutch. They do not weigh. They pick up the pencilled list we left on the table and they do one small honest thing with it.

Take the pencil and cross out the old heading. Cross out What I Get so hard the paper dents. Write above it in plain letters the heading it should have carried all along. What I Was Told I Get While Something Did To Me. Now read the list again under that heading, slowly, out loud if you can.

“It calms me” — under the new heading it reads as what it was. A tug quieted, then planted again. Not calm added to a hard day, but a brief hush of its own noise while the hard day sat untouched.

“It finishes food” — under the new heading it reads as interruption, not finish. The food finished itself. The dose burned over the clean taste and left sour where sweet had been.

“It gives me a break” — under the new heading it reads as rent charged for standing still. Legs and eyes and lungs asked for air and movement. The dose rode in the pocket and took the credit for what air and movement did.

“It goes with mates” — under the new heading it reads as standing between you and mates with something to tend. The laugh held the hour. The friends held the warmth. The dose held the lighter.

Read it that way and notice what the hands want to do next. They do not want to argue the list line by line. They do not want to fold it away for later. They want to put it down. A list that adds nothing needs no answering. It needs leaving. Leave it on the table where the scales were. You came in carrying paper to defend paper. Walk out of this room empty-handed in the best sense — nothing to carry, nothing to weigh, nothing to defend.

That lightness is not a trick of mood. It is what happens when both memories are allowed their true size. The lighting second stays small and brief, a nerve tapped and fading. The doing-to stays large and daily, to breath and to pocket and to timetable and to pride. No weighing between them. No re-counting. Just seeing. And you, larger than all that held you, with hands free.

So begin this part of the walk in the mood it deserves. Not with dread. Dread belongs to sacrifice, and we just saw there is no sacrifice here.

3. BEGIN BY FEELING GREAT TO BE ESCAPING
There is no doom here, only freedom ahead.

SUMMARY
- The harm-against-help scales kept smoking alive by assuming help was real.
- Listing reasons out loud shows food, pauses and company already owned the pleasure.
- Work and talk and meals gave the good, while the dose arrived after and claimed it.
- A rescuer that lifts for a second and then holds you in is not a friend.
- The heaven second lives only in the lighting and fades within pulls.
- Plenty is done to breath, pocket, timetable and pride, while nothing is added for nerves or taste or company.
