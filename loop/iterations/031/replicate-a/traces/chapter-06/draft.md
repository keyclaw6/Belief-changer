Chapter 6
HUNGER, SATISFACTION AND REAL FOOD

IN THIS CHAPTER — the grey-plate fear, the market at nine, warm bread in the hand, the plain favourite meal, the peach-child remembered, hunger trusted and satisfaction heard

*Eating was always the joy; BAD SUGAR only stood in front of it and took the thanks.*

THE GREY FEAR YOU CARRY IN

You arrive with the fuel story dead and the afternoons explained, and I honour you for looking hour by hour without flinching.

The buzz exposed as borrowed brightness. The fog exposed as the bill. The body honoured as the one who fuelled you all along while the dose took the credit.

And now, in the quiet after that seeing, an older fear speaks up. It does not speak at the desk. It speaks at the table.

Without sweets eating will be grey.

Without dessert what is love at the table? Without chocolate what is comfort after a hard day? Without my treats what is left that I actually like? Food will be dull duty. I will chew and swallow and feel nothing.

Dear friend, I know that fear by heart. We all lived it. We called pudding love and called love pudding until we could not tell which warmed us. We called the sweet finish the point of the meal and the meal the waiting room.

I do not scold that fear. I invite it to the table to eat and see.

What if eating itself was always joy, and joy returns whole the moment nothing stands in front of it?

Come and eat with me once, hungry and attentive, and judge by your own mouth.

THE MARKET AT NINE

Come with me to the market at nine on an ordinary morning, hungry in the honest way morning makes you hungry.

No theory. Air, voices, colour. Crates of peppers glossy as varnish. Tomatoes still warm from the van. Herbs smelling green and sharp when you bruise a leaf between finger and thumb. A baker's stall with loaves cracked open at the top, steam finding its way out, crust singing faintly as it cools.

You have not eaten since night. The belly is empty and clear, not gnawing, not panicked, simply open. A clean hollow that says: food would be good now.

Take a piece of warm bread. Just that. Feel the crust resist then give. Hear it. Smell the wheat. The crumb is soft and slightly sweet from the grain itself, chewy, alive. Chew slowly. Notice how the mouth waters to meet it, how the first bite tastes brightest, how the second settles deeper. Notice the hands warming. Notice the head clearing, not flashing, clearing.

Walk on. Taste a grape, a slice of apple, a soft peach when in season. Let the juice run. Real sweetness bursts and then rests. It does not scream for a second mouthful while the first is still there. It arrives, it pleases, it fades cleanly and leaves gratitude behind.

Now sit for your plain favourite meal. Not a prescription. Not a plan. Your own favourite from real food — soup that steams the face, roast vegetables gone crisp at the edges, potatoes, rice, beans, fish or meat if that is your food, bread to wipe the bowl, fruit to finish. Eaten because you are hungry. Eaten slowly enough to notice.

Watch what your body does when left to its own marvellous work.

The first spoonfuls taste almost loud. Hunger is the best seasoning, and you brought plenty. Midway the edge softens. Flavours round. You pause without deciding to pause. You breathe. You talk. You laugh. Then, quietly, enough arrives.

Not stuffed. Not deprived. Satisfied. A warm full stop. The bowl half-finished and you do not care. The body says: thank you, that was enough, go and live. And you can rise and go, light-limbed, clear-headed, with no rustle calling you back.

Ask yourself right there with the spoon still warm:

When did a box ever close like that, with that calm full stop?

When did a sweet dose ever let you walk away mid-packet, content, without a backward glance?

When did flash ever feed the way hunger met feeds?

There is only one honest answer. Real food satisfies. The dose excites and empties. You knew it in your belly long before you had words for it.

HUNGER YOU CAN TRUST

We were taught to fear hunger. The Trap taught us to read every hollow as emergency and every emergency as need for something sweet and quick.

Hunger is not emergency. Hunger is information.

True hunger arrives gradually, open and patient. It grows like morning light, not like an alarm. It welcomes real food — any real food — and makes it taste wonderful. It does not shout for one bright packet. It says: eat soon, eat well, and it will wait while you choose.

You remember this signal from childhood, like that child with the peach who ran off mid-bite when enough came.

No counting. No bargaining. No guilt. The body led and joy followed.

That design never left you. It was only drowned by spikes. The loud echo-hunger after sweets — blown and empty together, mouth coated yet calling for more — is not hunger. It is the aftermath of flash. Learn the difference once by feel and you own it for life.

True hunger: empty, clear, calm, glad at the thought of a meal.

Echo-hunger: twitchy, urgent, narrow, fixed on one sweet hit, never calmed by eating, only multiplied.

Which one built your afternoons? Which one emptied the evening box? Which one ever said stop?

Trust the first. Smile at the second until it passes. Your body manufactures what it needs to keep blood steady and brain lit from plain meals eaten with appetite. You do not need a flood to function. You need hunger honoured and satisfaction heard.

I learned this at my own table with something like shame at how simple it was. Mornings I once dosed early and crashed by ten, I ate plainly when hungry and found ten o'clock clear. Evenings I once grazed joylessly for hours, I ate a real meal hungry and found the evening free — sofa, talk, sleep, without the hand moving on its own. If you live with diabetes, take anything that affects blood sugar or appetite, are pregnant, or carry medical risk around food, follow your clinician first — this changes a belief, it does not touch your care.

IS FAVOURITE A PLAY ON WORDS

Now I hear your sharpest objection, and I welcome it because it proves you are thinking.

Favourite food without sweets? That must be a trick of words. You call plain food favourite to coax me, but you mean second-best. You mean worthy and dull. You mean what I should like, not what I do like.

No trick. No catch. No play.

I mean favourite in the plain sense your tongue understands: what tastes best, satisfies deepest, leaves you gladdest you ate.

Think of your own evidence, not my adjectives.

Recall a holiday meal eaten truly hungry — company laughing, bread passing hand to hand, vegetables bright, meat or beans savoury, fruit shining. Did you love that hour because of the pudding, or did the pudding ride on the love of hunger met, warmth, company, rest earned? Remove the sweet finish in memory and replay the hour. Does the warmth vanish? Does the laughter thin? Does the bread lose savour?

Or take the opposite. Recall the lonely box eaten standing at the counter, not hungry, already full, hand moving fast. Was there company? Was there hunger met? Was there satisfaction? Was there love? Or was there speed and coating and regret before the swallow ended?

The fact is settled: the meal gave. The company gave. Hunger met gave. The dose sneaked a ride and claimed the fare.

You call dessert love because love was in the room when dessert was in the hand. Celebrating with chocolate felt warm because celebration is warm. The warmth belonged to the people, the laughter, the relief of the day done. The chocolate sat in the middle of warmth the way a stone sits in sun and gets called warm. Warm it, the sun did. Sweet it, the scene was already.

Take the scene without the stone and the sun still shines.

REAL SWEETNESS STAYS

But fruit is sweet, you say. Bread turns to sugar. Everything is sugar, so nothing matters. Why draw a line?

Hear the line drawn plain, kindly, without moral paint.

When this book says sugar, it means BAD SUGAR — refined and added free sugars and junk processed hits eaten as sweet flashes, from drinks and bars and biscuits and cakes and their grazing cousins. It does not mean the natural sweetness inside whole fruit, vegetables, and plain meals. That sweetness belongs to real food. It comes wrapped in water, fibre, flavour, bulk that lets hunger and satisfaction do their work. It pleases and then lets you stop.

No good-food scolding. No bad-food shame. No eating plan handed over. Your mouth and belly remain the authority.

Taste the difference yourself while you read on exactly as normal. Eat an apple hungry and note the arc: bright arrival, clean fade, quiet done. Then eat a bar restless and note the arc: flash arrival, loud call, never done. Same word sweet in common speech. Opposite experience in the body. One belongs to eating. The other belongs to the Trap.

And hear this gently, because reward kept many of us longer than hunger did. Treating yourself when you feel you deserve one, celebrating, rewarding yourself after a hard day — that frame made the dose feel like self-payment. I do not forbid you enjoyment and I do not lecture sweetness. I ask you to notice where the payment really landed.

Did the treat pay you, or did it charge you? Did the evening dose thank you for virtue, or did it leave you flat and foggy and reaching again? Did love grow in the house with each box, or did secrecy and regret grow?

Real payment satisfies and frees. False payment excites and binds. You felt both a hundred times. Trust the feeling you already own.

Eating returns to its right size when the false payment leaves: hunger, joy, enough. Nothing counted. Nothing punished. Nothing earned by being good this week.

Eat what you want, when you are hungry, stop when you are satisfied, and never punish yourself again.

Read that again slowly, not as a rule to obey with a clenched jaw, but as permission remembered. What you want, when the body truly wants it, is real food. When you are hungry, food tastes marvellous because hunger makes it marvellous. When you are satisfied, stopping is pleasure, not effort — the pleasure of enough. And punishment — the Monday restart, the scolding mirror, the secret vow — never taught the mouth anything but to want louder.

I promise you as one who feared grey plates for years: plates do not go grey. They go bright. Hunger colours them. Satisfaction frames them. Company lights them. The palate wakes after years of coating and finds flavour where it forgot to look — pepper bite, tomato acid, herb lift, crust chew, peach perfume. Ordinary food tastes more, not less, when the blast stops blinding the tongue.

Walk into tomorrow's meals like this and watch. Breakfast hungry, tasting each bite, stopping at the full stop. Lunch hungry, savouring savoury depth, stopping at the full stop. Evening hungry, company near, fruit to finish, stopping at the full stop. Between meals, no grazing to fill time, no sweet to fill mood. If true hunger calls early, answer early with real food. If satisfaction says done, lay the spoon down gladly and go and live.

That is the whole inhabiting. Not a system. A return.

6. TRUST HUNGER AND SATISFACTION
Eat when hungry, stop when satisfied, enjoy real food.

SUMMARY
- Food without BAD SUGAR does not go grey; hunger makes real food taste bright and satisfaction makes stopping glad.
- True hunger is clear and patient and welcomes a meal, while echo-hunger after sweet flashes is urgent and narrow and never says enough.
- The plain favourite meal eaten hungry satisfies for hours and closes with a calm full stop the box never gives.
- Warmth at celebrations belonged to people and hunger met and rest earned, not to the sweet that rode along.
- Natural sweetness inside whole fruit and plain meals stays part of eating; BAD SUGAR as flash doses is what never satisfies.
- The body keeps itself steady from plain food eaten with appetite, and trusting that signal returns joy to eating.
