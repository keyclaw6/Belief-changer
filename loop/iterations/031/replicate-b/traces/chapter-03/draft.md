Chapter 3
WHAT DOES IT DO FOR YOU

IN THIS CHAPTER
The kitchen table in the morning with yesterday's wrappers. The notebook and the empty benefit column. The 3pm desk drawer. The dentist's mirror. The shop till and the crumpled receipt. The quiet sofa at night.

*Forget for a moment what it does to you, and ask what it ever did for you — you will find you cannot name one thing.*

You Walk In Cleared

You walk into this chapter cleared of one charge.

Last night we stood together in that bright 10pm kitchen and watched your hand move before thought had finished. We saw the half-empty shelf. We saw the blur after one permitted biscuit. We saw Monday virtue turn into Friday night. That was not free choice and it was not weak character. That was the Sugar Trap closing quietly round a decent person.

Take that acquittal with you. You need it for what comes now, because I am going to ask you to look with colder eyes than before, and I want no shame in the room while you look.

Up to now you have argued like this, as we all did. I know it harms me a little. Teeth, weight, money, the afternoon fog. But I get something from it. Life would be greyer without it. On balance the benefit outweighs the harm.

That sounds fair. It sounds adult. It is the last kind wall holding the trap up, and I am going to take that wall down brick by brick, gently, with your own day as the witness.

I speak as one who built that same wall for years. I told myself I knew the costs and accepted them. I told myself a man who works hard has earned his pleasures. I told myself the glow in the mouth was worth the fog an hour later. I believed it because I needed to believe it. We all do that when we feel caught. We call the cage a home so the bars hurt less.

We will not do that any longer.

The Day On Paper

Let us do something plain together. No lecture. No scolding. A plain audit of one ordinary day.

Take yesterday. Not a bad day. Not a binge day you hide from. An ordinary, respectable, moderate day. The kind you would defend.

Morning. Tea with two sugars, or that sweetened cereal that tastes bright for three minutes. Mid-morning biscuit with the second cup, eaten standing, barely tasted because the phone rang. Lunch that was decent enough, finished with something sweet because the meal felt unfinished without it. Afternoon. That low, flat hour when the eyes go heavy. Chocolate from the drawer. Cola, or sweet tea. Ten bright minutes, then the fog again. Evening. Pudding because it was there. Biscuits while the programme played, hand to mouth without looking. Night. The small sweet to close the day.

Write it if you like on a scrap of paper. Time. What you took. What happened next. Mood. Mouth. Wallet. Minutes.

Now draw two columns beside that list. On the left, what did it do TO you. On the right, what did it do FOR you.

Be honest as a friend would be honest at midnight. No showing off. No self-attack. What is true?

What It Did TO You

Start with the left column, because it fills itself.

Mood first. Did yesterday feel steady and clear? Or did it swing? Bright for minutes after each sweet hit, then flat, then twitchy, then hungry for the next? Did the afternoon bring that heavy eyelid fog? Did the evening bring that full-but-empty feeling where the belly is loaded and the mouth still calls? We rode that roller coaster so long we called it normal. Short lift, fast drop, reach again. I would have something quick and sweet but now know that will only give a short term lift which will inevitably be followed by a fast drop. That was not my poetry. That was our life.

Teeth next. You know this without me frightening you. Free sugars feed the acid in plaque that rots teeth. That is plain mechanism, not fate. Brushing and fluoride and a good dentist matter, of course they do, and no single sweet writes your future in the chair. The fact is frequent sweet doses through the day keep that acid working overtime. Look in your own mouth. The fillings. The bills. The careful smile in photographs. That column has entries.

Money and time next, because they add quietly. A bar here. A packet there. A drink at the station. A pudding no one really wanted but everyone paid for. Add the week. Add the month. Add the minutes spent thinking about it, fetching it, queuing for it, hiding it, regretting it. Small coins make a heavy bag when carried daily.

And body load. I will say this flat, because honesty outranks force, and then I will lift the weight at once so you do not carry fear out of this room. Across whole populations, high added-sugar intake walks hand in hand with heavier hearts, higher pressure, tired livers, low-grade inflammation, appetite knocked off its hinge. Guidance now says keep free sugars below a tenth of daily energy, under fifty grams for most adults, with lower better, because ordinary eating sits far above it. Feeling fine does not mean intake is low. That is guidance for crowds, not a sentence on you. If you live with diabetes, take medicine that touches blood sugar or appetite, are pregnant, or carry worry around food, let your clinician lead on eating and use this book for belief alone.

Do you see? The left column writes itself from your own day. Mood swung. Mouth paid. Purse leaked. Hours leaked.

I do not want you to stop from fear of that column. Fear never freed anyone. I want you to see it clearly once, then set it aside, because the real question is not there at all.

And What Did It Do FOR You?

Now the right column. The benefit column. The column the whole defence rests on.

I ask you as an investigator, not as a judge. What did yesterday's sugar do FOR you?

Name one solid thing. Not what it did TO your mood for ten minutes. What it gave you that you did not already own.

Was it pleasure? Take that bright minute with the chocolate. Was the pleasure in the chocolate, or in stopping for a minute, sitting down, breathing out, being kind to yourself after hours of strain? Would a pause, a stretch, a laugh, a warm drink have held the same pleasure without the fog after? When you strip the moment bare, what part needed sugar?

Was it company? That shared dessert, that laugh round the table. Who made the warmth, the sweet or the people? Did the love live in the bowl or in the faces round it?

Was it hunger met? Did the sweet settle you till the next meal, the way soup settles, bread settles, fruit settles? Or did it wake hunger an hour later, louder, more nagging, dressed up as need?

You feel the pinch because the column stays empty. Everything we write on the right, when looked at straight, slides to the left. Pleasure that was really relief. Company that was really people. Hunger met that was really hunger woken.

Tell me in your own dialect, the words we all used. It was sugary somethings to get me through. It was a treat when you feel you deserve one. It was celebrating with chocolate. I have heard each one in my own mouth. I say them with warmth, because the person who says them is honest. The reading is false.

Ask three plain questions and answer with your day in front of you.

First, if it truly gave, why did you need it again so soon? A true gift lasts. Bread lasts. A meal lasts. A kind word lasts. Why did this gift evaporate in minutes and leave you lower than before?

Second, if it truly helped, why did the helped hours get worse, not better? Why did the afternoons that were topped up fog hardest? Why did the evenings that were rewarded feel flattest?

Third, if it truly loved you, why did it take while it gave? Money, teeth, clarity, calm — why does every small glow send a bill?

There is only one honest answer, and it lands like a key turning. Nothing was given. Something was taken and a fraction was handed back as relief.

A Genuine Treat Or Fuel

Here is the phrase that has run your life without ever being examined. I want to lift it into the light and hold it there until you see through it.

You believed bad sugar was a genuine treat or fuel.

Say it slowly. A genuine treat or fuel. That is the whole case for the defence in five words. A treat says it gives joy. Fuel says it gives energy. Treat for the heart. Fuel for the body. Who would refuse both?

We breathed that phrase in before we could spell. Sweet means love. Sweet means reward. Sweet means go. Teachers gave it for good work. Grandmothers pressed it into tears. Advertisements poured laughing families round it as if laughter lived inside the packet. Tired parents bought peace with it at the till. We learned to read every dip as a call for it and every triumph as a reason for it.

Look at what those five words really cover.

Treat? A true treat can be taken singly, noticed, finished, forgotten. A peach can. A meal can. Yesterday's sweets could not. They rushed. They blurred. They called for more before tasting ended. I just crave sweets and when i eat them, I feel like i cant stop. That is not treat behaviour. That is dose behaviour. A treat releases you. A dose recruits you.

Fuel? True fuel builds and steadies. Real food eaten hungry gives hours of clear, quiet energy. Yesterday's top-ups gave minutes of buzz and hours of fog. That is not fuel behaviour. That is a loan shark's behaviour. A small bright loan, a heavy dim repayment.

So the phrase fails at both ends. Not genuine. Not treat. Not fuel. A learned label stuck on a tiny body echo — that empty, restless dip after the last hit — misread as proof of a gift.

Do you see the trick? The low you felt was real. The reading of the low was false. The low did not prove you needed sugar. The low proved the last sugar was wearing off.

Once seen, those five words can never rule you again. Whenever the old glow whispers, test it against that exact phrase. Was that a genuine treat or fuel? Or was it a dose that took more than it handed back? You already know yesterday's answer. The right column stands empty.

Who Rescued Whom?

Let me put one picture before you, not as a full story for tonight, only as a question to carry.

What if a man pushed you into a ditch every morning, then came at noon with a clean hand and lifted you out, and you thanked him with all your heart for the rescue?

Would you call him kind? Would you say you needed him? Would you pay him for the lift?

Or would you see, with a flash, that rescuer and pusher were the same man, and the thanks belonged nowhere?

Ask that of yesterday. Who made the 4pm fog that the 4pm bar then lifted? Who made the evening flatness that the evening packet then soothed? Who dug the hole that the next dose was thanked for filling?

We were warm, grateful friends to our rescuer. We never looked at his other hand, the hand that dug.

Keep that question in your pocket. We will open it fully later when your eyes are wider. For now let it do its quiet work. Every time relief arrives after a sweet, ask who sold you the discomfort it relieves.

For The Rest Of Your Life

Now widen the lens, because a day is a year in miniature.

Multiply yesterday by weeks. By years. By decades.

Do you want to ride that lift-drop-reach loop for the rest of your life for something that never gave? Do you want to pay the mood bill, the teeth bill, the money bill, the time bill, year after year, for an empty right column? Do you want to stand at midnight kitchens at fifty, sixty, seventy, telling the same kind lie, I have managed to be good this week, while the box stands empty again?

Look at that picture steadily. Not to frighten you. To bore you with its sameness. The trap has no new act. Only repetition. The same bright minute. The same dim hour. The same regret. The same vow. That is what continuing means. Not drama. Dreariness. A treadmill painted like a treat cupboard.

Now turn the picture over.

Imagine mornings, shops, meals, evenings lived clear for the rest of your life with nothing to miss and nothing to count. Hunger arriving clean. Food tasting bright. Stop arriving quiet. No drawer calling. No till ambush. No midnight rustle. No Monday vow because Friday never collapsed. That is not fantasy. That is the natural baseline waiting beneath the noise, ready to stand up the moment the false benefit is seen through.

Which rest of life do you want? The loop that takes and thanks you for the taking? Or the clear days that ask for no thanks because they take nothing?

You do not have to earn the second picture. You have to stop paying for the first.

It does plenty TO you. It does nothing FOR you.

That is yesterday's audit in one line. Read your paper again. Left column full. Right column empty. Mood swung TO low. Teeth worked TO decay. Purse drained TO coins. Hours drained TO minutes. Nothing handed FOR joy that was not already yours, FOR energy that did not already live in your body, FOR love that did not already sit round your table.

It does plenty TO you. It does nothing FOR you.

Say it in your own mouth until it sounds ordinary, because ordinary truth is what frees. No drama. No struggle. A plain seen fact. The benefit you weighed against the harm never existed. There is nothing to weigh.

Once that lands, the whole harm debate falls away. We need not argue whether one sweet harms a little or a lot. We need not count grams or trade horrors. If there is no benefit, the amount of harm stops mattering. You do not negotiate a safe number of ditches to be pushed into. You stop walking with the pusher.

3. JUDGE ONLY BY WHAT IT DOES FOR YOU
Forget harm versus benefit and ask for the benefit.

SUMMARY
- An ordinary day fills the cost column from your own life and leaves the benefit column empty.
- Frequent sweet doses keep mood swinging and keep plaque acid working, while money and hours leak daily.
- The lift that feels like help is relief of a low the last dose left behind.
- Treat and fuel fail their own test because a true gift would last and settle.
- The rescue you thanked was the same hand that dug the hole.
- Continuing means repeating the same loop for years for nothing given.
- Freedom means clear days with hunger and satisfaction leading again.
