### HISTORY VERSUS HEREDITY — WHAT THE NUMBERS REALLY SHOW

Let us run the historical evidence operator together, because this is where the "born this way" story collapses under its own weight.

The addictive-personality story says: a small group of people carry a defective gene, a weak character, an unlucky brain. They become addicts. The rest don't. If that were true, numbers would stay small and steady across history. The defect would be rare.

Is that what we see?

No. We see the opposite.

When researchers use the same addiction-like questionnaires we use for alcohol or tobacco and point them at highly palatable, sugar-rich foods, around one in seven adults and around one in nine children score in that range. Roughly fourteen percent of adults. Twelve percent of children. About the same share as we see for alcohol or tobacco on those same kinds of self-report scales.

Do those numbers sound rare? Do they sound like a quirk in a few unlucky personalities?

They sound like a trap that catches many.

And crucially — those questionnaires are self-report checklists of experience: loss of control, craving, needing more to get the same effect, use despite wanting to stop. They are not a doctor's diagnosis of a disease you carry for life. They describe a *pattern* people recognise in themselves. The pattern is real. The label "diseased personality" is not.

Think of what that means.

If fourteen percent of adults share the very same "I can't stop after one bite" experience, are fourteen percent of us born defective? Or has something in our world become engineered to produce the same experience in many different kinds of people?

Brilliant, normal, strong-willed people — teachers, nurses, drivers, parents — all reporting the same loop. All blaming themselves. All saying "I just crave sweets and when I eat them, I feel like I can't stop."

Does that sound like fourteen percent of human nature suddenly broke? Or like a trap became very good at catching human nature?

You know the answer. And I want you to feel the relief hidden in it.

If the trait made the Trap, the Trap would be small. If the Trap made the trait, the Trap will be large — and history will show it growing as the product changes, as portions grow, as sugar is added to everything, as snacking becomes normal.

And history does. Our grandparents did not live with biscuits in every meeting room, milkshakes the size of meals, sugar in bread, in sauces, in "healthy" yogurt. We do. The engineered availability changed. The reported loss of control changed with it.

That is not heredity. That is manufacture.

Scientists themselves are openly split on what to call this. Some labs showcase how intermittent sugar access in animals can drive bingeing, dopamine spikes and a dip afterwards, and cue-triggered craving. Other leading neuroscientists — at Cambridge and elsewhere — look at the same data and say: careful, the bingeing appears when access is intermittent and restricted, not when sugar is simply there; human evidence for a true sugar addiction diagnosis is thin and contested; "sugar is addictive" remains an open question, not a closed verdict.

Do I tell you this to confuse you? No. I tell you to free you.

Both sides, even disagreeing, agree on what you feel: under certain schedules — diet, restrict, crave, binge — binge behaviours appear. The name is contested. The experience is not.

Does that sound like proof you were born an addict? Or proof you were caught in a schedule that creates the very restlessness you then call your personality?

Pick any member of your family who says they "can take or leave it." Watch them on a week of intermittent treats — skip breakfast, tell them to keep the special ones, make sweets visible and then forbidden. Will that person look calmly free? Or will that person start to look oddly like you on a diet week — a little edgy, a little preoccupied, a little "I-need-to-eat-something-RIGHT-NOW"?

You have seen it. We have all seen it.

The fact is, the pattern travels. The personality does not.

### IT'S THE OTHER WAY AROUND — HOW THE TRAP MADE THE TRAIT YOU CALL ADDICTIVE

We need to make visible what the Sweet Story kept invisible.

We have named the two monsters before. The Nibbler is the tiny physical loop — an empty, restless, faintly edgy gnawing, barely perceptible, already dying when you stop feeding it. The Sweet Story is the big brainwashing that interprets that gnawing as "I need sugar, I am an addictive person, I cannot cope without a treat."

The Nibbler is trivial. The Sweet Story is enormous. Kill the Story and the Nibbler starves with delight.

How did the Story make you feel addictive?

In two quiet, plausible ways that brain imaging has made visible, without proving destiny.

First, crowding. Think of a loud room where everyone shouts. Eventually you turn up the volume to be heard. In the brain's reward circuits, repeated intense hits — especially fat plus sugar together, the foods the Trap engineers to be "lonely together" — can blunt the signal, so the same dose feels like less and you learn to chase more to hear anything at all. That is why "family size now" feels normal. Not because you are greedy. Because the circuit was crowded. The interpretation that fits the imaging is that tolerance-like blunting may explain needing more. It is not proof of a broken brain. It is consistent with why escalation happens and stops when the hits stop.

Second, hyper-reactivity. The wrapper, the smell, the aisle, the advert, the time of day — your brain learns cues that predict the dose and fires *before* you taste anything. That is why craving can hit hard while the food is still in the packet. You have not eaten. You are not hungry. Yet the gnawing starts. That is not personality either. That is learned reactivity to cues, firing ahead of the bite.

Do you see the inversion?

We thought the craving proved we were addictive. It's the other way around. The engineered hits created blunting and cue-reactivity, which created the craving, which we then labelled "addictive personality."

We thought we needed more because we were more needy. It's the other way around. We needed more because the Trap steadily crowded the signal, then blamed us for noticing it had gone quiet.

It's the other way around. Every single time.
