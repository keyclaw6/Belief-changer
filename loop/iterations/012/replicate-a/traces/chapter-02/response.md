### IF YOU LIKE IT, WHY DO YOU NEED THIS BOOK?

You like sugar. Is that true?

If it is true, why are you holding this book?

We need to answer that honestly together. Not harshly. Honestly.

We all know what real liking feels like. You like your favourite music. Do you need a book to stop listening to it? You like a walk on a clear morning. Do you hide the evidence of that walk? Do you promise yourself you will not walk again until Monday? Do you argue with yourself in the shop about whether you are allowed to take a walk?

You know the answer. Of course not.

So why does BAD SUGAR need rules? Why does it need "I don't keep it in the house" and "once a week is fine" and "I've been good this week so I can"? Why does it need Monday restarts and hidden wrappers and family size where small once did? When did liking something start to feel like managing something?

Think of the last month. How many quiet bargains did you make? Just one biscuit with tea. No biscuits before lunch. Nothing after eight. Just a treat on Fridays. Just the small bar, not the big one. How many of those bargains lasted? A day? An evening? An hour? And when they broke, what did you tell yourself? That you lacked discipline? That you were weak?

We fell into that story together. All of us did. We thought the problem was us.

Here is the fact that ends that story. You are not uniquely weak.

On validated questionnaires that ask about addiction-like feelings toward highly palatable food — not a doctor's diagnosis stamped on you, simply self-report scales where people tick what they feel — about 14 in every 100 adults and about 12 in every 100 children reach the threshold. That number is similar to what we see for alcohol and tobacco on the same kind of scales. Does that prove you are diseased? No. It proves you are normal. It proves normal wiring meeting an engineered food supply produces the same stuck feeling in millions. Is that evidence that millions are defective? Or that the trap is good at its job?

Do you see the relief in that? Does it soften the self-blame, even a little?

If you wanted to quit a hobby you enjoy, would you need a book on how to quit it? Would you feel uneasy before you even opened the first chapter? Would you scan the pages hoping for a trick to make the deprivation easier?

The fact is you are not reading this because you love sugar. You are reading this because a part of you already senses sugar is not free.

Do you feel that flicker of unease? Is it true that you sense something has taken hold? That daily grazing has become invisible because everyone does it? That the culture tells you a biscuit with every tea and a sweet after every meal is normal, so you never call it a problem?

We will make that sense visible. And we will be warm about it. There is nothing shameful here.

### "JUST A TREAT" — WHOSE VOICE IS THAT?

Listen to the thought that arrives, uninvited, at 3.30 p.m. or at 9 p.m. on the sofa. What does it sound like?

"Just a treat."

"I deserve it."

"I've been good this week."

"Once a week is fine."

"I don't keep it in the house — except for the kids."

"Just this once and then I'll be strict."

Does that sound like freedom? Does that sound like a person calmly choosing a pleasure? Or does it sound like someone negotiating with something?

Whose voice is that, really? Yours? Or a script you were handed?

We were handed that script early. Family. Advertisements. Friends. The office tin passed round at eleven. The cinema ritual. The birthday cake as love. The well-meaning adult who said, "Have a sweet, you’ve earned it." Over years, that repetition becomes brainwashing. Not a insult. A description. An installed belief that BAD SUGAR is pleasure, comfort, reward. We did not decide that belief in a calm, examined way. We absorbed it whole, before we ever questioned it. Did you fear stress when you had your first sweet as a child? Did you need reward then? Did you decide then that chocolate equals love? Or did someone tell you it did, and you believed them because you trusted them?

We fell for that because we were open-hearted. Not because we were foolish.

And once the belief is installed, the behaviour feels like choice. Of course it does. How could it not? If you believe sugar gives you a genuine lift, what could be more logical than to choose it when you feel low? If you believe it comforts, what could be kinder than to reach for it when you are tired?

Do you see the trap in that logic? Does choosing on false information feel like free choice? Or does it feel like being guided?

If I sell you a ticket I swear will take you to the beach, and you board the train eagerly, are you freely choosing the beach? Yes. Are you freely choosing to end up in a goods yard in the rain? No. You were conned.

That distinction is everything. You are not weak. You were misled.

And the misguiding does not happen once. It happens every day. The world grazes. Daily. The packet on the desk. The cereal that is dessert for breakfast. The white bread that behaves exactly like sugar in your body but is called savoury. When everyone grazes, no one calls it grazing. When everyone snacks, snacking looks like normal eating. How would you know you were trapped if the cage was decorated to look like a kitchen?

Do you recognise that? Have you told yourself, "I’m fine — I don’t have a real problem like other people"? Has that story kept you comfortable while the trap stayed shut? That story is part of the trap. We will close it with kindness, not with judgment.

You have absolutely nothing to lose and everything to gain by seeing this clearly. Keep that promise in mind. You risk nothing by looking.

### THE CONFIDENCE TRICK THAT STOLE YOUR CHOICE

I promised you a picture that dissolves choice without shame. Here it is.

A confidence trick is not about stupidity. It is about phoney information.

A well-dressed stranger sells you a special coin. He says, "Put this in your pocket. It will fill your wallet while you sleep." You are sensible. You are cautious. But his information seems plausible. Everyone seems to have one. Your parents had one. Your friends have one. The advertisement shows happy people holding theirs. So you buy the coin.

For a little while you feel richer. Perhaps you do. Then you notice your wallet is lighter. Coins leaking away. Bills thinner. You feel vaguely low, slightly restless, a little flat. You do not connect that feeling to the coin. Why would you? The coin was sold as help.

The same stranger returns, friendly, concerned. "You look low," he says. "You need a top-up. Another coin will lift you." You buy the refill. You feel briefly topped up. Flatness lifts for twenty minutes. Then you are lighter again. Lower again. More restless again.

Are you low because the coin wore off? Or because the coin itself drained you and sold you the drain as relief?

That is exactly what BAD SUGAR does. That is the Sugar Trap.

We need to name it properly, because once named it becomes visible. This is not sugar as food. This is the Sugar Trap — the engineered loop where a dose creates the very emptiness the next dose pretends to solve. You were not born wanting it. You were taught to want it. You tried your first sweets on phoney information: that they were a treat, a reward, a normal part of eating. That information was false. On that false information you made a sensible choice to try. The trap did the rest. Every dose after the first did two jobs at once. It briefly quieted the low the previous dose created, so it felt like help, and it guaranteed the low would return.

Is that not the perfect con? To cause the discomfort and then sell the cure? To be both perpetrator and rescuer, while you thank the rescuer?

Do you see now why you hold this book? If you truly chose sugar the way you choose a hobby you love, you would not need a method. You would either enjoy it or you would stop. The very fact you are reading — the history of rules, the creep from small to family size, the "once I start I can't stop" evening — proves choice was removed. Not by you. By the trap.

Let me ask you plainly. If you wanted to stop drinking water, would you need willpower? Would you need a book? Would you bargain with yourself about water? No. Because water genuinely helps you and you know when you need it. BAD SUGAR mimics that feeling. It creates a mild dip and then, when you feed it, the dip lifts for a moment. You mistake the ending of its own discomfort for a benefit. We all did. We all fell for the coin.

Are you conned? Yes. Are you defective? No. Ever.

I know this is hard to accept. You may be thinking, "But I do enjoy it sometimes. Surely that counts." We will examine every "sometimes" together in the next chapters. For now just allow the question: if it truly pleased you, would you feel relief or regret ten minutes after the packet is empty? Would you hide the wrapper if it were joy?

You were conned, not defective. The Sugar Trap removed your sense of free choice before you knew choice was at stake. See that, and blame relocates where it belongs. Off you. Onto the trap. Stay gentle with yourself. Get stern with the trap.

This is not a small point. This is the whole foundation. While you believed you freely chose sugar because you liked it, you had to blame yourself for not stopping. Once you see you chose on false information and the trap then held you, self-blame collapses. What remains is relief. Good. Let it remain.

### WHY THE YO-YO WAS BUILT INTO THE TRAP

If choice were real, moderation would be easy. Is moderation easy?

Ask anyone who has tried. "I'll just have a little." "I'll cut down." "I'll keep it to weekends." "I'll allow one treat." What happens? One bite becomes the box. Why?

Because the schedule is the trap's form.

Here is what careful work shows, in a way that must be stated cautiously and honestly. In animal research, when subjects are given intermittent access — a window of sugar, then a gap, then a window again — they escalate. They binge. They take larger volumes. They show craving. When the same subjects are given ad-lib access with no forbidden window, those escalation signs do not appear. This is animal work, smaller in magnitude than drug effects, not a diagnosis stamped on you, but the pattern is clear and the permitted inference is plain: intermittent access drives escalation while unrestricted access does not.

Does that pattern remind you of anything human? Diet, then binge. Restriction, then loss of control. "I don't keep it in the house" followed by the late-night shop for the family size. "Just a treat" that was never just one. The yo-yo you thought proved you lacked discipline is the schedule doing its job. The method of "cut down" teaches you to value the dose more.

Think. What do we make precious? The forbidden. What do we fixate on? The scarce. Tell a person they can have as much air as they want and they forget air. Tell them air is restricted until Friday and they dream about air. BAD SUGAR works the same way when you diet it, limit it, make it a special occasion. You make the hit more potent in your mind. You intensify the illusory relief when it comes. You guarantee creep.

We did this together, did we not? We told ourselves moderation was reasonable and adult. Sensible. We told ourselves total freedom sounded extreme. And then moderation taught us to binge.

Do you see the inversion? The sensible-sounding rule was the trap tightening. The extreme-sounding freedom is the door out.

This also explains why willpower feels necessary. If you believe the dose helps, and you then forbid the dose, of course you must fight. That fight is the Willpower Method — pushing on the hinges while the door says pull. You have felt that fight. It felt huge. It felt like proof that sugar is powerful. It was proof that the method was wrong.

Do you have to use willpower to not eat something you see does nothing FOR you? Do you need discipline to avoid a food that never rises above the baseline of a person who never started? Or do you simply stop wanting it once you see the trick?

We will demolish every claimed benefit for BAD SUGAR in the coming chapters — the lift, the taste, the reward — and you will see there is nothing FOR you to fight for. But even now you can feel the logic shift. If intermittent access drives the binge, then every attempt to control sugar by keeping it scarce was not failing you. It was feeding the Sugar Trap.

Are you starting to suspect that the trap is cleverer than you gave it credit for? And simpler to escape than you feared? Good. Hold both.

And allow one more honest limit. I am describing a pattern, not labelling you. This is self-report and behavioural pattern, not a disease identity you must carry for the rest of your life. You are not condemned. You are not helpless. You are not stuck. The pattern can be switched off by switching off the belief that powers it. That is what we are doing.

### THE TUG-OF-WAR YOU THOUGHT WAS YOU

Inside you there has been a pull. We need to name it, or you will keep mistaking it for yourself.

You want to stop. You want to keep. You want to be free. You want the comfort. You feel proud when you restrict. You feel punished when you restrict. You feel relief when you give in. You feel shame when you give in. Does that sound familiar? Does it sound like you are torn?

We call that torn state the Tug-of-War.

Picture it plainly. A thick rope. You in the middle. One team shouting, "Stop! Think of your health, your energy, your teeth, your freedom for the rest of your life!" The other team shouting, "Go on! Just one! You deserve it! You need it! Life is dull without it!" You pull one way, then the other. Exhausting. No wonder you feel tired. Who would not feel tired pulled like that every day for years?

Here is the thing that changes everything. Both teams are not you.

Both ropes are held by the Sugar Trap.

I know this is hard to accept. But test it. When the "just one" thought arrives, does it arrive as a calm, examined decision? Or as a sudden urge, a little whine, a nudge that speaks in your voice but serves the trap? When the "you must stop, you are weak" thought arrives, does it arrive as kind truth? Or as a harsh, shaming bark that also serves the trap by making you feel defeated?

We put those voices there. The Con — not yet named fully, but you sense it — fed you the belief that BAD SUGAR helps, then the Willpower Method fed you the belief that stopping must be hard. Between those two, you were tugged both ways and called it personality.

It was not personality. It was the Sugar Trap holding both ends of the Tug-of-War while you stood in the middle believing the pull was you.

Do you see the relief if that is true? If the pull is not your nature, you are not torn. The trap is torn and you are caught in its game. Step out of the game and the pull stops.

We are not collapsing this War today. We are naming it. That is enough. We want you to notice, tomorrow and the next day, each time the rope tugs. Notice which script speaks: "just a treat" or "you failed again." Label it quietly: there is the Sugar Trap pulling. There is the Tug-of-War. Not me. The trap.

And notice, too, the horizon this reframe opens. You are not choosing between pleasure and health for the rest of your life. You are choosing between slavery and freedom for the rest of your life. Same phrase, opposite meanings. Hooked for the rest of your life or free for the rest of your life. Which is deprivation? Which is gift?

You have absolutely nothing to lose and everything to gain by seeing the War for what it is. Keep that promise close.

Short verdicts now. Plain. Let them land.

You did not choose to be hooked.

The hook was engineered.

You are not the Tug-of-War. You are the one being tugged.

Once seen, it cannot be unseen.
