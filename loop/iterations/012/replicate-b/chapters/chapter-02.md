### THE STORY YOU TELL YOURSELF ABOUT CHOICE

Do you choose sugar because you like it?

That is the story most of us tell. I told it too.

"I choose it. I like it. I could stop if I wanted to. I just don't want to right now."

Does that sound familiar?

If you believe you freely choose it, then the whole problem feels like your fault. Your taste. Your weakness. Your decision. And if it is your decision, why do you need a book to change it?

Ask yourself that question plainly. If you wanted to quit a hobby you genuinely enjoy, would you need a book on how to stop?

Would a person who loves playing tennis need a manual to stop playing tennis?

Of course not. They would just stop. Or just play less. No conflict. No secrecy. No shame. No book.

Do you need a book to stop eating broccoli?

We fell into a strange place with BAD SUGAR. We say "I choose it" in the afternoon and then we feel chosen by it in the evening. We say "just a treat" while we feel a tug that does not feel like a free choice at all.

Which is it? Choice or compulsion?

I want to settle this now, because everything else depends on it. If you are freely choosing, then freedom means sacrificing a pleasure you want. If you are not freely choosing, freedom means escaping a trap you never wanted.

We need to see which is true. Not as an idea. As your own lived experience.

Do you feel free when you eat BAD SUGAR? Or do you feel a little caught?

### TAKE THE HONEST INVENTORY

Before I show you the trick, do a quick inventory with me. No judgement. Just facts.

You hold this book because some part of you wants to be free. That is honest. That is brave. What else is true?

Have you tried to moderate?

Have you said, "I don't keep it in the house"? Did it stay out of the house? Or did it find its way back in on a tired Thursday?

Have you said, "just a treat"? How many times did that treat become two, then the packet?

Have you said, "once a week is fine"? Did the week keep its shape? Or did the special occasion slowly stretch — a birthday, then a bad day, then a Friday — until once a week meant most days?

Have you tried "I will be good this week"? Did being good make you crave the reward even more?

You know the pattern I mean. We set a rule. We keep it for a while. Then we break it. Then we make a new rule. The rules get clever. The result stays the same.

Is that the behaviour of a free choice?

Consider the society around you. This matters. When everyone grazes, no one thinks grazing is a problem. When the office has biscuits every day, when the petrol station sells family-size chocolate at the till, when your friends offer cake as love, daily BAD SUGAR never reads as a problem. It reads as normal.

But what does normal mean here?

On validated self-report scales — simple questionnaires where people tick how they feel about highly palatable food — about fourteen in every hundred adults and about twelve in every hundred children score in the range that looks like addiction. That figure sits in the same neighbourhood as alcohol or tobacco on the same kinds of self-report scales.

What does that tell you?

It tells you that you are not uniquely weak. It tells you that many ordinary people feel a pull they did not choose. It does not mean you have a disease. Those scales are self-report, not a medical diagnosis. I am not labelling you. I am not calling you defective. I am saying: if so many people feel "once I start I can't stop," the thing doing the pulling is the product and the pattern, not your character.

We were all taught to graze. We were all taught to call it a treat. Society made the abnormal feel normal. So your feeling of being normal while being hooked is not proof you are free. It is proof the trap is everywhere.

Do you see? The inventory points one way.

You have not been freely choosing. You have been trying to control something that was controlling you, with rules that were never going to work.

Why did the rules never work? Because the schedule itself is part of the trap.

### THE SCHEDULE IS THE FORM OF THE TRAP

Here is something vital that is easy to miss. It is not just the sugar. It is the pattern.

In careful animal work, when access to sugar is intermittent — on, then off, then on again, with periods of going without — intake escalates and binge volume grows. When the same sugar is available freely all the time with no restriction, that escalation does not happen. No binge pattern. No dependency signs.

Can you hear what that means for you?

The diet-binge yo-yo you know so well — restrict, crave, allow a little, lose control, restrict again — has the same shape. The pattern drives the pull. The on-off schedule makes each dose feel more precious, more charged, more like relief. Ad-lib grazing without rules does not produce the same trap. Intermittent rules do.

This comes from animal work, so we must be cautious translating it to people. It is a pattern inference, not a full explanation of your life. And it does not mean chemistry does not matter. It means the schedule is the trap's form.

Think of your own attempts. Did going without make you want it more? Did "I will only have it at weekends" make Friday feel electric? Did cutting down make each bite feel like a hit?

That is not weakness. That is the schedule doing its job.

We blame ourselves for lacking control. The fact is the schedule removed the feeling of control before you entered the room.

So we have two facts side by side.

First, you are not alone and not uniquely flawed — many ordinary people report the same hooked pattern on the same kinds of scales.

Second, the intermittent pattern you were taught to use to control it — the very diet rules and moderation promises we all learn — is the form that keeps the pull alive.

Does that sound like free choice? Or does it sound like a trap that was built around you while you thought you were choosing?

It is the second. And once you see how the trap was built, the shame falls away.

### THE CONFIDENCE TRICK THAT REMOVED YOUR CHOICE

Now I want to show you the trick itself. I will be warm to you and harsh to the trap. You deserve warmth. The trap deserves none.

Imagine a man in a smart coat. He stops you in the street. He has a coin. He says this coin will make you feel better whenever you feel flat. He shows you charts. He shows you happy people holding the coin. Your family held it. Your friends hold it. Everyone holds it. He says, "Try it. No risk. Everyone does."

You try it. It does nothing special at first. But you trust the information. He told you it helps. So you ascribe any good feeling after to the coin. You had a laugh with friends — the coin was in your pocket, so you credit the coin. You rested after work — the coin was in your hand, so you credit the coin.

Then the second part of the trick. The coin is designed to drain a little of your money every hour you carry it, quietly, invisibly. You feel a little worse. A little emptier. A little restless. You do not connect it to the coin. Why would you? The man told you the coin *relieves* emptiness.

What do you do when you feel empty? You rub the coin, as instructed. For a moment the draining stops. You feel relief. You think, "See? The coin helped."

Do you see the con?

The man sold you a problem and then sold you the brief pause of the problem as the solution. You were not foolish to buy it. You were given phoney information and you acted logically on it. The con was in the information, not in you.

BAD SUGAR works the same. We were sold the idea that it gives us a genuine pleasure, a reward, a lift. We believed it. So we reached for it whenever we felt flat, tired, or in need of love. Each dose created the next low — a little restlessness, a little emptiness, the very feeling you wanted to fix — and then, briefly, relieved it. We then credited the relief to the dose.

We fell into the Sugar Trap the way anyone falls into a confidence trick: with trust, not stupidity.

You were conned. Not defective.

Do you feel the difference? If you were defective, you would need to fix yourself with effort. If you were conned, you need to see the con and it loses its power. The first demands willpower. The second needs understanding.

And here is the question that closes the circle. If you truly freely chose BAD SUGAR because you like it, would you need to read a chapter called "Are You Really Choosing?"

Would you be taking an inventory of failed moderation attempts? Would you be hiding wrappers? Would you be saying "just a treat" through a mouth that already wants the next one?

You would not.

The fact is you are reading this because some part of you already knows the choice was removed. The Sugar Trap removed it while you were told you were choosing. That is what traps do.

There is no shame in being conned. There is only relief in seeing it. Because once you see it, you stop blaming yourself and you start blaming the trap. And the trap, unlike you, deserves the blame.

This is the Sugar Trap: a set of false beliefs about benefit, kept alive by an on-off schedule that makes each dose feel like relief, sold to you as normal by a world that is itself trapped.

Do you see it now? Can you feel the weight shift — from "what is wrong with me?" to "what was done to me?"

Good. That unease you feel is the old story wobbling. The relief underneath is the truth arriving.

You have not lost control. You were never given real control to begin with. The trap took it. You can take it back.

And I want to name the strange state this creates inside you. It is called the Tug-of-War.

You feel it now, don't you? One rope says, "I want to be free." The other says, "But I like it. I need it." You pull both.

Whose ropes are they?

Both are held by the trap.

One rope is fear that you will never escape. The other is fear that life without it will be flat. Both fears speak in your voice, but both were planted by the con. We have not yet collapsed that war — we will, fully, in a later chapter. For now, just name it when you feel it. Say, "There is the Tug-of-War." That name alone loosens it.

Because you do not need to win the Tug-of-War by pulling harder. You need to see who is holding the ropes. And then you let go.

### THE QUESTION THAT PROVES YOU ARE NOT FREE — AND THE PROMISE THAT YOU CAN BE

Let me bring this to a point. I want to be plain and then be kind.

If BAD SUGAR were a genuine free choice that made your life better, this book would be useless. You would not be here. You would not have tried to moderate. You would not know the floor-and-tin feeling. You would not know the "just a treat" lie you tell with love for yourself and a little shame behind it.

You are here because a part of you senses the trap. That sense is correct.

Do you really choose it? Or does the trap choose for you and then tell you that you chose?

You know the honest answer. You felt it in the inventory. You felt it in the schedule. You saw it in the con.

And here is the promise that follows.

If the choice was removed by a con, the choice can be returned by seeing the con. No need to become someone else. No need to become stronger. You need to see clearly. The trap lives on belief. Correct the belief and the desire collapses. Then behaviour follows without a fight.

Will you let that be true? Will you allow that the unease you feel now is not weakness but intelligence — your mind noticing the trick?

You have absolutely nothing to lose and everything to gain by seeing this through.

Think what "free choice" really costs you for the rest of your life if you stay inside the Sugar Trap. Money, every single day. Energy, every afternoon. Peace, every evening when you bargain about tomorrow. And think what freedom gives you for the rest of your life if you step out — the same days, but without the tug, without the bargaining, without the empty gnaw that was never yours.

Same horizon. Two opposite lives. One trap.

You were not weak. The Willpower Method you were taught — the whole apparatus that says this is a genuine pleasure you must deny through effort — made you feel weak while it kept the con alive. We do not use that method here. We see the con. We name the ropes. We follow instructions.

And that brings us to the peak of this chapter. Everything so far has been to show you that your choice was taken. The way back is not to pull harder.

The way back is to follow the method that returns choice to you.

This is not an abstract idea. It is a practical step you take now.

The trap told you that you were freely choosing and that you were failing. The fact is you were conned and the trap was failing you. You were never defective.

You are not choosing to stay trapped. You were trapped into thinking you chose it.

See that now, and something shifts.

It was never you. It was the Sugar Trap. And the Tug-of-War you feel was never you fighting yourself — it was the trap pulling both ropes.

You can put the ropes down.
