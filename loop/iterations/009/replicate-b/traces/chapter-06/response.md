### YOU SAY YOU LOVE THE TASTE — LET'S TEST IT

We left the last chapter in the cinema.

You saw the warmth was the people. The laughter was the film. The celebration was the occasion. BAD SUGAR was sneaking a ride.

And still, the FOR column whispers one last defence.

Not energy. Not reward. Taste.

You say, "I love the taste." You say it plainly. Confidently. As a connoisseur.

Do you?

I mean you, directly. If you are P-02, the in-denial moderate who says "just a treat" and "I don't keep it in the house" — this is your fortress. You don't deny the grazing. You defend it as liking. "I just enjoy it."

If you are P-01, the loss-of-control binger who says "I just crave sweets and when i eat them, i feel like i cant stop" — you also say it. One bite becomes the box, and you tell yourself you finished the box because it tasted so good.

Let me ask you, with warm complicity, as someone who said the same for years.

If you genuinely love the taste, why does your own behaviour prove you don't?

The fact is, we are not going to argue about flavour in the abstract. We are going to use your own kitchen as evidence. No theory. Your actions.

Start here. Do you sweeten coffee?

Many of you do. You say you love coffee. Yet you add BAD SUGAR to it. Why would you paint over a taste you love? Do you add sugar to a peach you love? No. You add sugar to cover a bitter taste you do not love. The sugar is not the pleasure. It is the mask.

Do you eat white flour alone?

Take the base of almost everything we call BAD SUGAR. White bread. Plain pasta. White rice. A spoon of refined sugar from the bag. A slab of plain white flour and water. Would you sit and eat that with delight? Would you crave a bowl of dry white flour for its gorgeous flavour?

Of course not. You would push it away.

So how can you claim you love the taste of BAD SUGAR, when BAD SUGAR itself — unsweetened, unaccompanied, unbuttered, unchocolated — is tasteless to you?

You don't love the taste of BAD SUGAR. You love the company it arrived with. And we can prove it in one scene.

You may feel a flicker of disbelief. Good. Keep an open mind. I know this is hard to accept. You have believed your whole life that sugar tastes wonderful on its own. Question what you think you know about that taste, just for this chapter.

If BAD SUGAR were truly delicious, would it need disguise?

If the taste were the pleasure, would you need to hide it inside something else to want it?

No. And no.

That is the crack in the connoisseur story. We are about to widen it until the whole benefit falls through.

### THE LONELY CARB — WHY BAD SUGAR CANNOT STAND ALONE

We were all conned young. Not foolish. Conned.

BAD SUGAR learned early that it cannot get into your mouth on its own merit. It is a lonely carb. Bland. Flat. Dull. Try to eat it naked and you taste nothing worth returning for.

So what does a lonely ingredient do? It invites company over. Fat. Butter. Chocolate. Cream. Salt. Crunch. Vanilla. Roast. The house-party gatecrasher does not arrive alone. It arrives with friends who do all the talking, then takes credit for the party.

Think of every BAD SUGAR product you say you love.

You say you love the taste of biscuits. Would you love dry flour and sugar without butter?

You say you love pastries. Would you love white flour paste without butter and chocolate?

You say you love chocolate sweets. Would you love gritty sugar without cocoa butter?

You say you love sugary drinks. Would you love sugar-water without fizz, flavour, and cold?

You say you love white bread with jam. Is it the bread you love, or the butter and the jam that made the bread edible?

The fact is, lonely carbs invite company over because without the company they would be tasteless. They do what every gatecrasher does — they stand next to the popular guests and hope you think the charm is theirs.

Ask your own memory.

When you tasted a plain rice cake, did you think "delicious"?

When you tasted unsweetened white flour, did you think "more"?

When you licked a teaspoon of plain granulated sugar, did it feel like a treat, or like grit?

You know. Grit. Not treat.

There is no **a genuine pleasure or treat** in that lonely taste. There is a taste you learned to call love because it always arrived holding someone else's hand.

We will now throw the company out and see what is left.

### THE GATECRASHER — THE HOUSE PARTY THAT STOLE THE CREDIT

Here is the demonstration. This is the house-party gatecrasher.

Imagine a house party. The hosts are Butter, Chocolate, Salt, Cream, Crunch, Roast. They are warm. Familiar. Their taste is real. They fill the house with aroma and richness.

BAD SUGAR turns up uninvited. No charm. No gift. It walks in with the hosts, stands in the middle of the room, laughs loudly, and lets you assume the good feeling in the house is because of it.

You have guessed. The pleasure you call "sugar taste" was always the hosts.

Let's isolate the variable.

Keep BAD SUGAR. Remove the company.

Taste plain white sugar. Taste dry white pasta without salt or oil. Taste white bread straight from the bag, no butter, no filling. Taste a sweet you claim to love after you have scraped off the chocolate and the cream and the salt.

Is it a genuine pleasure or treat? No. It is sweet and then cloying. It needs water. It needs the rescue of flavour.

Now keep the company. Remove BAD SUGAR.

Taste butter on warm, whole food. Taste salt on roasted nuts. Taste cocoa without the sugar load. Taste cream without sugar. Taste the crunch of a biscuit made without refined sugar — you still get crunch, butter, aroma.

Do you still get pleasure? Yes. Immediate. Rich. Real.

Which test proves the source?

Your own mouth does.

You were not tasting BAD SUGAR and adding butter to improve it. You were tasting butter and chocolate and salt and crunch, and BAD SUGAR was **sneaking a ride**.

We all made the same mis-assignment. We credited the gatecrasher because he arrived with our friends. We remembered the party as his party.

Do you see how this explains the family size?

The trap does not need you to love sugar. It needs you to believe you do. Once you believe, you interpret every hit of company — croissant, ice cream, sweet drink, biscuit — as proof that BAD SUGAR gives you pleasure.

Ask three trap questions whose only honest answer concedes the point.

If you love BAD SUGAR itself, why do you only love it when it brings chocolate, butter, salt and crunch?

If BAD SUGAR tasted wonderful alone, why do you reject it alone?

If the pleasure came from sugar, why does removing the company remove the pleasure, and removing sugar while keeping the company keep it?

You know. The company was the pleasure all along. BAD SUGAR was only ever sneaking a ride.

Let me land the pair.

It was not you loving sugar. It was sugar using what you love.

And the shorter verdict:

The taste was never his. It was theirs.

Does that amuse you? It should. The connoisseur thought he had refined taste. He had a gatecrasher in his mouth taking the bow.

Hold that amusement. It will help you in the next test.

### THE NON-USER TEST — WHAT HAPPENS WHEN YOU REMOVE THE COMPANY

We have not finished. A belief that felt like fact needs more than one proof.

Look at the non-user. The clean baseline you once were and will be again.

Take a person who never learned to want BAD SUGAR. A small child before the brainwashing, or an adult who genuinely lives free of it now. Put a plate before them: a plain sugary drink, sticky sweets gone soft in the packet, lukewarm sweet pastry on a hot day.

Does the non-user inhale and say "heaven"?

No. They often screw up their face. Too sweet. Too sickly. Too much.

Put the same plate before a trapped user at the same moment. The user salivates. The wrapper rustles. The cue fires.

Is the chemical on the plate different? No. The belief about it is.

The fact is, the smell is not alluring to the non-user. The wrapper is not exciting to the free person. The "irresistible taste" you feel before the first bite is not taste at all. It is anticipation manufactured by memory plus cue.

We did not love sugar from birth. We learned to call that anticipation "love."

Ask yourself, with honesty.

Have you ever opened a packet of sweets you had craved all day, eaten one, and felt "that's it?" Flatness. Slight disappointment. The anticipation was better than the taste.

Have you ever craved a specific biscuit, bought it, tasted it, and needed a second immediately to chase what the first did not quite deliver?

Have you ever eaten past flavour? Mouth full, jaw tired, still reaching, no longer tasting?

You have. We all have.

That is not the behaviour of someone enjoying a genuine pleasure. That is the behaviour of someone chasing a moment that never arrives. The hit never lives up to the promise. So the brain orders another hit to close the gap.

If the taste were truly wonderful, would one be enough? Would you taste it, satisfy, and stop?

The person enjoying a real pleasure does stop. A person eating real fruit — a genuine sweet — tastes, enjoys, and is done. No binge. No hiding. The flavour satisfies. The person in the Sugar Trap does the opposite. One bite becomes the box. "I just crave sweets and when i eat them, i feel like i cant stop" — that is not a connoisseur. That is a person feeding a cue, not savouring a flavour.

Look again at your moderates, P-02. "Just one won't hurt." "Once a week is fine." "I don't keep it in the house."

If you truly loved the taste, why would you need rules to live with it? You need no rule to manage how often you taste an apple you like. You eat it when you want it and you stop. The rule is proof of trap, not proof of love.

The rule is you bargaining with the gatecrasher.

Let me ask you directly.

Do you love the taste? Or do you love the idea that the taste gives you something — relief, permission, a moment that is yours?

Because taste alone does not make you hide wrappers.

Taste alone does not make you eat until you feel sick.

Taste alone does not make you eat when you are not even hungry.

That is not taste. That is the trap using taste as its cover story.

There is no **a genuine pleasure or treat** in BAD SUGAR as taste. The taste story is the Sweet Brainwashing interpreting the gatecrasher's company as the gatecrasher's gift.

Be warm to yourself here. We were all fooled the same way. The dish was sweet. The company was wonderful. The credit went to the wrong guest. Seeing it is not loss. It is comedy.

You didn't love sugar. You loved what sugar stood next to.

### THE COMBO TRICK — WHY ONE DOSE NOW NEEDS THE FAMILY SIZE

There is one more piece to lay plainly, and I will keep its limits honest.

You have noticed it needs more now. A single biscuit once satisfied. Now you need three. A small chocolate once felt like a treat. Now you buy the family size and tell yourself it is for sharing.

Is your taste more refined? Or is something else crowding it?

The research gives us a plausible picture, not a final verdict. Refined fats and carbs together seem to have a stronger effect on the brain's wanting system than either alone. The phrase matters — seems to. That "seems to" is honest. Imaging hints the combo may hit harder together, and hints that after repeated hits the brain can become both blunted to the same dose and hyper-reactive to the cue — the wrapper, the smell, the aisle, the advert.

What may you honestly take from that?

You may take that the feeling of needing the family size now is mechanistically plausible. You may take that the cue firing before you taste — the smell from the bakery, the rustle of the packet — has a real analogue: your system firing at the predictor, not at the pleasure. You may take that escalation is not your greed and not proof you love taste more than before.

What may you not take?

Not that imaging proves causation. Not that a combo is proven supra-additive as settled fact. Not that you have brain disease. Never that.

Why does that matter here?

Because it kills the last romantic defence of taste. You thought "I love taste so much I want more." The other way around is truer: you want more because the schedule of hits has made more seem needed, while the taste itself has not improved at all.

Do you taste the third biscuit more than the first?

No. You taste it less. The first has novelty. The third is chase.

If taste were the driver, would more taste like more pleasure?

No. More tastes like less. That is how you know taste is not driving.

Think of your own escalation.

Did you decide, as a gourmet, to need bigger portions for finer flavour?

Or did the bigger portion become needed to get the same brief lift that a small one used to give, while the flavour stayed flat or got worse?

You know.

The gatecrasher brought louder friends to keep your attention. Not better taste. Louder want.

And that want feels like love until you test it without company and watch it vanish.

So hold the leaving belief steady.

You love company — fat, chocolate, butter, salt, crunch, context, warmth, occasion. You loved those. BAD SUGAR travelled with them and stole the thanks. Stripped of company, it has no taste worth returning for. The pleasure you called sugar was always other things **sneaking a ride** in reverse — the good tastes being used as a vehicle for the lonely carb.

There is no **a genuine pleasure or treat** to sacrifice here. There is a mislabel to correct, and once corrected, the want collapses because there is nothing left to want.

### IF YOU LOVED IT, WHY DO YOU HIDE IT?

I want to close the gate on taste with self-incriminating evidence. The kind that cannot be argued with because it is your own behaviour.

If you love the taste — truly, calmly, connoisseur-like — answer these.

Do you love it enough to taste it slowly, with attention, and stop when you have had enough?

Or do you eat it quickly, barely tasting after the first two bites, reaching for the next before the last is swallowed?

Do you love it enough to eat it openly, at the table, with people, savouring?

Or do you eat it alone, quickly, guiltily, with the wrapper hidden afterwards?

Do you love it enough to remember the flavour an hour later?

Or do you remember only the vague relief and the wish for another tomorrow?

The fact is, the way you use BAD SUGAR does not look like love of taste. It looks like feeding a restlessness you misread as taste.

A person in love with taste tastes.

A person in the trap feeds.

We have all been there. I have stood in the kitchen eating biscuits I no longer tasted, telling myself I was treating myself because I loved them. I did not taste them. I was quieting the faint, restless need for a fix and calling that quiet love.

Ask the killer question.

If BAD SUGAR tastes so good, why don't you eat it like a connoisseur — slowly, openly, satisfied — instead of like someone trying to get enough of something you never quite taste?

Why?

You know.

Because it was never about taste. The taste story was the Sweet Brainwashing giving the Gremlin a cover story the modern world respects. "I love taste" is respectable. "I feel a little empty and restless and the cue is firing" is not. So the brain dresses the need as preference.

Strip the dress. There is no **a genuine pleasure or treat** underneath.

There is a gatecrasher with no taste of his own, living off the reputation of the hosts.

And do you feel the amusement now? The connoisseur becomes the audience to his own con. It is not shameful. It is wonderfully light to see. You thought you had a palate for sugar. You had a palate for butter, cocoa, salt, crunch, and the situation that made any sweet feel like love for a second.

Remove BAD SUGAR. Keep the feast.

You lose nothing.

You recover the hosts.
