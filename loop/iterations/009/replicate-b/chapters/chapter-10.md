### IN HER OWN WORDS — SARAH

Read this slowly. This is not theory. This is a woman who was where you are — clever, capable, and quietly convinced she was just a bit greedy — who stepped out of the Sugar Trap and stayed free.

*I started sugar the way everyone does. Birthday cake at five, cola at seven because the other kids had it, biscuits after school because Mum said I'd been good this week. By twenty-five I was not a big eater in my head. I told myself I don't keep it in the house — which was true, I didn't, I ate it in the car on the way home so the house looked clean. In my office drawer there was a family-size bar broken into "sensible" pieces and a pack of "healthy" oat biscuits that were basically sugar and palm oil. I ate between two and three hundred grams of chocolate most weeks, plus biscuits, plus a sugary drink at lunch. I knew the number but I never said it out loud.*

*I was tired at three p.m. every single day and I told myself I needed sugar for energy. I would have something quick and sweet but now know that will only give a short term lift which will inevitably be followed by a fast drop. That was my line after, but at the time I thought the lift WAS energy. I would get the spike, feel sharp for twenty minutes, then crash harder, then crave again by four-thirty. If anyone said anything I said I just liked the taste. Celebrating, rewarding myself, going to the cinema, etc with chocolate — that was me exactly. Hard day? Chocolate. Good day? Chocolate. Cinema without chocolate felt pointless.*

*What changed wasn't willpower. Willpower had failed me for fifteen years. Every diet was the same. Be good Monday to Thursday, be "bad" Friday to Sunday, feel ashamed Monday again. With the willpower method I could white-knuckle for three weeks, then one birthday would wipe it. I thought I was weak-willed. I wasn't. I was willful in the wrong direction — I used a strong will to keep persisting at something that went against all my instincts.*

*The first two weeks after I stopped BAD SUGAR were wobbly in a way that was almost funny. Day three I was restless and empty and I kept thinking about the office drawer. Day four my doctor — who I'd told I was stopping — said, "A little sugar won't hurt, just have a small treat, you're being extreme." I remember standing in the kitchen, holding a biscuit someone had offered, and hearing his voice and my old voice at once. I put it down not because I was being strong but because I finally saw it: that biscuit was not kindness, it was the pot talking. I was not depriving myself. I was refusing to get back in the water.*

*After that the stability came. By week three the three p.m. crash had gone. Not better — gone. I had not realized how much energy I spent negotiating with myself every day until the negotiation stopped. Food got simpler. I ate fruit and it tasted ridiculously sweet, like my tongue had woken up. Plain yoghurt with berries — sweet enough. I didn't change my life. I went to the same cinema with the same friends. The film was wonderful. I had not missed chocolate at all; I had actually watched the film. I lost no weight that month and didn't care — I had my afternoons back. That was freedom. Six months now. No counting. No drama. When BAD SUGAR crosses my mind I don't feel loss. I think how marvellous it is to be free.*

I ask you to read Sarah's arc not as a promise of what you will feel on day three, but as what is common: honest wobble, then steadiness, and the authority conflict where you prevail not by discipline but by clarity. She was not exceptional. She was conned, like we were. Then she saw.

Do you see yourself in her drawer? In her car? In her cinema seat?

We all have that drawer.

And we can all leave it empty and feel nothing but relief.
