Chapter 10
NO SAFE SWEET

*There is no careful way to keep a little of the poison and call it control.*

THE FRIDAY BARGAIN

You know this bargain because we all signed it.

No BAD SUGAR Monday to Thursday. Clean days. Good days. Then Friday night, the special sweet. The allowed bar. The dessert that does not count because it was earned. You write it on the calendar as if ink could hold it.

Watch that week as it truly runs.

Monday starts loud with resolve. You tell the kitchen you have managed to be good this week already, and the phrase steadies you till noon. By three the head thins. By eight the hand circles. You answer with talk about Friday. You picture the bar in its paper. You rehearse the first bite. Tuesday sharpens the picture. The bar grows brighter in the mind than any bar ever tasted in the mouth. You run the shop shelf in your head while answering emails. You hear paper in the traffic. Wednesday you walk past the shop and your eyes go to the shelf without asking your feet to stop. You do not buy. You note that not buying proves the rule works. You stand a second too long before the bright row, reading names you swore you had dropped, and walk on with a small hum in the chest that you call pride. Thursday you are tired and short and full of small static, and you soothe it by adding a little to Friday in your head. A larger bar. A drink beside it. A pudding after. You tell yourself the adding proves you are honest about desire. Friday afternoon the hours slow. Work thins. The clock is watched. The tongue rehearses flood. Saturday morning you tell yourself you will borrow nothing from Friday, yet the date already slides.

Then Friday comes.

You eat supper fast, half tasting it, because the mind is already past it. Hunger is not met, it is hurried through to reach paper. The paper opens with that bright crackle. The first square floods loud. The second less loud. The third hurries. Chewing quickens. Talk thins. The room that was full narrows to palm and mouth. You promised one bar. The mouth asks for a drink to wash film. The drink lays fresh film. The film asks for more. Before the programme ends you are counting change for the corner shop, or opening the backup drawer, or finishing edges that were meant for others. The eyes weigh what is left before the mouth has cleared. The hand returns before decision.

Saturday morning the bin holds proof and the mind holds arithmetic. You tell yourself Friday was too large, so next Friday will be smaller. Or Friday was too small, so next Friday will be kinder and therefore hold. Or you will move the day to Saturday to suit a party. By Sunday night Thursday has already joined Friday in your head, just this once because the week was hard. A square on Thursday surely cannot ruin Friday if Friday is allowed. By Tuesday Saturday has joined as well, because a crumb on Saturday surely cannot matter if Friday is allowed. By the next Friday the allowance has grown a border of small permissions that no calendar shows.

If the rule worked, why does the rule always grow?

Look closer at what the rule does to Monday to Thursday. Those were meant to be clean days. They become waiting days. The eye visits shelves. The mind rehearses paper. Conversation bends toward sweetness. You weigh whether a taste on Tuesday ruins Friday or sharpens it. You bargain whether a small bite today can be subtracted from Friday. You tell colleagues about the plan with a laugh that asks for applause. All that traffic never lived in plain weeks when supper was eaten hungry and stopped at satisfied. The rule claimed to shrink thought about sweetness to one night. It spreads thought about sweetness across five days and then floods the sixth. Imagine Friday gone and the week handed back free for work and people and weather, with no date to polish and no border to patrol.

Did the Friday dose taste better for being waited for, or did waiting only lend it gold that the tongue never found? Did one allowed night quiet the week, or did it fill Monday to Thursday with pictures and bargaining that undosed weeks never held? If Friday truly satisfied, why did Saturday start to itch before the wrapper had settled?

There is only one honest reading. The wait made it precious. The precious made it louder. The louder made it spread.

That is how the Sugar Trap holds after the best case has been answered. Not by force. By permission. One kept sweet keeps the whole belief lit, and the lit belief teaches the mouth to call for a sweet hit to close the day.

We told ourselves moderation would tame the want. It did the other way around. The rule did not tame the want. The rule fed the want.

THE CLIFF EDGE

Let me put the Friday logic where you can see its shape, because the mind nods at moderation till the body shows the drop.

You stand with me at a cliff path on a clear morning. Grass short. Gulls loud. Sea white far below. A friend walks beside you, kind, sensible, careful with money and punctual with lifts. He points to the edge.

"Jump," he says. "Only fall a few metres."

You laugh. He does not laugh. He explains. He has measured. He has a rule. Fridays only. Just off this ledge, just a short fall, just a small one, just for the weekend, just because you were good. He has a calendar. He has ink. He will count metres the way you count squares. He will land on a kind ledge he has picked from above, dry and wide enough for one person on one night.

Would you jump?

You would not argue about wind or shoes or courage. You would look at the man you trust and understand in one cold second that the sentence breaks itself. Jumping is falling. Falling is not measured by intention. The cliff does not read calendars. The ledge he points to narrows when seen from below, wet with spray, crumbling at the lip, with gulls nesting where his foot would land.

That is the Friday bar in its true clothes.

"I will take BAD SUGAR in a small fall, on one night, in one paper, and not fall further." The mouth says metres. The body knows falling. A dose is a fall. The height is not set by the day written in ink. It is set by what the dose does once it lands.

Give the tongue one permitted flood after four days of pictures and the hand does what hands taught by years of floods do. It reaches before decision. It hurries. It films. It thirsts under sweet. It points to the shop, the drawer, the backup cupboard. You call that weakness. I tell you flat it is shape. The gap taught the flood to shout. The flood taught the gap to ache. Gap plus flood drives binge the way a cliff drives falling. Not by character. By shape.

You say, but surely a small fall is safer than daily falling. I answer with warmth for you and steel for the lie. A small scheduled fall keeps the eye on the edge all week. It fills Monday with rehearsal. It fills Thursday with static. It turns Friday into theatre and Saturday into tidying. Daily grazing dulls without pictures. Scheduled allowance sharpens with pictures. Both keep the wire humming. Neither loosens it. You do not measure the fall, you step back from the edge and escape the whole measuring game.

Put the pincer straight. If there were any true benefit in the dose, you would need it often, not rarely. If there is no benefit in it often, there is no point in it rarely. You cannot have it both ways, precious enough to wait for all week and empty enough to leave alone. Either it gives, and the wait is torment, or it gives nothing, and the wait is theatre for nothing.

Look at your own weeks for proof, not my words. Which weeks held more thought about sweetness, weeks with a firm Friday allowance or weeks with plain suppers that met hunger and left thought clear for work and people? Which nights circled longer, dosed Fridays with film and second reach, or plain Tuesdays with soup and bread and water? Your record convicts without a lecture.

The cliff does not bargain. It does not do small. The kind friend with the calendar is not kind in that sentence. That voice is the Sweet Con, dressed as common sense, asking you to jump carefully.

ONE, THE EMPTY CUPBOARD, AND TOMORROW

Now lay the three smaller bargains beside Friday and watch them run the same film.

First: just one.

"I will have just one square. One biscuit. One spoon. One cannot matter."

We all spoke it standing at the counter at ten at night. We meant it. The hand took one. The tongue flooded loud for seconds. Tasting thinned by the third bite. Hurry arrived. The narrow call that sneers at soup rose louder than before, that empty, twitchy, slightly shaky, need-something-sweet-now feeling taught by years of floods. The hand returned before the mouth cleared. Then wrapper rustle, box empty before the swallow ends.

Stay with those ten minutes, because they convict the whole phrase. Before the paper the evening was quiet, programme murmuring, blanket warm, talk low. After one the quiet thins. Chewing hurries. The palm weighs. The eye counts. The mind bargains whether a second piece ruins one or completes one. The programme plays on unwatched while attention runs stock. That haste is not hunger. True hunger grows slowly, open and patient, welcomes bread and soup and fruit, waits kindly for the table.

Ask your memory straight. How many boxes closed at one after they were opened for one? How many evenings latched kindly after just one? You know, because you lived both kinds of evenings. Plain evenings closed kindly and left the mind free for talk and rest. Dosed evenings reordered.

One carries the binge because the craving, once triggered, carries the binge. That carrying is the trap signature, not your nature. It does not mean you are helpless for life. It means a lit wire hums. Watch how fast the second reach follows the first, how talk thins while chewing, how the eye weighs what is left before the mouth has cleared. Take the belief out and the wire cools. Keep one exception and the wire stays warm.

It does plenty TO you. It does nothing FOR you. Hold just one against that verdict. What did it do TO you? Film, hurry, thinning talk, thirst under sweet, second reach, morning bins. What did it do FOR you? Name one thing the room, the people, the hunger, the night did not already own. You cannot, however long you stare.

Second: the empty house.

"I don't keep it in the house, so I am safe."

We emptied cupboards with pride and told ourselves the front door was a wall. Then watch where the feet went.

The office drawer filled. The desk jar filled. The car door filled. The bag filled for traffic. The café stop lengthened. The till bar joined the petrol. The work birthday plate was visited twice. The child's party bag was taxed at the door. The canteen pudding that was never liked became liked at half past three. The walk home bent by the kiosk without asking. The trolley paused at the bright row a second too long.

The trap does not live in the cupboard. It lives in the belief that a sweet hit is what seals the hour. Move the paper and the reading walks with you. It finds new shelves. It learns new hours. It calls half past three an order and half past eight an order and cinema an order and tired an order.

If the house was the problem, why did the hand find paper everywhere else? If distance proved control, why did the mind spend the day mapping where paper lived?

The house rule did not cool the wire. It strung the wire across town. You proved you could walk past your own shelf while spending the whole afternoon thinking about the shop shelf, the drawer shelf, the till shelf. That is not quiet. That is patrol. Plain days hold no patrol. Hunger comes, food meets it, thought moves on to work and people and weather.

Third: tomorrow.

"I'll quit tomorrow. After the birthday. After the holidays. After this box."

We spoke it kindly, as if delay were care. Look at what delay does.

Tomorrow lends today a glow it never owned. This last box must be enjoyed properly, so tasting strains, hurry grows, pictures brighten. The last week becomes a farewell tour, each dose watched too closely, each flood lent gold by ending. You eat slower to memorise and faster from hurry at once. You photograph wrappers in the mind. You tell yourself you will miss this, and the telling teaches missing before anything has left. You buy a little extra to see you through, then a little extra to finish the extra. By the time tomorrow arrives the mouth has been taught for seven more days that paper is precious, and tomorrow needs another tomorrow to recover from the tour.

If tomorrow were safer, why does every waited-for quit arrive heavier than the day it was promised? If the box must be finished first, why does finishing always order another box?

Delay is not preparation. Delay is permission wearing a kind face. The body does not need a date. The wire does not cool by rehearsal. It cools when the belief is pulled, and the belief is pulled by seeing, not by finishing.

Add the quiet cousin of tomorrow. Weaning.

"I will step down slowly. Smaller bars. Fewer days. Lighter hits."

Tapering a wire that lives on pictures only brightens pictures. Small hits between gaps shout louder than plain days ever did. Each reduced dose is watched, weighed, praised for being small, mourned for being small, followed by counting to the next. The mind lives on the edge all week, measuring metres off a cliff. You weigh squares, you split bars with a knife, you log bites, you compare weeks. You wake thinking of the small square at noon and sleep thinking whether the small square at noon held. All that measuring keeps the eye on the edge. You do not need to rehearse falling to learn ground. You need to see the cliff and step back.

THE SWAP THAT KEEPS YOU HOOKED

There remains the cleverest bargain, the one that sounds like science and tastes like sense.

"I will swap. Diet drinks. Sweeteners in tea. Honey rather than sugar. Dark rather than milk. Juice rather than cola. A healthy bar. A protein bite. I will keep the sweetness and lose the harm."

I meet this head on because it keeps more readers on the edge than Friday and tomorrow together.

Take the packet in your hand and read it with investigator eyes. Bright letters. Light claims. Zero this, skinny that. Pour it. Sip it. Note what the tongue reports and what the mind does next.

The tongue floods sweet without calories and the mind learns the same lesson it always learned. The narrow call is answered with brightness. The answer quiets for minutes and returns louder. The hand circles at the old hours. Half past three orders brightness. Evening orders brightness. The packet changes. The reading does not. The Nibbler is not fed by calories. That grumble is the Nibbler dying when unfed, and it is kept alive by belief, not by numbers on a packet. Feed the belief with bright taste and the grumble never learns silence. Starve the belief and the grumble thins on its own.

Follow the swap through a plain day. Morning tea with white tablets, sweet on the tongue, nothing in the belly. The mouth expects flood, the belly receives no closing, the mind notes the gap and points to mid-morning. Mid-morning bar with bright claims, chewed at the desk, loud for one bite, dulling by the third, talk thinning on the call, wrapper folded small to hide count. Afternoon bottle with zero on the label, cold flood, film under sweet, thirst rising under coating, second reach before the bottle empties. Evening spoon of honey for kindness, coating, hurry, bins to hide. Different papers. Same hurry. Same film. Same circling. Same bargaining about tomorrow.

Watch the honey spoon closely, because kind words hide it. The jar stands wooden and honest. The spoon turns slow. The tongue coats just the same. Hurry follows just the same. The mind calls it nature and lends it gold the tongue never found. Honey in tea at midnight does what paper does at midnight, answers the narrow call with brightness and reorders the same call an hour later. Dark for milk does the same. Juice for cola does the same. The bright claims change. The throat learns the same lesson, that hours are sealed by floods.

You say, but surely a lighter hit is less harm. I answer flatly. Less of what does nothing FOR you is still nothing FOR you, with the wire kept warm. The question was never which paper harms less. The question was what benefit any paper gives at all. Held to daylight the benefit column stays empty however light the packet.

Run the comparison your tongue already knows. Bread breaking loud after true hunger, crust cracking, soup steaming, vegetables bright as paint, fruit bursting cool, satisfaction latching quiet. That eating loudens as hunger meets it. Beside it, the bright swap, loud for one sip, dulling by the third, coating, hurrying, thinning talk, pointing to more before swallowing ends. One side needed story and brightness to borrow gold. One side brought its own gold from field and oven and orchard. Bread breaking loud after hunger is marvellous without a bell, and the old favourite shows beside it as sticky clutter that learned to sit at dinner and take praise for dinner.

Now hear the objection aimed at the method itself, because it will come from kind mouths and must arrive pre-answered.

"This totality sounds extreme. Surely a balanced person can take a little. You are making a religion of zero."

I welcome the doubt and I return it with interest. What is extreme? A body that must have bright floods to face afternoons, bins hidden under newspaper, hands circling in the dark row while love speaks beside you, that is extreme. A plain supper that meets hunger and closes kindly, mouth fresh till noon, tables where talk runs late, that is not extreme. That is ordinary.

Balance with a con is not balance. It is tenancy. You would not call a man extreme for refusing small falls off a cliff. You would call the friend with the calendar extreme for measuring them.

Totality is not hardness. Hardness was counting, weighing, bargaining, starting Mondays, mourning Fridays, hiding rustle. Totality is the end of counting. One rule that removes a hundred rules. No edge to watch. No metre to measure. No tomorrow to polish.

THE SEATBELT ON A QUIET ROAD

Let me give you the margin plainly, because fear will use accidents to sell deliberate falls if you let it.

Drive with me on a quiet road in daylight. Hedgerows slow past. The engine hums low. You wear the belt across chest and lap. It sits light. You do not think about it. It does not drive the car. It does not choose the speed. It does not steer into walls to prove itself.

A jolt comes. A pothole. A sudden stop. The belt locks for a second and holds. You breathe, loosen shoulders, drive on. No lecture. No panic. No week of penance. No diary entry. The jolt does not mean you meant the ditch. You do not pull over to scold yourself. You do not turn round to test the pothole again to prove the belt held.

That is what the body does with an accidental crumb. A sauce thickened without asking. A bite taken in polite half-light before the mind caught up. A recipe changed without warning. A spoon that was not what it seemed. The body copes, steadies, drives on. The crumb revives nothing unless the reading is let back in. The taste may flash, the old thought may knock, and then quiet returns because you did not invite it, praise it, or polish it. The road stays quiet and your driving stays yours in freedom that needs no counting.

Now see the misuse. A driver who steers toward walls because a belt is fitted. Who tests the ditch on Fridays to prove the belt holds. Who calls deliberate swerves blips. The belt was fitted for jolts, not for erratic driving. Use it to excuse steering and the road soon teaches what the belt was never built to teach.

So with BAD SUGAR. Guard the belief, not the behaviour with panic. An accidental taste does not unwind you. A deliberate exception does, because it rehangs the bell and lends gold to foil again. The difference is never the size of the crumb. It is who steered. Did the road jolt you, or did you turn the wheel toward paper to test the belt? Did surprise touch the tongue, or did planning buy the paper, hide the paper, picture the paper all week?

If you answer to a clinician for blood sugar or food trouble, follow their advice first.

Keep no special sweet the way you keep no special ditch. Not in the house, not at work, not for Fridays, not for parties, not for travel, not for being good, not for being tired. Not one paper kept to prove you could keep it. A kept sweet is a kept wire. It hums in the drawer and teaches the eye to visit. It loudens in the mind on dull afternoons and points the feet to shops. It asks to be counted, weighed, bargained with. Empty is quiet. Kept is loud.

You stand now where seeing turns. The cinema row cleared. The mugs showed labels can lend taste. The slow pot showed small steps can hide a long climb. Friday showed waiting makes precious. One showed carrying. The empty house showed the reading walks. Tomorrow showed delay polishes. The swap showed new paper keeps old humming. The belt shows accidents do not steer.

It does plenty TO you. It does nothing FOR you. Let that verdict sit beside the road. One side films and hurries and reorders. One side meets hunger and closes kindly. Both cannot be dinner.

You are one step from the last ordinary treat taken with attention, and you walk it with clear hands on an ordinary morning, clear of paper and bargaining.

11. NEVER KEEP A SPECIAL SWEET
One exception keeps the whole Trap alive.

**SUMMARY**
- A Friday allowance fills the week with pictures and lets Thursday and Saturday join, so the rule feeds the want it claims to tame.
- Jumping carefully off a cliff is falling, and a small scheduled dose is a fall the calendar cannot measure.
- One permitted bite carries the binge, an empty cupboard moves the search across town, and tomorrow polishes the glow it promises to end.
- A lighter packet or sweeter swap keeps the same old reading, so the wire stays warm while paper changes.
- An accidental crumb is a jolt the body steadies past, while a kept special sweet steers toward the ditch and rehangs the bell.
