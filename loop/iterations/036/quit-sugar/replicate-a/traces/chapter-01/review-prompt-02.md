Chapter 1
THE INVITATION

*You are not about to be weighed, scolded, or deprived; you are about to see clearly what has really been happening with sweetness.*

IS THIS ANOTHER DIET LECTURE?

I know what you are thinking as you open these pages.

"Oh great. Another diet lecture."

I would have thought the same. I have sat where you sit now, book in hand, half hoping it might help, half scoffing at the idea that a book could change anything about biscuits and cola and cake. We have all read the warnings. We have all been told to cut down, to be good, to count, to measure, to be careful. We have all made the Monday promise and watched it fade by Wednesday. If this were another version of that, you would be right to close it now.

It is not.

This is not a diet book. I am not going to weigh you, count your calories, hand you a meal plan, or tell you that you are weak. I am not going to ask you to count grams, to earn points, to be good this week, or to punish yourself for last night. I have no interest in scolding you. I like you. I was you.

What I am going to do is much simpler and much stranger. I am going to ask you to look, with clear eyes, at something you think you already understand. Sweetness. The quick bite at the desk. The sugary somethings to get me through the afternoon. The evening box that starts with one and ends empty. You believe you know what those things do for you. I am asking you to question what you think you know about sweetness, not because I want to lecture you, but because you have been given phoney information about it from the day you were born.

If you live with diabetes, take medication that affects blood sugar, are pregnant, or have a history of distress around food, talk to your clinician first and follow their advice while you read.

You are wary. Good. Bring that wariness with you. Bring your full cupboard too. You do not need to throw anything away tonight. You do not need to clear the shelves or warn the household or brace yourself for a hard week. There is no hard week coming. What is coming is good news, and it starts with a definition so plain you can put it in a box and keep it.

But first let me tell you who I am and why I can speak to you with such confidence.

I KNOW HOW THIS FEELS

I did not come to this as a nutritionist with a chart. I came to it with sticky fingers.

For years we lived the same loop in my house that you live in yours. A proper breakfast, a proper lunch, and then that slow slide after three. The head goes a little dull. The work piles. Someone puts biscuits on a plate in the meeting. I would have something quick and sweet and tell myself it would help me get through. Ten minutes of buzz. Then the fog again. Then another reach. By ten at night the day's good intentions were gone and my hand was in the cupboard before I had decided to open it.

I tried what you have tried. I tried being good. I tried allowing a little and found that a little never stayed little. I tried rules. No sugar before six. No biscuits in the house, only cake at weekends. Fruit on weekdays, pudding on Sundays. Every rule collapsed in the same place: the evening, tired, the wrapper rustling, the voice saying, "I feel like i cant stop once I start, so why did I start?" Then the regret, then the vow to try harder tomorrow.

I am not proud of those nights, and I am not ashamed of them either. I understand them now. At the time I thought they proved something about me. I thought I lacked character. I thought other people could take one and leave it and I could not, so the fault must be mine.

The fact is, the fault was never mine. It was never yours either.

I did not quit BAD SUGAR through character. I quit it when I saw it clearly, and when I saw it clearly I quit it easily, immediately and permanently. That sentence will sound outrageous to you now. It sounded outrageous to me before it happened. I had failed too many times to believe in easy. Yet easy is exactly what happened, not because I found a stronger lock for the cupboard, but because I no longer wanted the cupboard to be locked. The want went. When the want goes, there is nothing to carry.

You will think: "Too good to be true? That was my reaction too." Hold that thought. Keep it beside you while we talk. I am not asking you to believe me yet. I am asking you to read on with an open mind and let your own experience judge.

Ask yourself this. If sweet snacking were a freely loved hobby, like gardening or music, would you need a book to help you stop it? If moderation were working, why does the cupboard tell a different story from the rule book in your head? If you were truly choosing this, why would you feel that tug of unease after the box is empty? Those are not accusations. They are plain questions, and they point to a plain answer you already sense: something has taken hold of you that you did not vote for.

We were not foolish to fall for it. We were conned. There is a world of difference between those two things. A foolish person blames himself. A conned person, once he sees the fraud, simply stops handing over his money. No shame. No drama. Just clarity.

And clarity is all this book offers. You have, in reading on, you have absolutely nothing to lose and everything to gain. That is not a slogan. It is arithmetic. If I am wrong, you will have spent a few evenings reading and you will carry on as before. If I am right, you will walk out of the same loop that has cost you money and time and evening after evening of quiet self-reproach, and you will not miss it.

WHAT THIS BOOK MEANS BY SUGAR

We cannot talk straight until we mean the same thing by the word sugar, so let us fix it now in plain language and keep it fixed for the rest of the book.

> BAD SUGAR means refined sugar and added free sugars and junk processed carbs eaten as sweet hits, including sugary drinks, confectionery, biscuits, cakes, desserts, sweetened cereals, chocolate bars, ice cream, pastries, and equivalent grazing doses of starchy snack-carbs taken for the same hit.
>
> When this book says sugar, take it to mean BAD SUGAR.
>
> Natural sugars inside whole fruit, vegetables, and plain meals as cooked and eaten are not the target. They belong to real food.

Read that twice. It matters.

I am not talking about an apple. I am not talking about carrots, or potatoes in a stew, or milk in tea, or a meal you cooked and ate hungry and enjoyed. I am not here to make you afraid of food. Food is one of the great joys of a day, and no one needs a book to make eating grey.

I am talking about the manufactured dose. The packet, the bar, the bottle, the box that was built in a factory to hit fast and leave you wanting more. The biscuit that is gone in two bites and calls for a third and a fourth. The cola that burns cold and sweet and leaves the mouth sticky for another. The cereal that claims to be breakfast and behaves like dessert. Those things are not food in the old sense. They are products, and they were designed to be used the way you use them: quickly, repeatedly, without hunger, without satisfaction.

Call them doses, because that is how we use them. We do not eat a dose because we are hungry. We take it to change how we feel. Tired, so take one. Flat, so take one. Bored, stressed, deserving, sociable, so take one. "I've managed to be good this week, so I can have a treat when you feel you deserve one." "I want to get off the roller coaster, but I need sugary somethings to get me through today." That is our dialect. I know it by heart because it was mine.

Notice what is missing from that dialect. Hunger. Satisfaction. Those two plain guides that tell a child when to start and when to stop have been pushed aside. A small child will eat a peach hungrily, glow with juice, stop mid-bite, and run off to play. No counting. No guilt. No second peach to fix the first. The body leads. We once ate like that. BAD SUGAR taught us to eat by the clock, by the mood, by the wrapper, by the offer.

"But fruit is sugar too, so nothing matters, right?" I hear that one often, and it is worth answering flat. No. An orange is not a cola. The sugar in whole fruit arrives inside water and fibre, chewed slowly, inside a meal that satisfies. The dose in a bottle arrives stripped, fast, and large, built to spike and fade. To call them the same because the chemistry book uses one word is like calling a river and a burst pipe the same because both hold water. Your body knows the difference. Your evenings know the difference.

This is why diets that treat all eating as a maths problem keep failing you. They lump the peach with the packet and tell you to fight both. You cannot fight food. You should not have to. All you have to do is follow all the instructions. That sentence is the whole contract of this book, and the first part of it is to keep the line where I have drawn it: BAD SUGAR on one side, real food on the other.

Let me make the line photographable in your mind. If it was made to melt fast on the tongue, to crunch sweet from a packet, to pour sweet from a bottle, to be eaten standing, driving, typing, watching, without a table and without hunger, and to leave you reaching again within the hour, it is BAD SUGAR. If it grew, was cooked simply, is eaten at a table when hungry, chewed, tasted, and leaves you satisfied, it is food. You do not need a laboratory to tell them apart. Your own afternoons have been teaching you for years. That short term lift then fast drop you describe so well is not character. It is the signature of the dose.

WHY YOU ARE HOLDING THIS BOOK

Let us be blunt with each other, kindly.

You did not pick up this book because life with BAD SUGAR is marvellous. You picked it up because some part of you is tired of the loop. The money is not the main cost, though the money is real enough when you add the daily till receipts across a year. The teeth are not the main cost, though dentists will tell you what frequent sweet doses do to enamel. The main cost is quieter: that sense of being driven. The hand moving. The box emptying. The promise remade.

If you are like I was, you carry two pictures of yourself. One is the sensible adult who manages money, work, family, and keeps a dozen plates spinning. The other is the night figure at the cupboard, eating standing up, a little hidden even from yourself. Those two pictures do not fit together, and the gap between them aches.

I want to remove that ache, not by making you try harder, but by showing you there was never a split in you. There was a con around you.

Think of any confidence trick you have read about. The victim is not stupid. The trick works because false information is given by people he trusts, in a setting that looks normal, until handing over the cash feels like his own idea. Sweetness was sold to us that way. Loving parents pressed it as reward. Advertisements tied it to laughter and love. Shops placed it at every till. Friends offered it to be kind. We learned before we could read that sweet means treat, that sweet means energy, that sweet means deserved. Who would question that? We all absorbed it. We were brainwashed, and I use that word carefully, to mean taught a false belief until it felt like our own thought.

That is why scolding never worked. You cannot scold away false information. You can only replace it with true information and watch the feeling change on its own.

So here is the plain truth about where you stand. The fact that you are holding this book proves the decision is already made. A person who is happily and freely enjoying something does not buy a book about quitting it. You would not buy a book called How to Quit Listening to Music You Love. You bought this because you want to be clear of it. You are not here to be persuaded to want freedom. You already want it. You are here to be shown how to see it so the want for the doses dies.

Does that land? Let me put it as questions, because your own answers carry more weight than my assertions.

If BAD SUGAR were truly your friend, why would you hide the evidence? Why would the wrappers get buried, the box turned to hide how far down it is, the shop trip timed when no one is watching? Friends do not need hiding.

If BAD SUGAR were truly a choice, why would the hand move before the mind has finished debating? What choice moves before thought?

If BAD SUGAR were truly a small pleasure you could take or leave, why would leaving it feel frightening to imagine? Small pleasures do not frighten us when we picture a week without them.

There is only one honest set of answers to those three questions, and it is this: you are not choosing. You are caught. And to be caught by a fraud is not a verdict on you. It is a verdict on the fraud.

You will look into that full cupboard tonight and feel free around it. Keep that picture. Not defiant. Not deprived. Just clear. That is where we are walking, and we are walking there by understanding, not by effort.

This is good news, and it is only good news. There is no punishment coming. There is no weighing, no counting, no confession. There is only a series of plain seeings that will add up to a different feeling whenever sweetness crosses your mind.
