Chapter 3
WHAT DOES IT DO FOR YOU

**IN THIS CHAPTER** — Dawn kitchen and cold tea, a pocketful of wrappers, the dentist's lamp, a hand from a ditch, the long years ahead

*Forget how much it harms you and ask what it ever did for you — name the gift and the trap falls.*

THE KITCHEN YOU LEFT LAST NIGHT

We stood together last night in that bright kitchen at ten o'clock, and we saw something plain and kind.

The hand moved before thought had finished. The packet lightened while the mind was still debating. The tidy of wrappers followed, quick and quiet. That order told the truth. Choice weighs and then acts. The Sugar Trap acts and then hands you reasons afterwards to cover the movement.

I stay beside you in that kitchen because what we saw there changes everything we do now. You are not weak. You were never weak. You were a capable adult answering a planted call in a rigged room, and the shame you carried out of that room belonged to the con, never to you.

Keep that seeing with you this morning. Do not throw packets away. Do not make new laws for yourself. Live as normal while your eyes learn. I ask only that you bring the same investigator's eyes from last night into daylight, because daylight is where the next lie hides.

In the dark the lie says, you cannot stop. In daylight the lie says, why would you want to. It lets you admit the night looks trapped, so long as you still believe the day holds something good. It concedes harm, to protect benefit.

You hear it in your own kind voice: I know it rots teeth and piles on weight and leaves me flat, but I enjoy it. But it gets me through. But life would be grey without the odd treat.

That but is the whole trap talking. We are going to look at it now, gently and fully, until there is nowhere left for it to hide.

THE WRONG PAIR OF SCALES

We have weighed this thing on the wrong scales all our lives.

We put harm on one side and pleasure on the other, and we ask which weighs more. A little harm, we say, for a lot of comfort. A little risk around the waist and teeth, for energy and reward and kindness in a hard day. We tell ourselves we are being honest and adult to admit both sides. We tell ourselves the scales just tip toward keeping it.

The fact is those scales are rigged.

Harm against benefit sounds fair only while benefit is real. If benefit is empty, the weighing is fraud. You do not ask whether a forged note does more harm than good. You ask what good is there at all.

That is the turn I invite you to make with me now. Forget for a little while how much it harms. We know it harms. Put harm to one side entirely. I will not frighten you with lists. I want one thing from you, and one thing only, spoken plainly between friends.

Tell me what it does for you.

Not what it does to your teeth and skin and wallet and afternoons. What it does for you. Name one true gift that belongs to BAD SUGAR itself, that would be missing from your life without the dose, that no break and no laugh and no meal could give.

Hold that question while we walk through an ordinary day together. Carry it like a lamp. If there is a gift, it will show itself. If there is none, that seeing will do more for you than a hundred warnings about harm ever could.

A DAY ON PAPER

Take yesterday. Not a bad day. Not a binge day you would confess with lowered eyes. An ordinary moderate day, the kind you call harmless.

Morning. Tea with two sugars and a frosted cereal that claims whole grain on the box. The spoon sweet, the milk sweet, the mouth coated sweet before the day has begun. Do you taste grain and milk? Or do you taste the coat over them? Note the time taken to buy it, pour it, rinse the bowl with that faint grit left behind. Note the price on the receipt at the till, small and repeated, week after week.

Mid-morning. The desk drawer opens. A biscuit with coffee, then another because the first left the mouth seeking rather than settled. Ten minutes of hurry in the fingers, papers shuffled a little faster, then the faint dip in attention that sends the eyes to the clock. You call that the lull before lunch. Look closer. The mouth wants water. The teeth feel filmed. The purse is a coin lighter for the vending run. What did those ten minutes give that the stretch of legs to the window and the cold air on the face would not have given?

Lunch eaten fast, with a sweet drink beside the plate. The meal itself could have closed hunger cleanly. The drink keeps hunger open. You finish feeling fed and oddly unclosed at once, as if the meal happened beside you rather than inside you. The afternoon starts with that coated tongue and the second search beginning already.

Three o'clock. We all know three o'clock. The light flattens. The head thickens. A chocolate bar from the shop downstairs, eaten at the desk without leaving the chair, wrapper crackling low so no one hears. A brief brightening, talked up loudly in the head, then the fog thickening again by four, and the eyes back on the drawer. You say sugary somethings to get me through. I hear you with warmth, because I said those words for years. Look at what got you through. The work got done by you, tired and trying, in spite of the spike and dip, not because of it. The break gave the rest. The chat with the colleague gave the laugh. The bar gave ten minutes of buzz and an hour of flatness and the need for another.

Evening. The shop on the way home. The packet for the children that loses a handful in the car. The dessert after supper that you planned as a small treat when you feel you deserve one, eaten standing while supper is cleared, barely seated, barely tasted past the third spoon. The sofa dose with television, hand to mouth in rhythm with the programme, not with hunger. Count the wrappers in the pocket and the bin. Count the minutes spent buying, unwrapping, hiding the evidence, brushing away crumbs, promising tomorrow will be lighter. Count the money again, not as a fortune in one day, but as a quiet tax laid on every single day.

Night. Teeth brushed with that extra scrub to lift the film. Mouth dry in the night. Sleep a little broken, morning mouth stale. You wake tired and call it age or stress or poor sleep, and reach for the sweet tea to lift the tiredness.

Now lay the day on paper in two columns, as an investigator would.

In the TO column write what BAD SUGAR did to you yesterday. To the teeth filmed and acid-washed after each dose. To the appetite kept open past satisfaction. To the mood lifted for minutes and lowered for hours. To the wallet taxed coin by coin. To the time spent in shops and queues and secret eating and clearing away. The list fills fast. You lived it. You can feel it in the mouth and the purse and the four o'clock head.

In the FOR column write what it did for you. Not what the break did for you. Not what the laugh with a friend did for you. Not what sitting down for five minutes did for you. What the dose itself added that would be missing without it.

You stare at that column. You want to write taste. You want to write lift. You want to write reward and comfort and celebrating with chocolate. Keep your pen honest. Taste of what, that left the mouth coated and seeking? Lift that left you lower within the hour and reaching again? Reward that left you a little ashamed and a little flat, counting that you had been good all week and then undone it in ten minutes? Comfort that left the trouble untouched and added a new trouble of regret?

I know this is hard to accept, and I do not ask you to accept it on my word. I ask you to look at your own paper. Where is the gift? Which hour of yesterday would you guard as precious because BAD SUGAR was in it? Which hour would turn grey tomorrow if the dose were missing but the break and the laugh and the meal remained?

Ask yourself three plain questions, and answer as you would to a friend you love.

If the dose truly treated you, why did the mouth feel worse after than before, and why did the want grow rather than close? If the dose truly fuelled you, why did the flat hour return deeper each time and call for another within the hour? If the dose truly gave, what did you carry into sleep last night that you would mourn losing?

There is only one honest set of answers. The FOR column stays empty. The day gave you moments, and the dose rode on their backs and claimed their credit.

TEETH AND THE QUIET BILL

Let us speak of the body for a moment, flatly and without scolding, because the body keeps books even when the mind looks away.

Free sugars feed the film on teeth, and that film turns each dose into acid on enamel. That is how decay begins, dose by dose, not by one wild night but by the steady wash of sweetness over teeth that never quite clear. Brushing and fluoride matter, of course they matter, and no honest person pretends they do not. They soften the bill. They do not cancel the mechanism. A mouth bathed many times a day in sugary liquid and soft starch lives in acid far longer than a mouth that meets plain food and water. I am not writing your personal future here, only the shape of the bill as it falls across millions of mouths.

Look wider, still without panic and without preaching. The guidance drawn for whole populations says keep free sugars below ten in every hundred calories, below about fifty grams a day for most adults, with further gain below five. Ordinary eating now sits far above that line, not in strange houses but in normal kitchens like yours and mine. Feeling fine does not mean intake is low. Feeling fine while the slow warming goes on is exactly how gradual harm works. The water warms so slowly around daily habit that the swimmer never marks the change.

I lay that guidance here not to frighten you into action. I do not want you to act from fear, and fear will not keep you free. I lay it here to close one excuse: the excuse that a moderate habit sits safely inside wisdom. Normal now means far above guidance. Harmless now means dosed many times a day. The moderate you describe on paper is already a heavy user by the measure of the body, and the body, not the packet, writes the final account.

Hear my care for you in this. If you live with diabetes or take medicine that changes blood sugar or appetite, or carry any condition where food change carries risk, your clinician leads and this book only changes a belief. I give no eating plan and no medical counsel. I speak of population lines to show why feeling fine misleads, never to lay a sentence on you.

And having seen the TO side in its quiet truth, come back at once to the only question that matters. Even if teeth held and guidance vanished, even if the bill never arrived, what would remain in the FOR column? Harm can be debated study by study. Emptiness cannot. A thing that does so much to you and nothing for you needs no further trial.

WHAT IF THE HAND THAT LIFTS YOU PUT YOU DOWN THERE?

Let me ask you a question that sounds strange until it sounds obvious.

What would you call a man who tripped you into a ditch each morning on your way to work, then happened by at noon and held out a hand to pull you out, and took your thanks and your coins for the rescue?

You would not call him kind. You would call him cheat. You would be warm to yourself for wanting help at noon, and harsh to him for arranging the fall at dawn. You would see in one flash that rescuer and perpetrator were the same hands.

Keep that picture while we look at the small tug you know so well.

There is a faint bodily echo after sweetness leaves the system, a restless, empty pull for something sweet again, as if the floor had dropped an inch beneath the mood. It arrives as mouth hunger without stomach hunger, as fidget in the hands, as the thought circling to the cupboard though supper was enough. It feels like need in the moment. It reads in the head as, I need something now.

And there is a story laid over that tug from childhood onward, that sweetness treats and fuels and mends. The tug fires, the story names it, the dose follows, the tug quiets for minutes because a new dose covers an old echo. Then the echo returns, a little louder for being fed. We thank the hand that lifts us at noon and never ask who put us in the ditch at dawn.

I do not ask you to fight that tug. I ask you to see its two halves together: a tiny physical echo, and a big learned belief that reads the echo as proof of a gift. Name both halves here and the mist thins. The echo is real and small and passing. The belief is installed and loud and false. The dose did not lift a natural low of life. It lifted, for minutes, the low its fellow dose had dug, and charged you wrapper and money and tooth film and flat afternoon for the service.

Is that rescue? Or is that the oldest trick in the book, played in broad daylight with bright packets?

THE GHOST CALLED BENEFIT

We come now to the ghost itself.

You entered this chapter conceding harm but holding benefit. You said, it may rot and cost and drag, but it treats me and fuels me. You said it the way we all said it, kindly, reasonably, the way brainwashed sounds when it speaks in our own voice.

Look at that benefit closely, the way you looked at the evening hand. Where does it live? Not in the hour after, where flatness and fresh want live. Not in the mouth after, where film lives. Not in the purse after, where lightness lives. Not in sleep after, where staleness lives. It lives only in the minutes of the dose, and even there it lives as quieting of a tug the last dose left.

That is the whole secret. The benefit is a belief reading a tiny body echo as a gift.

Think of wearing shoes a size too small all day for the joy of taking them off at night. The evening ease feels real. The feet sing. You could swear the taking-off gives pleasure. The fact is the other way around. The pleasure is the ending of a pain you arranged. No one would praise tight shoes as foot care. No one would pay daily coin for the privilege of limping toward evening relief.

BAD SUGAR fits that shoe exactly. We dose, we drop a little, we read the drop as need, we dose to lift the drop, we call the lift a gift. The gift never rises above the state of a person who never wore the shoe. It only returns you toward normal for minutes, then guarantees the pinch returns.

Once seen, the phrase that fooled us can be spoken plainly. What we called treat and help was never a genuine treat or fuel. Say those words slowly and feel how much falls with them. A genuine treat would leave thanks, not want. A genuine fuel would leave power, not flatness. What leaves film and fog and fresh want within the hour cannot stand in that company.

Try the variable stripped bare, the way an investigator strips it. Take the laugh you love, the break you need, the company you cherish, the rest your legs beg for, and lift the dose out of the picture. Does the laugh fail? Does the rest refuse? Does the film fail to arrive? Or does the scene stand brighter without the rustle and the coating and the clock-watching for the next? You know the answer because you have lived both halves. The scene carried the joy. The dose sneaked a ride and sent a bill.

It does plenty TO you. It does nothing FOR you.

Read that verdict twice and let it stand without softening. It is not anger speaking. It is arithmetic from your own day. Plenty to teeth and mood and wallet and time. Nothing to the life that laugh and bread and hunger already give. When arithmetic is this plain, debate ends.

FOR THE REST OF YOUR LIFE

A last weighing remains, and it is measured in years.

Count the day we logged and stretch it forward. Not as threat, as arithmetic. Fifty grams here, a handful there, coin by coin, wrapper by wrapper, film by film. Multiply one ordinary moderate day by weeks, by years, by decades. Picture the shop trips in rain, the drawer refilled, the evening tidy repeated ten thousand times, the dentist's lamp a little brighter each visit, the afternoons dimmed and re-dimmed. Do you want to pay that tax and live that flatness for the rest of your life for something that never gave?

Now turn the glass over. Picture the same years with the tax unlaid. Mornings with a clear mouth. Hunger arriving cleanly and closing cleanly. Money staying home. Time unspent in queues for a lift that never lifts. Teeth meeting food rather than acid wash. Afternoons met by legs and air and work you own. Picture those ordinary days returning, season by season, for the rest of your life, with nothing missing because nothing was ever given.

Both pictures stretch the same length. Only one holds a gift.

I know the second picture may feel distant while the tug still whispers. That is why I do not ask you to act from fright at the first picture. Fright fades and the old story rushes back. I ask you to act from seeing. The seeing does not fade, because arithmetic does not fade. Once the FOR column is seen empty, it stays empty. A seen con is a dying con, and what dies from seeing needs no guarding by effort.

You came here fearing life would be greyer without sweetness. Look at your own paper again. Where was the colour? In the dose, or in the laugh and the break and the meal the dose interrupted? The colour was yours all along. The dose only dimmed it and sold it back for minutes at a high price.

Let the relief of that land softly. The burden was never the future without it. The burden was the daily tax with it.

3. JUDGE ONLY BY WHAT IT DOES FOR YOU
Forget harm versus benefit and ask for the benefit.

SUMMARY
- The harm debate keeps benefit alive by accident, so the fair question is what the dose itself adds.
- An ordinary moderate day fills the TO column with film, flatness, money spent and time lost.
- The FOR column stays empty when break, laugh, rest and meal keep their own credit.
- Frequent free sugars bathe teeth in acid, and brushing softens without cancelling that wash.
- Normal eating now sits far above population guidance, and feeling fine does not mean intake is low.
- A tiny bodily echo plus a learned story reads as need, and the dose only quiets for minutes what the last dose left.
- What looked like treat and help leaves want rather than thanks, and want is not a gift.
- Stretched across years, the daily tax buys nothing that the day itself did not already give.
