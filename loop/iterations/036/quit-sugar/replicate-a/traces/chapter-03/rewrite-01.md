Chapter 3
WHAT DOES IT DO FOR YOU

*Forget how much it harms you and ask what it ever did for you — name the gift and the trap falls.*

You saw how the Sugar Trap moves the hand before thought has finished, and I want you to bring those same clear eyes into daylight with me.

THE WRONG PAIR OF SCALES

We have weighed this thing on the wrong scales all our lives.

We put harm on one side and pleasure on the other and we ask which weighs more. A little harm, we say, for a lot of comfort. A little risk around waist and teeth, for help and reward and kindness in a hard day. We tell ourselves we are being honest and adult to admit both sides. We tell ourselves the scales just tip toward keeping it.

The fact is those scales are rigged.

Harm against benefit sounds fair only while benefit is real. If benefit is empty, the weighing is fraud. You do not ask whether a forged note does more harm than good. You ask what good is there at all.

We were taught to debate harm because harm can be debated forever. One study, one headline, one uncle who ate sweets till ninety, one scare that fades by lunch. While we argue harm, benefit sits safe in the corner, unexamined, glowing. That glow is the whole trap talking. It lets you admit the night looks trapped so long as you still believe the day holds something good.

You hear it in your own kind voice. I know it rots teeth and piles on weight and leaves me flat, but I enjoy it. But it gets me through. But life would be grey without the odd treat.

That but is where I want us to stand now, gently and fully, until there is nowhere left for it to hide.

Put harm to one side entirely. I will not frighten you with lists. I do not want you reading defensively, arguing for an old friend against me. I want you curious. Tell me what it does for you.

Not what it does to your teeth and skin and wallet and afternoons. What it does for you. Name one true gift that belongs to BAD SUGAR itself, that would be missing from your life without the dose, that no pause and no laugh and no meal could give.

Hold that question while we walk through an ordinary day together. Carry it like a lamp. If there is a gift, it will show itself. If there is none, that seeing will do more for you than a hundred warnings about harm ever could.

A DAY ON PAPER

Take yesterday. Not a bad day. Not a binge day you would confess with lowered eyes. An ordinary moderate day, the kind you call harmless.

Morning. Tea with two sugars and a frosted cereal that claims whole grain on the box. The spoon sweet, the milk sweet, the mouth coated sweet before the day has begun. Do you taste grain and milk? Or do you taste the coat over them? Note the time taken to buy it, pour it, rinse the bowl with that faint grit left behind. Note the price on the receipt at the till, small and repeated, week after week. Note the mouth from the first ten minutes, already filmed, already calling for water.

You call that breakfast. I ask you with warmth, what did the dose add to breakfast? Hunger would have welcomed plain food. The body would have closed cleanly on eggs and bread and fruit. The sweet coat did not close hunger. It opened it and left the tongue seeking.

Mid-morning. The desk drawer opens. A biscuit with coffee, then another because the first left the mouth seeking rather than settled. Ten minutes of hurry in the fingers, papers shuffled a little faster, then the faint dip in attention that sends the eyes to the clock. You call that the lull before lunch. Look closer. The mouth wants water. The teeth feel filmed. The purse is a coin lighter for the vending run.

Ask plainly. What did those ten minutes give that legs stretched to the window and cold air on the face would not have given? You did the work, tired and trying. The pause gave the rest. The biscuit gave hurry and film and the need for another.

Lunch eaten fast, with a sweet drink beside the plate. The meal itself could have closed hunger cleanly. The drink keeps hunger open. You finish feeling fed and oddly unclosed at once, as if the meal happened beside you rather than inside you. The afternoon starts with that coated tongue and the second search beginning already.

Think of the shop queue at lunch. The coins counted. The bottle cold in the hand. The first gulps bright and loud in the mouth. Half an hour later the brightness gone, the mouth dry again, the teeth edged, the bin holding another plastic shell. What stayed with you from that drink past one o'clock? The thirst returned. The hunger never closed. The money did not return. The time in the queue did not return.

Three o'clock. We all know three o'clock. The light flattens. The head thickens. A chocolate bar from the shop downstairs, eaten at the desk without leaving the chair, wrapper crackling low so no one hears. A brief brightening, talked up loudly in the head, then the fog thickening again by four, and the eyes back on the drawer.

You say sugary somethings to get me through. I hear you with warmth, because I said those words for years. Look at what got you through yesterday hour by hour. You sat there. You answered the mail. You spoke to the colleague and laughed at his story. You stood and stretched and looked out at the rain. Those were yours. The bar gave ten minutes of buzz and an hour of flatness and the need for another. It rode on the back of your effort and claimed the credit.

Evening. The shop on the way home. The packet for the children that loses a handful in the car. Count that handful honestly. Not seated. Not hungry after supper. Eaten with one hand on the wheel at lights, sweet dust on the fingers, the bag folded quickly before the door opens. What did those miles taste of? Petrol and hurry and a mouth now too sweet for supper.

Supper itself, cooked simply and eaten fast because the edge was already blunted. Then the dessert you planned as a small treat when you feel you deserve one, eaten standing while supper is cleared, barely seated, barely tasted past the third spoon. The first spoon bright. The second less bright. The third eaten to finish rather than to enjoy. The bowl rinsed with that sticky ring to scrub.

Then the sofa dose with television, hand to mouth in rhythm with the programme, not with hunger. Count the wrappers in the pocket and the bin. Count the minutes spent buying, unwrapping, clearing away, brushing crumbs from the cushions, promising tomorrow will be lighter. Count the money again, not as a fortune in one day, but as a quiet tax laid on every single day.

Night. Teeth brushed with that extra scrub to lift the film. Mouth dry in the night. Sleep a little broken, morning mouth stale. You wake tired and call it age or stress or poor sleep, and reach for the sweet tea to lift the tiredness.

Now lay the day on paper in two columns, as an investigator would.

In the TO column write what BAD SUGAR did to you yesterday. To the teeth filmed and acid-washed after each dose. To the appetite kept open past satisfaction. To the mood lifted for minutes and lowered for hours. To the wallet taxed coin by coin. To the time spent in shops and queues and hurried eating and clearing away. The list fills fast. You lived it. You can feel it in the mouth and the purse and the four o'clock head.

In the FOR column write what it did for you. Not what the pause did for you. Not what the laugh with a friend did for you. Not what sitting down for five minutes did for you. What the dose itself added that would be missing without it.

You stare at that column. You want to write taste. You want to write lift. You want to write reward and comfort and celebrating with chocolate. Keep your pen honest. Taste of what, that left the mouth coated and seeking? Lift that left you lower within the hour and reaching again? Reward that left you a little flat, counting that you had been good all week and then undone it in ten minutes? Comfort that left the trouble untouched and added a new trouble of regret?

Look at the clock through that day. Seven in the morning till eleven at night, dose to dose to dose. How many minutes of that day would you guard as precious because BAD SUGAR was in them? The tea that coated? The drawer biscuit that filmed? The lunch drink that kept hunger open? The bar that fogged? The car handful that blunted supper? The standing dessert barely tasted? The sofa handful eaten to the programme? Which minute would turn grey tomorrow if the dose were missing but the pause and the laugh and the meal remained?

None. That is the answer your own paper gives.

TEETH AND THE QUIET BILL

Let us speak of the body for a moment, flatly and without scolding, because the body keeps books even when the mind looks away.

Free sugars feed the film on teeth, and that film turns each dose into acid on enamel. That is how decay begins, dose by dose, not by one wild night but by the steady wash of sweetness over teeth that never quite clear. Brushing and fluoride matter, of course they matter, and no honest person pretends they do not. They soften the bill. They do not cancel the mechanism. A mouth bathed many times a day in sugary liquid and soft starch lives in acid far longer than a mouth that meets plain food and water. I am not writing your personal future here, only the shape of the bill as it falls across millions of mouths.

Look wider, still without panic and without preaching. The guidance drawn for whole populations says keep free sugars below ten in every hundred calories, below about fifty grams a day for most adults, with further gain below five. Ordinary eating now sits far above that line, not in strange houses but in normal kitchens like yours and mine. Feeling fine does not mean intake is low. Feeling fine while slow change goes on around daily habit is exactly how gradual harm misleads.

I lay that guidance here not to frighten you into action. I do not want you to act from fear, and fear will not keep you free. I lay it here to close one excuse. The excuse that a moderate habit sits safely inside wisdom. Normal now means far above guidance. Harmless now means dosed many times a day. The moderate you describe on paper is already a heavy user by the measure of the body, and the body, not the packet, writes the final account.

Hear my care for you in this. If you live with diabetes or take medicine that changes blood sugar or appetite, or carry any condition where food change carries risk, your clinician leads and this book only changes a belief. I give no eating plan and no medical counsel. I speak of population lines to show why feeling fine misleads, never to lay a sentence on you.

And having seen the TO side in its quiet truth, come back at once to the only question that matters. Even if teeth held and guidance vanished, even if the bill never arrived, what would remain in the FOR column? Harm can be debated study by study. Emptiness cannot. A thing that does so much to you and nothing for you needs no further trial.

What would you call a man who trips you into a ditch each morning and then takes your thanks and your coins for pulling you out at noon?

THE EMPTY COLUMN

We come now to the ghost itself.

You entered this chapter conceding harm but holding benefit. You said, it may rot and cost and drag, but it treats me and fuels me. You said it the way we all said it, kindly, reasonably, the way brainwashed sounds when it speaks in our own voice.

Look at that benefit closely, the way you looked at your day. Where does it live? Not in the hour after, where flatness and fresh want live. Not in the mouth after, where film lives. Not in the purse after, where lightness lives. Not in sleep after, where staleness lives. It lives only in the minutes of the dose, and even there it lives as quieting of a want the last dose left.

Think of the morning tea. Sweet, hurried, gone. Did it leave thanks? Or did it leave a mouth that wanted water and a hand that wanted more by ten? Think of the drawer biscuit. Did it leave power? Or did it leave film and a clock watched till lunch? Think of the evening dessert. Did it leave love? Or did it leave a bowl to scrub and a faint wish you had sat down and tasted supper?

We do the same test with every dose and the answer repeats. What leaves film and fog and fresh want within the hour cannot stand as gift.

Try the day stripped bare, the way an investigator strips it. Take the laugh you love, the pause you need, the company you cherish, the rest your legs beg for, and lift the dose out of the picture. Does the laugh fail? Does the rest refuse? Does the film fail to arrive? Or does the scene stand brighter without the rustle and the coating and the clock-watching for the next? You know the answer because you have lived both halves. The scene carried the joy. The dose rode along and sent a bill.

I know this is hard to accept, and I do not ask you to accept it on my word. I ask you to look at your own paper.

Ask yourself three plain questions, and answer as you would to a friend you love.

If the dose truly treated you, why did the mouth feel worse after than before, and why did the want grow rather than close? If the dose truly fuelled you, why did the flat hour return deeper each time and call for another within the hour? If the dose truly gave, what did you carry into sleep last night that you would mourn losing?

There is only one honest set of answers. The FOR column stays empty. The day gave you moments, and the dose rode on their backs and claimed their credit.

Once seen, the phrase that fooled us can be spoken plainly. What we called treat and help was never a genuine treat or fuel. Say those words slowly and feel how much falls with them. A genuine treat would leave thanks, not want. A genuine fuel would leave power, not flatness.

It does plenty TO you. It does nothing FOR you.

Read that verdict twice and let it stand without softening. It is not anger speaking. It is arithmetic from your own day. Plenty to teeth and mood and wallet and time. Nothing to the life that laugh and bread and hunger already give. When arithmetic is this plain, debate ends.

THE YEARS AHEAD

A last weighing remains, and it is measured in years.

Count the day we logged and stretch it forward. Not as threat, as arithmetic. Fifty grams here, a handful there, coin by coin, wrapper by wrapper, film by film. Multiply one ordinary moderate day by weeks, by years, by decades. Picture the shop trips in rain, the drawer refilled, the evening tidy repeated ten thousand times, the dentist lamp a little brighter each visit, the afternoons dimmed and re-dimmed. Do you want to pay that tax and live that flatness for the rest of your life for something that never gave?

Now turn the glass over. Picture the same years with the tax unlaid. Mornings with a clear mouth. Hunger arriving cleanly and closing cleanly. Money staying home. Time unspent in queues for a lift that never lifts. Teeth meeting food rather than acid wash. Afternoons met by legs and air and work you own. Picture those ordinary days returning, season by season, for the rest of your life, with nothing missing because nothing was ever given.

Both pictures stretch the same length. Only one holds a gift.

I know the second picture may feel distant while old stories still whisper. That is why I do not ask you to act from fright at the first picture. Fright fades and the old story rushes back. I ask you to act from seeing. The seeing does not fade, because arithmetic does not fade. Once the FOR column is seen empty, it stays empty. A seen con is a dying con, and what dies from seeing needs no guarding by effort.

You came here fearing life would be greyer without sweetness. Look at your own paper again. Where was the colour? In the dose, or in the laugh and the pause and the meal the dose interrupted? The colour was yours all along. The dose only dimmed it and sold it back for minutes at a high price.

Let the relief of that land softly. The burden was never the future without it. The burden was the daily tax with it.

3. JUDGE ONLY BY WHAT IT DOES FOR YOU
Forget harm versus benefit and ask for the benefit.

SUMMARY
- The harm debate keeps benefit alive by accident, so the fair question is what the dose itself adds.
- An ordinary moderate day fills the TO column with film, flatness, money spent and time lost.
- The FOR column stays empty when pause, laugh, rest and meal keep their own credit.
- Frequent free sugars bathe teeth in acid, and brushing softens without cancelling that wash.
- Normal eating now sits far above population guidance, and feeling fine does not mean intake is low.
- What looked like treat and help leaves want rather than thanks, and want is not a gift.
- Stretched across years, the daily tax buys nothing that the day itself did not already give.
