Chapter 9
WHO BUILT THIS WANT

IN THIS CHAPTER

*That hunger was never born in you; it was packed for you, and both fears that pull at you were packed with it.*

You have stood at the sill with clear mouth and found the morning whole without paper. You have sat at the table and found taste lingers without ash. You have stood in the doorway and found legs and air do the resting.

What remains is the question underneath. If mornings and meals and breaks never needed it, where did the want come from? Why does it still feel like nature, and why does fear still speak in your own voice?

THE FIRST DOSE WAS NEVER YOURS

We did not arrive at smoking the way we arrive at food or rest or laughter.

No child wakes with a need for paper and ash. No lungs ask before they have been bitten to be bitten again. No tongue asks for bite. What we call want came after the doing, not before it, and the doing came because someone else needed it to come.

Think of your own start and stay exact, because the start proves the rest.

Most of us were young. Fourteen, sixteen, nineteen, or newly at work wanting to look unshaken. A mate held out the pack. An older hand lit at the table and made it look like belonging. The hand felt awkward empty and the lit tube gave it somewhere to go. We took the first pull to show we could, to be inside the circle, to have a pause that looked grown.

Do you remember that first pull? Eyes watering. Throat burning. Head swimming. Cough bent double over the sink while the older smokers laughed and said you will get used to it. That cough was the body telling the plain truth. This does not belong here. We called the truth inexperience and we trained past it.

Ask it of that day and answer from what you lived.

Did you weigh what the tube does for you and against you and then choose it? Did you compare a life with feeds to a life without and decide the feeds were worth the rent? Did your body report pleasure and ask for more?

There is only one honest answer. There was no weighing. There was no born need. There was a con on phoney information — grown, calm, company, pause — and then repetition till the repetition made a clock, and then we called the clock me.

I lived that training as you did. I coughed and persisted. I learned to pull past the burn till the burn went quiet. I learned to carry the box everywhere and to panic when the count ran low at midnight. I learned to read the faint edginess between feeds as my own character. None of that was nature ripening. It was schooling.

We fell into the trap together, you and I and every smoker who ever learned to inhale. That sameness removes the blame. You were not weak to be trained. You were young and open and surrounded by painted boxes and lit hands, and a normal body learns what it is given daily. The blame does not sit on the trained. It sits on the trainer.

Who trained us? Not a friend. Friends do not count twenty gaps through your day. Follow the training back past the mate who held out the pack, past the film where the hero lit at the best line, past the father at the doorway, and you come to a bench and a factory and a plan. Someone decided the tube should look clean. Someone decided the filter should look kind. Someone decided the name should whisper mild and gold. Someone counted how many fades fit in a day and cut the box to hold that number.

That is why desire now feels like nature. It was fitted so early and fed so often that the fitting disappeared inside the feeling. Take the fitting out into daylight and the feeling changes its name. Not my hunger. Their schedule, installed by repetition.

The fact is you never chose this want the way you choose food you love. You were conned into the first ones, then the clockwork took over.

HOLD THE BOX IN YOUR HAND

Take the pack as it sits in your pocket today and look at it as an object, not as part of you.

Twenty tubes in a flip box. Cellophane crackle. Foil tongue. Filter tips all facing the same way like cartridges in a case. Print shouting from the side while the colours whisper from the front — gold, silver, blue, red. Lift one out. White paper. Brown filter. Tiny cork print to look like something grown. Weight almost nothing. Smell faintly sweet and chemical before lighting, foul after.

Hold the whole pack in the palm. Feel its weight as a day's supply box.

That is what it is. Not a friend. Not a pleasure kit. A dispenser for the day's feeds, counted out elsewhere. Morning feed. Mug feed. Work feed. Plate feed. Talk feed. Doorway feed. Night feed. Twenty measured doses through paper tubes, spaced by the fading of the last dose.

Ask it plain while the box sits in the palm.

Who counted the twenty? You did not wake with twenty needs listed. The maker counted twenty gaps. Who spaced the gaps through your day? You did not space hungers around meals and talk. The fading dose spaced them and meals and talk took the blame. Who decided the tube should look clean and the tip should look kind? You did not ask for kindness in a tube. The designer did.

We talk about our brand as if it held our character. Mild. Lights. Menthol. Regular. King size. Slim. We defend the packet in pubs as if taste lived there. Taste does not live in burning paper. What we defended was the familiarity of our own feeds arriving on time in a familiar dress. Take the dress away and the job stands bare.

See the theatre piece by piece, because each piece was built to let the bite pass.

The filter is not kindness. It is a stage prop that cools the mouth just enough for the lungs to accept what the mouth would refuse plain. The cork print is not nature. It is paint to make a chemical look grown. The gold ring is not quality. It is jewellery on a dispenser. The slim is not elegance. It is the same job in a narrower dress. The menthol is not freshness. It is flavour laid over bite so bite can slip through. The light name is not description. It is costume that lets the mind rest while the hand works harder underneath.

That box is the nicotine trap in your palm.

We paid for theatre and called it choice. I carried my brand like a flag for years and argued its taste across tables. What taste? Paper burning tastes of nothing a tongue would ask for twice. What I loved was feeds arriving on time, the thin quiet after each lift, the clock settling for an hour. The packet only dressed that settling in colours I could defend.

Once seen, the box cannot go back to being part of the hand. It stands as what it always was, a day's supply box made elsewhere and placed in your palm for profit, small and mean with no part of your life left inside it when each pleasure is given back to its owner.

The morning belongs to waking. The pause belongs to legs and air. The talk belongs to people. The box belongs to the seller.

THE PRODUCT IS NICOTINE

One of their own scientists wrote it down in an internal planning memo, not in a public brochure, not for you to read.

The cigarette should be conceived not as a product but as a package. the product is nicotine.

Read that in plain words. The paper is wrapping. The tobacco is stuffing. The filter is theatre. The brand is paint. What moves through all of it is the dose that keeps the faint edginess returning on time so the next tube will be wanted within the hour.

That is not my phrase. That is the maker's view of his own goods, written when he thought no smoker was listening.

Ask what that sentence does to every line we told ourselves.

“I smoke for taste.” Would a taste need to hide behind menthol and filters and mild names to be borne? Would a taste need twenty repeats a day to be enjoyed? Food tastes and holds. Drink tastes and holds. Music holds without re-lighting. No true taste behaves like this, bright for two minutes then thinner within the hour and demanding again.

“I smoke for pause.” Would a pause need a measured dose to be a pause? Legs pause. Eyes pause. Lungs pause in cold air. The pause you lived at the doorway stood whole without paper. What needed the tube was not the pause. It was the clock underneath the pause asking for its brief quiet.

“I smoke because I choose it.” Would a choice need a dispenser counted to twenty and carried everywhere, with panic when the count runs low and the shops are shut? Would a choice make you step out of films and meals and talks to tend it in rain? Choice does not panic and does not interrupt. Manufactured need panics.

The fact is the pleasure never sat in the tube. The tube rode on the pleasure of the moment and signed its name on it.

See the proportion. Farms and factories and lorries, chemists and designers and packet artists, all bent to one narrow end — that the faint edginess returns on time and that you read its return as your own desire. Not your hunger. Their schedule, set ticking in your body by repetition, then read by you as character.

That story is the Smokescreen doing its work.

It works by making the manufactured feel personal. Your brand. Your break. Your moment. Your type. Take the personal pronouns away and what remains? A box. Tubes. A drug that fades fast and must be fed fast. A mind taught to lend that cycle its most intimate words. Give the words back to their owners and the box stands alone.

“I need it to cope.” Did you need it before the first dose taught the need? Did the child at the sickbed through the night need it while hours passed without thought because attention was elsewhere? Coping was yours before paper joined it.

“I love it really.” Do you love the tube or the quiet of the tug going down for minutes? Would you love the tube if it gave no quiet because no tug had been built? Love does not need a low first in order to feel high after.

It is the other way around. Not that you smoke because you want. You want because you smoke on a schedule built elsewhere.

THE HOLE YOU SMOKE THROUGH

Look closer at the mild promise, because the mild promise proves the con better than any speech.

The machine says light. The packet says light. The numbers say low. The mouth says smoother. So the mind says kinder, safer, a step toward sense while still smoking.

Now watch the human smoker, not the machine.

The machine takes a fixed puff through a holder. The vent holes in the filter stay open to the air and dilute the smoke. The numbers come out low on the bench.

You do not smoke like a holder.

In most mouths the story runs different. Lips often settle over those tiny holes without noticing. Fingers often settle over them without noticing. The chest pulls a little deeper because the draw feels thin and the body asks for the accustomed press. The puff runs a little longer. The next tube comes as soon because the quiet does not hold longer. What the vents diluted on the bench, the human mouth typically undoes in life.

Not every smoker does it in quite the same way — one pulls deeper, another covers more of the vent, another simply lights sooner — but the bench and the mouth are different smokers, and machine-measured low does not mean you took low.

That is not accusation. That is design meeting body. Most of us closed the gap that stood between us and the accustomed quiet in one form or another. I did it for years while telling myself I had been sensible to switch to silver.

Ask it of your own silver packet and stay exact.

Did the day's count fall when you moved to light? Did the thin pull wait longer between tubes? Did the hand lift less often, or did it lift much as often and pull a fraction harder while the mind rested on the word mild?

For most smokers there is only one honest answer. The count stays much the same. The pull runs harder. The mind rests while the hand works harder. We bought the word and paid with the lungs.

The vents are a hole in a gas mask sold as fresh air. Cover the hole without meaning to and breathe harder through the mask, then thank the mask for being mild. That is the size of the fraud, done not in a back room but at your own lips, by your own fingers, without your knowledge, while the packet kept its clean colours.

And mark this, because the seller counts on you missing it. The answer is not to hunt for a stronger tube that lets you puff less. Strong or mild, filtered or plain, slim or king, the job is the same — to deliver the fading dose and bring you back within about an hour. To argue which costume is kinder is to stay inside the fitting room. The point is not the costume. The point is the fitting.

Does that make you angry? Good. Be angry at the fitting, never at the fitted. You were not foolish to cover the holes. You were a normal body seeking its accustomed quiet through whatever tube was offered, while the offer called itself choice. The anger belongs on the bench that measured with open vents and printed low numbers, not on the mouth that closed them in life.

“But at least lights are a step down.” A step down to what? For most, to much the same count, much the same clock, the same leash measured to about an hour? Would you call it a step down if the step lands in the same place?

See the mild theatre for what it is and the last sensible exception falls. For most mouths, not milder. Only harder puffed. Not kindness. Only costume.

STALE, BREATHLESS AND CHAINED

Now name what the box charges for its theatre, not in money first but in the state it leaves, hour by hour.

Money first, because money is the smallest part and still shames the arithmetic. Count the packs through a year. Count through ten years. Count what else that money could have held — coats that last, food that feeds, days that do not need counting. We did not count because counting would have forced the question, and the question would have forced the look. Small daily sums hide large lifetime theft. That is how dispensers like to be paid, coin by coin, never in a lump that would startle.

Then name the body as it is lived, not as the packet paints it.

Stale first. Mouth stale on waking. Nose dulled to tea and toast and rain. Fingers yellowed at the tips. Hair holding last night's doorway. Clothes aired on the line and still carrying it. Partner turning a fraction in sleep from the breath. Child stepping back from the hug while we told ourselves they did not notice. We noticed. That is why the mints, the wash, the spray, the gum, the window open in winter, the Febreeze and the garage door. A pleasure that needs hiding after it is not pleasure. It is mess rented as ritual.

Breathless next. Stairs that leave the chest heaving while the non-smoker beside you talks on steady. Hill that shortens the stride. Laugh that turns to cough. Morning cough over the sink that we called clearing, as if lungs were meant to clear ash every morning for life. The body came fitted to climb and laugh and run for buses without commentary. We laid bite across that fitting daily and called the narrowing normal. Normal does not wheeze on two flights. Narrowed does.

Chained last, and chained is the worst because it touches every hour. Cannot sit through the film without glancing at the watch. Cannot take the long flight without counting the gaps. Cannot stand the talk without stepping out to tend. Cannot face the row, the wait, the delay, without the hand moving. Twenty managements a day — light, shield, draw, tap, stub, wash — laid across tasks that others do unbroken. We called that habit. It was a leash measured to about an hour, tugged on schedule.

Put the three together till they stand as one state, not three complaints. Mouth coated, chest tight, day broken into feeds. Day after day. Year after year. That is the permanent address the box rents you while it calls itself pause and friend. That is life lived stale, breathless and chained.

Speak that triple as fact, not as insult. I lived inside it and told myself outsiders exaggerated. They did not exaggerate. The ashtray smell in the curtains was real. The stairs were real. The glance at the watch in the cinema was real. What was unreal was the line that said this state was the price of pleasure. It was not the price of pleasure. It was the product doing its work while pleasure stood elsewhere, untouched.

And here is the hard part stated flat, because you must hear it once at full force before we put fear back in its place.

This state does not stay still. Coating thickens. Tightness deepens. The leash shortens as years lay dose on dose. The doctors have their names for where that road runs — cells that forget their manners, vessels that narrow, lungs that lose their spring — and if you feel ill or worried, speak to a clinician for your own care. I do not say that to sentence you. I say it because the seller knew the road and kept counting the twenty while you walked it, and you have a right to be told plain what the twenty buys.

Do not change because you are frightened. Fear never freed anyone; it only made the hand shake harder for the next tube. Change because the box never gave you a single thing worth the rent, and you can see it now with the paint off. The fright belongs to the facts. The motive belongs elsewhere, in the clear morning you have already lived and liked.

THE TWO ROPES

With pleasure gone and cost seen, fear steps forward as the last guard at the door. It comes as two voices pulling opposite ways, and both feel like you.

First rope: “You will fail.”

It speaks in your own history. Drawer of patches and gum. Calendar marks. Promises to the partner. Monday starts. The shop at ten at night. The voice says you are the type who slides, that the fifth day will find you out in the doorway, that the night out will undo you, that wanting proves you cannot. It points to every past effort that ended at the till and says that ending is you.

I know that rope because I pulled it with both hands. I told myself my will was thin. I told myself others could but I could not. I read each slide back as proof of nature and smoked to steady the shame that smoking had built.

Second rope: “You will succeed and hate it.”

It speaks in pictures of the future without. Breaks with nothing in the hand. Meals that do not close. Stress with no off-switch. Nights out watching others lift while you sit empty. Mornings flat. Work flat. Talk flat. A smaller life lived in grey, holding out forever against a want that never fully dies. It says freedom is possible but not worth having, that you will be a stranger to yourself, that the prop held the person up and the person will sag without it.

I know that rope too. I stood outside pubs watching lit hands and felt left out before I had left. I pictured myself dull at tables, awkward at bars, short at work, and I lit to calm the picture that lighting had painted.

Pull the first rope and you guarantee the failure you dread, because a mind told it will fail stops looking and starts waiting to be proved right. Pull the second rope and you guarantee the grey you dread, because a mind told life will be flat reads every normal hour as deprivation and begs for the paint back. Either pull tightens the knot. That is the use of both.

See the geometry. Two ropes, one neck, both ends held by the same hands. The box taught the first fear by beating you with the wrong method till you blamed your nature. Every past effort was made while the belief was still intact, while each hour was framed as loss and each tug was read as proof you needed the tube. Of course those efforts ended at the shop. They were built to end there, because no mind holds out forever against a felt loss. That failure proved the method, not you.

The box taught the second fear by lending its name to every pleasure till you believed pleasure would go with it. Fail and you smoke. Succeed in gloom and you smoke to cure the gloom. Both roads lead back to the till.

That double pull is the tug-of-war of fear.

Name it when it speaks and it loses its cover. “I will fail” is not your character speaking. It is the memory of efforts made inside loss. “I will be empty without it” is not prophecy. It is the paint talking. You have already stood at the sill and at the table and at the doorway and found each hour whole without paper. Empty of what? Bite and sourness and six small managements. The kettle still sings. The sauce still lingers. Legs still rest on standing. Which of those did the tube supply? None. The grey you fear is life with the covering on. Take the covering away and colour thickens.

Ask the two questions that close the trap on fear.

If the first rope were true and you were unable, why are you able to go hours without thought when attention is elsewhere — film running, work gripping, child ill in the night — with the body steady and the mind clear? Unable bodies do not go quiet for hours. Clockwork briefly unfed goes quiet. That quiet proves ability, not inability.

If the second rope were true and life without were flat, why did the hour you lived without feel thicker, not thinner — more taste, more air, more continuity, fewer breaks in the run? Flat lives do not thicken when the supposed pleasure is removed. Robbed lives do not feel more continuous when the robber steps out. Yours thickened because nothing was removed but interference.

It is the other way around. Not that you fear because quitting is hard and life will be grey. You were taught to fear so you would not look, and not looking kept the box in the palm.

Let both ropes fall. There is nothing to hold on either end but paper.

SUMMARY
- The want was not born in me; it was trained in by first doses and kept ticking by feeds through the day.
- The pack is a day's supply box, and the tube is wrapping around a dose, not a taste or a pause.
- Mild and filter are theatre; in most mouths lips and fingers cover the vents in life and the hand pulls harder while the packet prints low numbers.
- The daily rent is a coated mouth, a tightened chest and a day broken into feeds.
- Fear of failing and fear of succeeding pull opposite ways but both ends are held by the same box.
