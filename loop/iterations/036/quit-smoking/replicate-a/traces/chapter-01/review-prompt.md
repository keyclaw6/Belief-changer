Chapter 1
LET THIS BE EASY

**IN THIS CHAPTER**
The staircase and the tight chest. The friend waiting at the top. The heavy smoker who stopped in a single evening. The promise with no catch. The single request that holds it together.

*You do not need to suffer to stop smoking; you only need to see it clearly.*

Too Good To Be True?

I know what you are thinking, because I thought it myself for years.

“This sounds too good to be true.”

You have smoked for years. You have lit the first one with morning tea and the last one with the night news. You have smoked to get through stress, to punctuate work, to fill breaks, to round off meals, to keep company on nights out. You have tried to cut back. You have tried patches from the drawer, gum from the chemist, white-knuckle evenings where you stared at the clock. And here comes another book telling you it can be simple.

I understand the tiredness behind your eyes as you open these pages. You are браced, if I may put it plainly, for another lecture. You expect graphs of damaged lungs. You expect to be told you are weak. You expect to be told to clench your teeth and hang on. If that is what this book was, I would not blame you for closing it now.

It is not that book.

I smoked heavily myself, day in and day out, for a long time. I am not a doctor wagging a finger from a clean desk. I am not a theorist who read about smoking in a library. I lit up in rain and wind, in doorways and cars, on platforms and balconies. I knew where every sheltered corner was in town. I knew the small arithmetic of packets and lighters and hours until the shops shut. I told myself the same stories you tell yourself. I believed I enjoyed it. I believed I needed it to think and to calm down. I believed stopping would leave a hole nothing could fill.

And I stopped, in the end, in one clear evening, without a struggle that scarred me, and I have never looked back with longing. Since then I have sat with thousands of smokers just like you and watched the same change happen in them. Not stronger people. Not special people. Tired people. Busy people. People who had failed before and were sure they would fail again. They did not become different people. They saw something different.

That is why I can speak to you as I do now, with complete confidence and without a trace of contempt for you. I have been where you are. We all start the same way, we smokers. We take that first puff because friends do, because it looks grown, because we are curious, because someone offers. We cough. It tastes foul. And then, puff by puff, we learn to tolerate it until we claim we like it. There is no judgment in that description. We were not foolish. We were uninformed. We did what young people do everywhere.

So let me ask you plainly, as one smoker to another, and answer honestly in your own head.

If you had never lit that very first cigarette, what exactly would be missing from your life right now? Would food have no taste? Would friendship be impossible? Would work be undone?

If cigarettes truly gave you something precious and irreplaceable, why are you holding a book about stopping?

Have all those hard, grinding attempts left you stronger and calmer, or simply more tired of the struggle itself?

You know the honest answers. Nothing would be missing. You are reading because some part of you already senses the bargain is poor. And those attempts left you tired, not stronger.

Your doubt does not prove you are closed-minded. It proves you have been disappointed before and are now protecting yourself. This time is different.

The Stairs Tell The Truth

Let me take you to an ordinary afternoon that has nothing to do with theories.

You are in town. You have just had lunch. You are a little late. You cut through the side entrance of the car park where the lift is out of order, and you take the stairs. Two flights. Concrete steps, a metal rail, a painted arrow pointing up.

You start up with your bag over your shoulder. By the halfway landing your legs feel heavy. Your chest feels tight. You are pulling air in through your mouth. You have to pause by the fire door, pretending to check your phone, while your heart thuds in your ears.

A friend comes up behind you, a non-smoker of your age, carrying the same sort of bag, wearing the same sort of shoes. He takes the same stairs at the same pace and arrives beside you breathing through his nose, talking in full sentences, glancing at his watch because he is thinking about the meeting, not about his chest.

You laugh it off. “Unfit,” you say. “Too many lunches.” He laughs too. You both walk on. But for a second, on that landing, with your hand on the rail and your phone as a prop, you felt something you do not say aloud. A small, cold recognition. This is not how a body is meant to climb two flights.

I ask you to hold that picture. Not as a threat. Not as a diagnosis. I am not your doctor and I will not pretend to read your chest like a chart. I am asking you to trust what your own body told you on ordinary concrete steps. We smokers learn to explain that moment away. We blame age. We blame weight. We blame the lunch. We blame the bag. We will blame anything except the one habit that fills our lungs with smoke twenty times a day.

Watch what happens next in that memory. You get outside. The air is cool on your face. The tightness eases as you stand still. Your hand moves, almost on its own, toward your pocket. You light up. The first draw scratches, then settles. You tell yourself, “There. That steadied me.”

Did it? The stairs are still behind you. The tight chest was there before the light. The cigarette did not carry you up the steps. Your legs did. Your heart did. Your lungs did, working hard on your behalf. The smoke added nothing to that climb. It only gave your pause something to do while your body recovered on its own.

That is the value of the stairs. They do not argue. They do not lecture. They show. Your body is honest even when your mind is still making excuses for the habit. Keep that honesty with you as we go on. You will need it less than you think, but it will help you at the start to know your body is already on your side.

I Was Where You Are

Let me tell you a little about me, because you have a right to know who is asking for your attention.

I was the smoker other smokers felt sorry for. I did not smoke five a day to be sociable. I smoked from the first cup to the last light. I planned journeys around where I could light up. I stood in the rain outside restaurants while my food went cold inside. I kept a spare packet in the car and another in my coat because the thought of running out made my stomach turn. I hid the extent of it from people I loved, not because I am a liar by nature, but because I was ashamed of how much I needed those little paper tubes.

I tried to stop the way most of us try. I picked dates. I told friends, then avoided them when I slipped. I chewed gum until my jaw ached. I wore patches that itched. I counted hours. I counted days. I made bargains with myself about weekends and holidays and parties. Each time I lasted a little while through sheer gritted effort, and each time I felt I was missing something, and each time I went back, I concluded the same thing: I am weak.

That conclusion was false. Utterly false.

The night I stopped for good, nothing dramatic happened. No illness. No scare in a hospital corridor. I had finished reading and thinking in a way I will share with you in these pages. I smoked my last cigarette of the evening, not as a treat to be mourned, but with my eyes open to what it actually was — hot smoke, acrid paper, a queasy tug in my throat. I stubbed it out, went to bed, and woke the next morning with a strange lightness. Not shakiness. Lightness. As if a noise I had lived with for years had suddenly switched off and I could hear the quiet underneath.

What surprised me most was what did not happen. I did not sit rocking in a chair craving. I did not snap at everyone around me. I went to work. I drank tea. I answered the phone. I did the ordinary things smokers are sure they cannot do without lighting up, and I did them with clearer attention than I had managed in years. Moments came when my hand twitched toward the old pocket, out of habit, and the thought passed through like a cloud and was gone. I did not wrestle it. I recognized it.

I tell you this not to boast. I tell you because you need to know the person speaking to you is not asking you to do something he has not done himself, and done the easy way. I did not stop because I am stronger than you. I stopped because I finally saw what smoking was doing and what it was not doing, and once I saw it, continuing made no sense.

We smokers share the same history. We learned to inhale poison without coughing and called that pleasure. We attached a light to every pause — after food, after work, after love, after argument — until we believed the pause belonged to the cigarette. We told each other stories in smoking shelters about how it helped us cope, how it helped us think, how it made coffee better and nights out brighter. Those stories felt true because we all repeated them. Feeling true and being true are not the same thing, as those stairs showed you.

You are not reading the words of a judge. You are reading the words of a fellow smoker who got out the far side and came back to hold the door open.

Why Reading Costs You Nothing

Now let us talk about the bargain I am offering you, because it matters that you understand you risk nothing by reading on.

Some books demand a leap of faith. They ask you to believe first and understand later. They ask you to suffer first and feel better later. I ask neither.

All I ask is that you read with an open mind and keep smoking exactly as you do now while we talk. Smoke as usual on the mornings and breaks we will picture together. Bring your packets with you into the next chapter. There is nothing you must put down tonight, no date you must circle, no count you must keep. Read in order. Do not skip ahead looking for the trick at the back. Let one idea settle before you pick up the next.

Why do I ask you to keep smoking for now? For a very simple reason. If I told you to stop before you have seen clearly, you would read every page through a haze of missing and longing, and you would miss the point. I want your mind clear and your habit undisturbed while you examine it. We cannot examine a picture honestly while we are afraid someone is about to snatch it away.

Think what that means in practice. You do not have to be brave to turn the next page. You do not have to be ready. You do not have to believe me yet. You only have to keep reading and notice whether what I describe matches your own days. If I am wrong, you will have lost an hour or two of reading time and smoked as usual throughout. If I am right, you stand to leave behind the cough in the morning, the sour taste, the money burned, the planning and hiding, the stairs that shame you.

Put it in plain arithmetic you can check for yourself. The position is simple: you have nothing to lose and everything to gain by reading on to the end and testing every claim against your own life.

Read that sentence again, slowly, and let it settle as a fact about this week, not as a slogan. Nothing to lose. You keep your cigarettes while we talk. You keep your routine. You keep your friendships and your coffee and your breaks. Everything to gain. Clearer breathing in the morning. Food tasting as it did when you were young. Evenings that do not revolve around stepping outside. Mornings that do not begin with a cough and a hunt for the lighter.

I know the voice that answers back, because it answered in me. It says, “But I have lost before. I have spent money on cures that did nothing. Why should words on a page be any different?”

That voice deserves an answer. You lost money before because you bought props to hold while the want stayed intact. You bought something to chew, something to stick on your arm, something to puff that was not a cigarette. The want stayed exactly where it was, and props do not remove want. This book does not sell you a prop. It removes the want by showing you the want was based on a misunderstanding. That is a different kind of work entirely, done in the mind, not in the mouth or on the skin.

So do not decide now whether you can stop. Decide only whether you can afford to keep reading while you smoke as normal and see. You can. Any smoker can do that much, however tired, however busy, however skeptical.

What I Ask Before We Start

There is one more thing to place on the table before we begin properly, and it is the most important promise in this book.

You have been told, by kind people and by unkind voices in your own head, that stopping is hard, painful, and slow. That you must steel yourself. That you must wait months to feel normal. That some people simply cannot do it. After twenty failures, who would not believe that?

I tell you the opposite, and I tell it to you flatly, without hedging, because hedging is what people do when they are not sure. I am sure.

It is possible to stop smoking easily, immediately and permanently without carrying a sense of loss into the rest of your life.

Let me unpack that, because each word matters and I do not want you to glide over them.

Easily means you do not need to fight yourself day and night. The struggle you remember was not proof that stopping is hard by nature. It was proof that stopping while still believing cigarettes give you something precious is hard. Keep the belief that they help and you will feel torn. Change the belief and there is nothing to wrestle. Ease is not a trick I add at the end. Ease is what happens naturally when the reason for wanting drops away.

Immediately means you do not have to serve time to earn back your health and peace. You do not become a non-smoker after a hundred hard days. You become a non-smoker the moment you smoke your last one with clear eyes and do not light another. Your body begins to settle from that hour. Your mind is already settled because you made the decision with joy rather than dread. There is no probationary period in which you are half one thing and half the other.

Permanently means you do not spend the rest of your life looking over your shoulder. This is not a pause until the next party or the next crisis. When you see what cigarettes do and do not do, you do not need to keep reminding yourself. You do not need anniversaries. You simply live as someone who does not smoke, the way you live as someone who does not drink petrol. No one congratulates themselves each morning for not drinking petrol. The question is closed.

I can hear the laugh that wants to come. “Easily? Immediately? Permanently? For me? After all my attempts?” Yes. For you. I say it knowing your history, not ignoring it. Your history of trying and slipping does not disqualify you. It qualifies you, because you have proved you want to be done with this. The only thing missing was the clear view that makes wanting and doing line up.

Ask yourself once more, quietly: if stopping really did mean losing a true friend and a true help, would ease even be possible to imagine? The fact that ease feels like a dream you dare not believe shows how much of the difficulty lives in the belief, not in the body. Bodies get over a missed dose quickly. Beliefs keep us smoking for decades.

I will not ask you to take my word for any of this on trust alone. I will show you, step by step, in ordinary scenes you recognize — mornings, meals, breaks, stress at a desk, drinks with friends — how the credit you give to the cigarette belongs elsewhere. By the time we reach your last cigarette, you will not need my promise. You will have your own evidence.

Until then, hold the two thoughts I have given you in this opening. They are enough to carry.

You risk nothing by reading while you smoke as now. And an easy, immediate, lasting stop is possible for a smoker exactly like you.
