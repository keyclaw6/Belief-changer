Chapter 5
PARTIES, PAUSES AND MILDS

*Your work threw the party, and the mild only made you puff harder for the same dose.*

The Pellet After Everything

You have watched the hand move after pressure and seen the papers sit still. Now we take the excuse that feels kindest, the one we keep because it sounds like kindness to ourselves.

"They are my little parties. I work hard. Surely I have earned this."

I spoke that way for years. I paid myself the same way. We all did.

Picture the chain as we lived it. The classroom door clicks shut. Forty faces gone. Chalk dust in the air. Voice tired in a good way. Before the next thought, the hand is in the pocket. Light in the corridor while the echo of our own voice is still in the ears. The shoulders drop. We call that minute the payment for teaching well.

An hour later at the desk. A paragraph fought for and found. The last sentence lands after three false starts. Push back. Neck stiff. A small lift in the chest. Lean back. Light. The smoke curls above the page while we read the line again. We call that minute the payment for writing the line.

Later still. The drive to the shops and back. Traffic, list, queue, till, bags. Engine ticking as it cools on the step. Bags on the step waiting to be carried in. Light before the door is opened. We call that minute the payment for getting it done.

Teaching a class. Finishing a story. Finishing a paragraph. Driving the miles. Driving to the shops. Almost everything finished got a dose lit on top of it.

I know how true the feeling is. The chest loosens a notch. The day gets a full stop. We say the dose crowns the work.

Stay with that full stop, because the detail gives the con away. What did the work feel like in the second before the match struck? There was already a lift there. The class had gone. The voices had listened. A joke had landed. The paragraph had clicked. The bags were won. The body knew it was done. Then the hand moved and the tube took its place on top of a feeling that was already complete.

Ask it of the corridor minute. The teaching was yours. The timing was yours. The patience with the slow row at the back was yours. The chalk on your fingers was yours. Where in that hour was there a gap that only burning paper could fill? There was no gap. There was a finished hour, warm and tired, and a tug that arrived on schedule and asked to be fed inside that hour.

Ask it of the desk minute. You stared at the dead sentence. You crossed it out. You walked to the window. You came back. You found the verb. Who did that? The dose was unlit in the pack while you did it. It sat silent while you struggled. It contributed nothing to the finding. Then, once found, it arrived to sign the work.

Ask it of the step minute. You drove the miles. You watched the mirrors. You braked soft in the wet. You remembered the milk without writing it down. You queued behind a slow trolley and kept your temper. Hands on the wheel. Eyes on the road. Feet on the pedals. The dose drove none of it. It waited in the pocket while you drove, then asked for its bow on the step.

Who Taught And Who Wrote

Put three plain questions to your own memory and answer from what you lived, not from what you were taught to say.

Who taught that class — you with your voice and your years, or the white stick burning in the corridor after?

Who found that sentence — you with your patience, or the ash lengthening above the page?

Who drove those miles and carried those bags — you with your hands and eyes on the road, or the dose lit on the step?

There is only one honest answer. You taught. You wrote. You drove. The dose arrived after and took the bow.

See how widely we spread that bow. Finished means light. Waiting means light. Good news means light. A hard hour got through means light. What kind of payment fits opposite moments so neatly? A true payment has a shape. A meal has a shape. A word of thanks has a shape. A ten-minute sit with your feet up has a shape. This one has no shape except timing. It arrives when the tug calls and takes the name of whatever you were doing.

I lived that spread and called it variety. Morning dose to start. Mid-morning dose to keep going. Midday dose to pause. Afternoon dose to finish. Evening dose to close. Each one named after its hour. Start, keep, pause, finish, close. Five names. One feed.

Watch the naming happen in real time and the trick stands out clean. You finish and feel good. The tug is also there, faint, asking. Two feelings arrive together — done-work lift and faint pull. We have been trained to read them as one feeling with one cure. Light, and the pull drops for a few minutes. The lift remains, but with the pull gone the total feels brighter, so we say the dose made the brightness. It did not. It removed only the dim it had brought itself.

Think of a child finishing a drawing. He holds it up. Face bright. No tube. No match. The lift is whole. Think of a man who never started finishing your exact paragraph. He leans back. He reads it twice. He smiles a little. He stands and pours water. The lift is whole. Only we have learned to put a filter between done and felt-done, and then to thank the filter for letting the feeling through.

The Pause That Crowned Nothing

We say the dose gives us a pause. Let us look at that pause with clear eyes.

You step to the doorway. Cold air on the face. Hands empty for a breath. Noise of the room behind you fading. You light. You watch the street. You think over the hour. You stub and go back in.

Take the tube out in your head and leave everything else. Doorway still there. Cold air still there. Five minutes still there. Quiet still there. Thinking still there. What is missing? Only lighting, sucking, watching the ember, tapping ash, checking the wind, counting minutes till you must go back.

The stopping steadied. The fuss borrowed.

I know the voice that answers, because it spoke loudly in me.

"But I need the break. Without it I would work through and burn out."

You need the break. We all need the break. Shoulders need dropping. Eyes need lifting far away. Lungs need slow air. The question is never break or no break. The question is what the break is made of.

A break made of standing, stretching, drinking water, looking at sky, saying two sentences to a mate — that rests any shoulders. A break made of that plus tending a burning tube — lighting in wind, shielding the ember, sucking hard, holding, stubbing half-burned when called — that rests nothing extra. It charges you for a pause you already owned.

Follow one ordinary workday and count what the pause-dose really added. Eight-forty, corridor, two minutes watching the clock while the tube burns. Eleven, stairwell, three minutes fanning smoke from the coat. One, bench, four minutes ashing into a can while the food cools. Three, doorway, three minutes nodding at a mate while thinking about the ember. Five, step, two minutes hurrying the last drags because the bags are waiting. Fourteen minutes of tending. Zero minutes of added rest.

We tell ourselves the dose marks the edge between work and not-work. Did it mark the edge, or did the edge mark it? The class ended. The paragraph ended. The drive ended. The edge was already there, sharp and clean. The hand moved inside the edge and claimed the edge was its doing.

Look at waiting, which we also call pause. Bus late. Reply late. Kettle slow. We light to fill the gap. Did the tube shorten the gap by one second? Did the bus come sooner? Did the reply arrive faster? Did the water boil quicker? The gap sat exactly where it was. Only the tug went quiet while we tended the tube inside the gap.

Look at starting, which we also call pause in reverse. Before the hard call. Before the marking pile. Before the long drive. We light to get ready. Did the tube put one ready thought in your head? Take the tube out and leave everything else. Chair still there. List still there. Breath still there. What is missing? Only the shared fuss of lighting and ashing. The readiness was yours. The steadiness was yours. The dose was only ever sneaking a ride on a preparing minute.

Who Threw That Party

Stay with the paragraph minute a little longer, because the feeling there is sweetest and therefore clearest.

You push the chair back. Neck stiff. Eyes dry. The last line reads right. A small lift rises in the chest — done, for now. In that lift the hand moves. Match flares. First draw bites. We call the bite pleasure and the lift payment and fuse the two.

Separate them in your head and the fusion breaks.

The lift came from meaning made. You wrestled a thought into words. That is human work, and human work pays in its own coin the second it lands. No one taught you to feel that lift. You felt it at school before any tube, when a sum came right. You felt it in games before any tube, when a ball went where you aimed. The lift belongs to done things. It arrives without permission and without smoke.

The bite came from paper and leaf and heat. Harsh at the back of the throat. Dry on the tongue. A little dizzy behind the eyes. Then, for a breath, the faint background pull goes quiet. We read that quiet as pleasure and glue it to the lift beside it.

It is the other way around. The work made the minute good. The dose signed its name on work it never did.

Think of taste, which we guard almost as closely. We say we love the taste after food, with coffee, in the pause. Yet watch what taste does in real life across one day. The first draw of the day bites harsh on empty lungs and sets us coughing. The midday one dries the mouth and makes the next bite of food blander. The evening one leaves a stale film that sends us for mints and water and a window opened. If a food tasted that way we would send it back and ask for the cook.

What we call taste is the hush of the tug for a breath, borrowed by the moment. After food we call it taste. After work we call it payment. After strain we once called it calm. The name changes with the hour. The feed stays the same underneath.

Look at the hands in that minute. One hand holds the pen that found the sentence. The other holds the tube that claims the sentence. Which hand did the work? You know. You have always known. The pen hand is a little ink-stained and tired. The tube hand is only busy. Busy is not doing. Tending is not making.

The Taste You Think You Love

I hear the next defence, because I held it longest.

"Perhaps about work. But the taste. I love the taste. After a meal, with coffee, that first pull — do not tell me that is nothing."

I will not tell you it is nothing felt. I will tell you what it is made of.

Picture the meal-end as we lived it. Plate pushed back. Warmth in the belly. Flavours still on the tongue — salt, fat, sweet, coffee bitter. Talk low around the table. That is a complete minute. Body fed. Senses full.

Then the hand moves. Light. First pull. Bite. Haze. The flavours on the tongue thin under smoke. The nose fills. The talk pauses for ashing. We say the dose finishes the meal.

Did it finish the meal, or did it cover the finish? Take the dose out in your head and leave everything else. Plate still there. Warmth still there. Coffee still warm. Flavours still clean on the tongue, lingering, separating, returning. What is missing? Only bite, haze, ash, and a sour that will ask for a mint in ten minutes.

We do a strange thing and call it love. We put smoke on top of taste and say smoke is taste. Watch a man who never started end the same meal. He lingers. He sips. He breathes through the nose. He tastes the after-taste lengthen. He does not rise to tend anything. His tongue stays clean for the next hour. Ours goes blunt and we call the blunting pleasure.

Ask it of your own tongue across the day, not of theory. When does the tongue feel sharpest — before the first dose of the day, or after the tenth? Every smoker knows the honest answer. Morning mouth before lighting is stale from the night, yes, but after brushing it is the cleanest it will be all day. By evening, after twenty lightings, everything tastes a little of ashtray — food, coffee, kiss, water. If the dose gave taste, the most-dosed tongue would taste most. It tastes least. The gift runs backwards.

Ask it of coffee, the closest ally. We say coffee and dose belong together, each makes the other better. Watch the pairing with clear eyes. Sip clean coffee. Note bitter, heat, lift. Now light and sip through haze. What improved? The coffee cooled while you managed the ember. The bitter thinned under bite. The lift you credit to the pair was already in the cup. The dose added dry mouth that asked for more coffee to wash it down, and more coffee that asked for another dose to sit beside it. Round and round. Two tugs feeding each other and calling the circle flavour.

Ask it of the first pull, the one we romanticise. Harsh. Hot. A little nauseous on empty lungs. Eyes watering. Is that flavour, or is that impact mistaken for flavour? Mustard has flavour. Orange has flavour. Fresh bread has flavour. Impact is not flavour. A slap has impact. We do not call a slap tasty. Yet we call the slap at the back of the throat tasty because for a second after the slap the background pull goes quiet. Quiet is not taste. Quiet borrowed by coffee is still only quiet.

Coffee, Food And The Dulled Tongue

Live one meal-end without the tube in your head, fully, and the credit reassigns itself.

You finish. You do not move at once. You sit. The heat of food settles. You breathe out slow. You take a sip of water. The mouth clears. You take a sip of coffee. The bitter stands clean. You hear the room. Forks. Low talk. Chair scrape. You feel the small, complete satisfaction of fed — a body signal older than any pack, older than any factory.

That signal needs no signature. Animals feel it. Children feel it. Men who never started feel it daily. It is the body saying enough, for now. We learned to interrupt it with lighting and then to thank the interruption for the signal it interrupted.

See how the interruption steals time as well as taste. The meal ends at one. The table is warm till half past. Without the tube, you would sit in that warmth, talk, digest, linger. With the tube, you excuse yourself, or you light at the table and change the air, or you hurry the sweet to get to the doorway. The lingering shrinks. The talk thins. The warmth is traded for tending. Then we say the dose made the evening. It made the evening shorter.

Take the morning version. Alarm. Feet find the floor. Mouth stale from night. Before food, before water, the hand finds the pack. First drag on empty lungs. Bite. Cough. Head light in a bad way. We call that taste to wake up. Did it wake taste, or did it punish a tongue that had just spent eight hours without a dose and was asking loudest? Watch the man beside you wake without it. Rinse. Drink. Bite of toast. The tongue wakes by use, not by smoke. Ours wakes by slap and then spends the day a little numb.

Take the work version. Mid-morning. Mouth dry from talking. You light. The dry deepens. You drink more coffee. The dry deepens again. By noon the tongue is coated. Lunch tastes blander than it should, so you salt more, sauce more, then light after to finish, which coats again. We call that loving strong flavours. It is loving a dulling and then chasing stronger hits to cut through the dull. The dose creates the need for punch and then takes credit for the punch.

Hear the objection that comes straight after, because it came from me.

"You speak of dulling, but I smoke when the mouth is clean. After brushing. After mints. I still want the taste then."

That observation is exact, and it proves the con rather than saves it. Once the nicotine trap has trained us, the Smokescreen will use any mouth as permission. Clean means light to crown it. Stale means light to clear it. After mints means light because the mint made the bite fresh. Before mints means light because the bite will need a mint. Opposite mouths, same hand. What kind of taste fits opposite mouths? A true taste has a shape. This one has no shape except timing.

Look at it straight. It does plenty TO you. It does nothing FOR you.

The Mild With Holes In It

I hear the next voice, because it spoke loudly in me.

"All right about payment and taste. But I smoke milds. Lights. Golds. I chose the softer kind. That must count for something."

I chose them too. The pack looked cleaner. The smoke felt thinner. The throat felt less hit. We took thinness for kindness. We told ourselves at least we had chosen the careful corner of the trap.

Hold one of those filters up to the light and look close. Around the paper, in a neat ring, sit tiny vent holes. You never put them there. They were put there for the machine that tests the dose. You never asked for them. You barely knew they were there. Most of us smoked them for years without seeing the ring.

Picture a mask meant to keep out bad air, with holes pricked round the edge. Through the holes, outside air rushes in and thins what comes through. On the bench it measures lower. On a human face, lips and fingers close over the holes without knowing, the chest pulls harder to get the feel it seeks, the draw runs longer, the puff runs deeper.

That is what we did with milds. We held the filter where the holes are. Lip over the ring. Finger over the ring. Drew without meaning to draw harder. Held a fraction longer. Took what the machine missed. Not in exactly the same way for every smoker — hands differ, lips differ, days differ — but the direction is the same. The human takes the dose the bench says he missed.

See the theatre in it. The white tip. The gold band. The word mild, light, smooth. The theatre says careful, modern, considered. The fingers do the unconsidered thing underneath — cover, pull, hold. The Smokescreen translates the theatre word into permission. Low number means I can light this one without thinking. Permission keeps us lighting.

I lived inside that permission and called it cutting sense. Same count. Same hours. Same tug at forty minutes. Same hand after class, paragraph, shops. Only the pack had changed its clothes. I showed the pack to doubters like a certificate. Look, mild. Look, lower. The certificate was written by a machine that never smoked like a man.

What The Machine Smoked And What You Smoked

The machine sips. You seek.

The machine takes a fixed puff through open vents and writes down a low number. It does not want anything. It does not remember the last sip. It does not sit through a morning of messages with a thin pull under the ribs. It sips air through open holes and reports thin.

You put your mouth where the vents are, close them without noticing, and puff like a smoker — because you are one — seeking the level the Nipper asks for. Lip seals the ring. Finger seals the ring. Chest asks. Throat remembers. You get the hit. The number on the packet stays low. The Smokescreen translates low number into safe permission, and permission keeps the chain unbroken.

Ask it of your own throat, not of the packet. If mild meant mild in the body, why did the day hold the same count? Why did the tug call at the same forty minutes? Why did the hand move just as fast after the class, the paragraph, the shops? If the dose were truly lighter where it matters, the clock would have stretched. The clock never stretched.

Follow the mild across one ordinary day and the sameness stares back. Morning mild on the step, harsh as ever on empty lungs. Mid-morning mild in the corridor, same two-minute tending. Midday mild with coffee, same dry mouth asking for water. Afternoon mild after the paragraph, same ash above the same page. Evening mild on the step with bags at the feet, same sour asking for a mint. The name on the pack says softer. The body keeps the same ledger.

Watch the puff itself, because the body tells the truth the packet hides. With a full-flavour tube you took a steady pull. With a vented tube you took a longer pull without deciding to, or a deeper pull, or a second pull close behind the first to top up. No one taught you to do it. The tug taught you. The chest pulls till the level feels right. If the sip is thinned by air, the chest pulls more sip. That is not weakness. That is a seeking body doing its job inside a tricked tube.

This does not make the unvented dose a friend or a clever choice. It makes the vent a theatre trick. Same nicotine trap. Softer curtain. To swap curtains and call it care is to be conned twice on the same stick — first when the stick stole the name of work and called itself payment, then when the filter stole the name of care and called itself mild.

Ask three plain questions of the mild and answer from what you lived.

Did the ring of holes teach your class, write your line, or drive your miles?

Did the low number on the side quiet the tug for one minute longer than the old number did?

Did the thinner feel leave your tongue cleaner, your coat fresher, your morning chest lighter in any way you could point to without squinting?

There is only one honest answer. The work was still yours. The clock was still the same. The mouth was still coated. Only the permission felt cleaner, and permission is not a body fact.

The Clock That Never Stretched

Take the packet in the palm and weigh what it really is. Not a flavour box. A day's supply box. Twenty tubes. Twenty feeds. Timed to the tug.

Morning two. Mid-morning three. Midday three with food and coffee. Afternoon four with work and waiting. Evening five with meal and step and nightcap air. Weekend a few more because hours stretch and tugs stack. The box empties on schedule because the body seeks on schedule, mild or not.

See how the box counts you while you count the box. You tap the side. Three left. You plan the shop run. You borrow one and note to repay. You keep a spare in the coat. You check the drawer before a trip. The dispenser has trained a manager. We call that habit. It is inventory for a need we did not have before the first experiment.

Hold the mild box beside the old box in memory and compare ledgers, not labels. Old box, twenty a day. Mild box, twenty a day. Old box, first lit within minutes of feet on floor. Mild box, same. Old box, last stubbed late with window open. Mild box, same. Old box, cough in the morning stairwell. Mild box, same. If the curtain were care, one line in that ledger would have moved. None moved.

I know the mind reaches for the exception. But stay with your own count, not with slogans. Name one week the mild stretched the clock past the hour. Name one day it cut the count without you thinking about count all day. Name one morning it left the tongue clean past noon. You cannot, because there is none to name. The filter lives at the light and dies on the tongue. What lives on is the same schedule and the same need for the next feed.

Look at the fingers that hold the vented tip. Yellow at the edge. Smell in the skin that soap lifts for an hour and then returns. Little burn on the coat pocket where an ember kissed. Those fingers covered the holes without knowing and pulled harder without deciding. They are not foolish fingers. They are seeking fingers, doing what any seeking body does when the sip is thinned — seek more sip. The fault was never in the fingers. The fault was in the ring that was put there for a machine and then hidden by lip.

Two Cons On One Stick

Put the two thefts side by side and the stick stands bare.

First theft. The stick stole the name of work. Taught class, found line, driven miles — all yours — signed by ash. The payment belonged to you before the match struck. The lift was already in the room.

Second theft. The filter stole the name of care. Vented sip, low number, soft word — all theatre — signed by permission. The care never existed in the body. The clock never moved.

Both names belong elsewhere. The payment belonged to you. The care never belonged to anyone. What remains when false names are lifted? A paper tube that does plenty to tongue, chest, coat, pocket, day, and nothing for class, line, miles, meal, or pause.

Watch tomorrow with those lifted names and the trick loses its polish. See the hand move after done work and say to yourself, there goes the tug, not the payment. See the lip cover the ring and say, there goes the seek, not the mild. See the flavours thin under haze and say, there goes the covering, not the taste. Three watchings, and the kindest excuse shrinks to its true size — a small pull mistaken for kindness.

Think of the paragraph you will write tomorrow. You will stare. You will cross out. You will find the verb. The lift will rise. The hand will move. In that second, remember whose lift it is. It is the writer's lift. It was there at school before any tube. It will be there when the tube is no longer signed. Let the writer keep his own bow.

Think of the class you will teach tomorrow. The door will click. The faces will lift. A joke will land. The tired-good feeling will come. The hand will move in the corridor. In that second, remember whose hour it is. It is the teacher's hour. No filter taught a single face. Let the teacher keep his own hour.

Think of the shops run tomorrow. The list. The queue. The bags on the step. The engine ticking. The hand will move before the bags are in. In that second, remember whose miles they were. They were the driver's miles. No vent drove a single yard. Let the driver keep his own miles.

Keep Your Ears Clear

This is the point where other voices get loud. A friend means well. An article means well. An old idea in your own head means well. Switch to milds. Keep the after-dinner one. Pay yourself with one after hard work. Save one for the pause. They speak as if they have watched your desk and your corridor. They have not. They speak from inside the same Smokescreen we are clearing.

You do not need a second argument laid over this one while this one is still working. Let one piece of clear seeing finish before you borrow another.

You have seen the payment was yours. You have seen the taste was covering. You have seen the mild was harder puffing for the same dose through covered holes. Let those three stand without a new voice painting over them.

5. IGNORE ANY ADVICE THAT CONFLICTS WITH THIS BOOK
Let this argument finish before you borrow another.

**SUMMARY**
- The lift after teaching, writing, or errands belonged to finished work, not to the dose lit after it.
- The pause steadied because stopping steadies, while the tube added lighting, watching, and ashing.
- Taste after food or coffee was covering, not flavour, and the most-dosed tongue tasted least.
- The mild filter measures lower on the bench because vents thin the sip, while lip and finger cover the vents in the hand.
- The daily count and the forty-minute clock never stretched, showing the body sought and found the same dose.
- Payment and care were names stolen by stick and filter, leaving a tube that acts on the day without acting for it.
