Chapter 3
WHAT DOES IT ACTUALLY DO FOR YOU

IN THIS CHAPTER — kitchen table, deep water, finished plate, back step, friends round the table

*Once you stop weighing what it costs against what it gives, you see there was nothing on the giving side to weigh.*

The List We Carry

You have seen something most smokers never look at straight. Your hand moves before your mind decides. Your midnight step needs mints and spray. A small tug gets translated by a large story. You carry the names for that now. The nicotine trap. The Nipper yapping to be fed. The Smokescreen turning the yap into need.

That seeing leaves a plain question, and I hear it because I asked it myself for years at my own kitchen table.

“All right. Perhaps I am caught. But surely it does something for me? Surely I get something back for the money and the cough and the hiding?”

It is an honest question. It is also the question that keeps the whole con standing. While you believe there is a cost on one side and a benefit on the other, you will go on weighing them. Harm against pleasure. Risk against comfort. Cough against calm. And weighing keeps you lighting, because weighing assumes there are two sides to weigh.

I am not asking you to weigh better. I am asking you to look at the other pan and see it is empty.

Take a pencil in your head and say your own reasons aloud, as I said mine. Mine read like yours will read.

“It calms me down.”

“It helps me think.”

“It tastes good.”

“It marks a pause.”

“It finishes a meal.”

“It keeps me company.”

“It rewards me after work done.”

Say them in your own raw words. Do not tidy them to sound sensible. Say what we all say in the kitchen and the car. The stick steadies me. I love the first draw. It is my little party. I need it with coffee, with a drink, after food, after work.

We all carry that list. For years I polished mine. I could argue each line like a lawyer. What I never did was check a single line against my own day.

That is what we do together here. Not a lecture. Not a scolding. A checking. You, with your memory of yesterday, and I, with mine, looking at three ordinary moments and asking one question of each. What did the paper tube add that was not already there?

The fact is, you already suspect the answer. You would not be reading if the list still convinced you. You remember too many doses that gave heaven for a minute and a sour mouth for an hour. You remember too many sticks smoked in rain and wind that you did not even want. You remember saying in your own plain words that the moment I light up I am in heaven, but I feel shitty afterwards, that whilst I love smoking I hate being a smoker. A benefit that behaves like that is not acting like a benefit anywhere else in your life.

So keep the list in front of you while we talk. We take it line by line, not to tease you, but to return each credit to its true owner.

The Hug That Would Not Let Go

Picture this, because it happened to us and we misnamed it kindness.

You are in deep water, out of your depth, arms flailing. A strong swimmer reaches you, puts his arms around you, holds your head up. You gasp with gratitude. You cling to him. You tell him you owe him your life.

What you do not see, because his face is so kind above you, is that it was his hand on your shoulder that pushed you in off the bank a minute before, when you were standing dry and breathing without thought.

Would you call that rescue? Would you thank him for the rest of your life and say you could not manage the river without him? Would you ask him to walk beside you in case you fall in again?

You would not. Once you saw the push, the hug would change its meaning completely. The arms that looked like rescue were the arms that made rescue necessary.

That is what a cigarette does, puff after puff. It puts its arms around you at the exact moment you feel edgy and low, and you thank it for holding you up. What you were never shown is that it put you in the water in the first place.

We stood dry before we started. Do you remember? Before the first experiment, before we learned to tolerate the burn, there was no hourly edginess asking for a paper tube. There was no hand reaching for a pocket at the kettle’s click. There was no flare needed to feel normal. We stood on the bank, eating without needing a finish, pausing without needing a prop, meeting friends without stepping out.

Then, puff by puff, we trained a need, and from that day on every dose has done two jobs at once. It has quieted for minutes the unease the previous dose left behind, and it has taken the credit for calming the day itself.

Listen to how we speak. “It calmed me after the argument.” Did it touch the argument? Are the papers on the desk moved? Is the sharp voice on the phone unsaid? “It helped me think.” Did it put a single thought in your head that a man without a dose could not have? “It keeps me company.” Did it say one kind word, or did it stand between you and the people you were with while you stepped out to feed it?

I am not mocking what you felt in that minute. That taken-care-of minute is real as a sensation. I felt it a thousand times. I would have sworn the dose had steadied me. The question is not whether the arms felt warm. The question is who pushed you in.

Ask it of your own yesterday, not of theory.

When the edginess rose before the dose, where had it come from? Did the bills, the boss, the traffic put a chemical tug under your ribs every forty minutes, or did the fading of the last dose put it there? When the dose quieted you for a few minutes, what had been quieted — the problem in your life, or the tug left by the last paper tube?

You know the honest shape. The problem sat untouched. The tug went quiet. Then the tug returned. That is not help. That is a hug that will not let go.

What Did It Put On The Plate?

Take food. This is where most of us keep our fondest line.

You finish a good plate. The taste is still in your mouth. Your body is satisfied. Your mind is easy for a moment. Then the hand moves. Tap. Light. Draw. And we say the dose rounds it off.

Did it? Be precise about your own mouth.

Food leaves flavour, warmth, fullness. What does smoke leave on top of flavour? Ash over taste. Bite over warmth. A sour film where richness was. Smokers who have eaten one evening without lighting notice the same thing. The meal lingered longer and cleaner without the tube. The plate did not need a finish. It was finished.

If the stick improved taste, why do we cough on the first puff of the morning on an empty throat? Why does the end of every stick taste bitter and hot and sucked too far down? Why do we remember only the lighting second and forget the stale tail? We say in our own plain words that the moment I light up I am in heaven, but I feel shitty afterwards. That heaven lives only at the lighting, and even that heaven is only the tug going quiet for a breath. It does not spread across the afternoon like satisfaction.

Think of the shape of a true pleasure. A good meal leaves you satisfied for hours. A good rest leaves you stronger for the afternoon. A laugh with friends warms the evening after. What does your heaven leave an hour later? Not satisfaction. A wish to repeat it. Not strength. The same tug, back again, asking for another dose.

I know the voice that answers back, because it spoke loudly in me.

“But I enjoy it! Are you calling me a liar? Do not tell me I feel nothing!”

I am not calling you a liar. I felt the flare myself. That lighting second, the bite and the settle, the ah-there — you feel what you feel. I am asking you to see what the flare is. It is not a gift laid on top of your day. It is a tug going quiet for a breath, then guaranteeing its return.

Put your yesterday beside that picture and be exact.

You ate at midday. You lit. Where was the taste of the food ten minutes later? Buried. You ate in the evening. You lit. Where was the easy fullness an hour later? Replaced by a thin edginess and a wish for another hit. You woke in the morning with a stale mouth. Did the thought of that morning dose come with pleasure beforehand, like the thought of breakfast, or with a nagging pull you wanted quieted?

You know the answer. We all know it.

The plate gave. The dose took. Food brought flavour, warmth, company to the table. Smoke brought ash, bite, and a bill to be paid within the hour. We confused the two because they arrived one after the other. Nearness in time is not sameness in cause. The sun does not rise because the cock crows, though the crow comes first every morning.

What did the tube add to the meal that the meal had not already given you? Nothing that stayed past the lighting. Nothing that did not turn to aftertaste.

What Did It Put Into The Pause?

Take the pause. This is where most of us keep our most reasonable line.

Work stops. Kettle clicks. You step out for five minutes. Shoulders drop. Noise dulls. You say the dose gave you the break.

Did it give you the stopping, the sitting, the air, the stretching of legs, the looking at sky? Or did it sneak a ride on a pause that would have rested you anyway?

A man without a dose who stops for five minutes gets the same stopping, the same air, the same distance from the desk — and he does not spend the five minutes tending a tube. We spent our breaks tending a dose. Lighting in wind. Sucking too fast. Watching the clock. Checking the pack. Stubbing half-burned when called back. The rest was the break. The fuss was the tube.

I lived that fuss for years. I told myself the back step was my thinking place. The truth in my own day was plainer. I did my thinking before the light and after the stub. During the stick I thought about the stick. Will it stay lit. Will the wind shred it. Will someone call me inside. Will this be the one that makes my chest rattle. That is not thinking. That is babysitting a paper tube while the pause runs out around you.

Look at your own break yesterday.

You stopped. You stretched. You looked out. Those three belonged to stopping, not to smoke. You coughed. You rushed the last third because the phone rang. You came back with a dry throat and a faint sourness and told yourself you felt rested. What rested you — the five minutes away from the screen, or the nicotine hit that quieted for minutes the tug the last hit had left?

Separate them in your head and the answer stands out clean.

The pause rests. The dose borrows. Air on the face rests. Legs moving rests. Eyes leaving the screen rests. The tube adds heat, hurry, and a timer ticking toward the next tug. It never gave you time. It charged you time and called the change rest.

Ask yourself why a pause without a dose feels thin at first. Is it because pauses need smoke, or because the tug is talking through the pause? A child pauses without a prop and finds the pause complete. A man who never started pauses without a prop and calls it lunch, tea, air. Only we hear a pause as an instruction to light. That instruction was not born with us. It was trained into us, puff by puff, until the Smokescreen could translate every natural stop in the day as proof of need.

Take the back step in your memory and remove the tube, leaving everything else. Step still there. Air still there. Five minutes still there. Sky still there. What is missing? Only the lighting, the ashing, the sucking, the stubbing. The quiet you love in that moment was the stopping. The hurry you hate in that moment was the dose.

Who Was Talking In The Laughter?

Take company. This is where most of us keep our warmest line.

A laugh round the table. Glasses down. Someone lights, someone offers, the circle glows. You say the stick belongs to that warmth.

Whose warmth is it? The night, the old joke, the friend you have not seen for months — did the paper tube put any of that in the room? Take the tube out in your head and leave everything else. Talk still there. Leisure still earned. Friend still talking. What is missing? Only the shared fuss of lighting and ashing and stepping out together. We mistook doing the same fuss at the same time for closeness. Closeness was the people. The stick was only ever sneaking a ride.

I hear the objection because I made it myself for years.

“You do not understand. It is sociable. It gives me something to do with my hands. It breaks the ice.”

I understand completely. I stood in those circles. I held the stick like a ticket that proved I belonged. The question I never asked until late was simple. Did the ticket buy the belonging, or did the belonging lend its glow to the ticket?

Look at your own evening.

You laughed before the light. You were already smiling when the hand moved. The story that made you laugh did not come out of the pack. The face you love across the table did not brighten because you lit. The dose arrived after the warmth started and left before the warmth ended, leaving ash and a need to slip out again soon. If the stick made company, why did you have to leave company to feed it on the step, in the doorway, by the bins, while the talk went on without you?

We tell ourselves we smoke together. Much of the time we smoke apart together, each tending his own tube in a loose ring, each watching his own ember, each stepping out at a different minute. True company does not ask you to leave the room to keep it. True company does not coat your mouth and ask you to chew a mint before you speak close.

And here is the tell that gives the whole list away. We attach the same reward to opposite moments. Cigarettes were my reward for — well, almost everything. Teaching a class. Finishing a story. Finishing a paragraph. Driving 500 miles. Driving to the grocery store. I heard a smoker put it in those exact words, and I recognised my own day in it. Morning dose. Work dose. Meal dose. Phone dose. Waiting dose. Bored dose. Busy dose.

What kind of helper is needed for opposite jobs? What calms you down and picks you up? What cures boredom and cures pressure? What belongs after a 500-mile drive and after a trip to the shop? A true helper has a shape. This one has no shape except timing. It arrives when the tug arrives and takes the name of whatever you were doing.

It was never the work that needed the pellet. The party was the dose, not the thing finished. You did the teaching, the writing, the driving. You brought the skill, the humour, the miles. The tube arrived after, put its arms around the tired minute, and took the bow.

Let me put three plain questions to you, and answer each from what you have lived, not what you were taught to say.

If the stick adds taste, why does food taste cleaner and linger longer when you do not bury it in smoke?

If the stick gives rest, why does the man resting beside you get the same stopping, the same air, the same distance from the desk, without lighting, coughing, or watching the clock?

If the stick gives company, what exactly did it say to your friends that your own mouth could not say?

There is only one honest answer to all three, and you feel it forming before you argue it away. It added nothing. It borrowed everything.

To You, Not For You

Now gather those three checkings into one picture and hold it still.

Food gave flavour. The dose laid ash on it. The pause gave rest. The dose filled it with fuss. People gave warmth. The dose stood beside the warmth and took the credit. In each case the good was already in the room before the hand moved. In each case the tube arrived late, quieted for minutes the tug left by the last tube, and signed its own name on someone else’s work.

The fact is, we were trained to read that signature as proof. Every time the tug went quiet we said, you see, it helps. Every time the day gave us something sweet we let the dose stand in the photograph. Puff by puff we handed it medals it never earned, until the Smokescreen could turn any tug into any need. Tired means light. Tense means light. Pleased means light. Finished means light. Waiting means light. The translation changed every hour. The tug stayed the same.

Do you see how kind this discovery is? If the dose truly added taste and rest and thought and friendship, leaving it would mean living thinner. You would have to mourn. But if the dose added nothing and borrowed everything, there is no thinning to fear. The meals, the pauses, the people remain exactly where they were, cleaner without ash, longer without hurry, closer without stepping out.

It never added. It only borrowed.

Read that again and let it sit without argument. It never added. It only borrowed. The sentence sounds small. It empties the whole ledger.

That is why the balance you kept for years cannot be struck. You were asked to weigh harm against benefit, risk against reward, cough against calm. You cannot weigh when one pan holds stones and the other holds shadows. Stones are real. Shadows are not. Your throat in the morning is real. Your wind on the stairs is real. Your pocket is real. Your evenings organised around windows and weather and witnesses are real. The benefit you set against them was a shadow cast by the tug going quiet for a minute and calling itself help.

I want you to hold the true ledger in your own words now, because words you speak yourself hold longer than words you read.

It does plenty TO you. It does nothing FOR you.

Say it slowly, the way you would say something you have checked yourself. It does plenty TO you — to your throat, to your wind, to your pocket, to your evenings, to your divided self on the cold step. It does nothing FOR you — not for your taste, for your rest, for your thought, for your friendship. Every FOR you were taught to list was a TO you mislabelled.

I know how strange that sounds after years of opposite teaching. I know the mind reaches for the exception. But stay with your own day, not with slogans. Name one hour the dose improved past the lighting minute. Name one problem it moved on the desk. Name one sentence it spoke to a friend. Name one meal it left cleaner. You cannot, because there is none to name. The flare lives at the light and dies on the tongue. What lives on is ash, aftertaste, and the next tug.

Once that reversal is seen, the weighing stops by itself. There is no decision to make between health and pleasure when pleasure was never on the table. There is only a con seen clearly, a hug recognised as a grip, a borrower asked to return medals that were never his.

3. BEGIN BY FEELING GREAT TO BE ESCAPING
There is no doom here, only freedom ahead.

I place this instruction at the exact moment the pan comes up empty, because an empty pan can look bleak if you stare at it alone. Do not stare at it alone. Lift your chest. If there was never anything on the giving side, there is no loss in seeing it. You are not being asked to mourn a friend. You have been shown the friend never gave you a thing. What lifts now is not only smoke. It is the weight of weighing itself, years of telling yourself you were weak for loving something harmful, years of telling yourself you must one day pay with misery to leave it.

Carry the sentence we earned today into every dose you watch from here on. When the hand moves, when the flare comes, when the mind says this one helps, this one tastes, this one belongs — answer with what you saw at the table, in your own calm voice.

It does plenty TO you. It does nothing FOR you.

**SUMMARY**
- The harm-against-pleasure balance assumed the dose gave something worth weighing, and your own day showed the giving side was empty.
- The arms that felt warm in the edgy minute made the edginess necessary by feeding the tug and taking credit for its brief quiet.
- Food carried flavour, pauses carried rest, people carried warmth, and the tube stood beside each and signed its own name.
- The flare you feel lives only at the lighting and leaves a sour mouth and a returning tug, not lasting satisfaction.
- Seeing the empty pan takes away the dread of leaving, and the talk ahead grows brighter because there is nothing real to miss.
