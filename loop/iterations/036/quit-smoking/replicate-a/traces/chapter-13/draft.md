Chapter 13
THE DAY RUNS STRAIGHT THROUGH

IN THIS CHAPTER
- Cold kitchen tiles, kettle steam and a sill with no ash on it
- The midday shop queue and the bright shelf behind the till
- Open air on the work break, hands with somewhere else to be
- Late laughter round a table with drinks

*There was never anything in the ordinary hour but you, and now you get to notice it.*

First Cup With Both Eyes Open

You wake and the hand moves half an inch toward where the pack sat. It does that because it did that for years, not because it knows something you do not.

We all lived that reach. Alarm, throat dry, feet not yet down before the fingers found paper. First drag on empty lungs while the kettle muttered. We called it waking. We called it starting. The mouth turned papery before the tea cooled and the chest felt narrower, and we called the narrowing a lift.

This morning put both feet on the cold floor and stand. Kettle on. Window cracked. Cold bites the face in a way that tells you you are awake before any sip tells you.

"I feel odd with nothing in my hand."

Of course you do for a second. The hand remembers managing — tap, shield, light — while the mind remembers checking gaps. Odd is not missing. Odd is the quiet where managing used to be.

Did the kettle ever need paper to boil? Did your tongue ever need sour to taste tea? Did the morning ever refuse to start until you narrowed it?

Drink it slow. Let taste sit on the tongue to its end. Notice the sill. No grey dust. No sour stub cupped in glass. No sprayed hair to wash before anyone hugs you. Wash the cup and smell the clean water after. That quick lift in the chest is yours reporting in. It was never the dose.

I stood at that same sink on my first morning and laughed at how loud the kettle sounded when nothing else was hissing beside it.

The Shop Queue At Midday

By midday you will have to buy something somewhere. Milk, bread, a paper. You will stand in line behind someone buying doses and hear the till tap and the foil crack.

We stood in that line a thousand times and did arithmetic without knowing it — have I enough for tonight, for the morning, for the drive. We weighed the palm for the box the way a man pats for keys. The box was a day's supply of hunger with a filter on it.

Stand there today and watch with clear eyes.

The man ahead steps aside to check his pockets twice. The girl at the till slides the bright packs forward without looking at them, the way she slides lottery tickets. No one looks soothed. They look administered to.

"But I always bought mine here. It feels strange to walk past."

Does the shelf give, or does it bill? Did it ever put milk in your fridge or money in your coat? If twenty years of paying never made the queue shorter, what would one more payment buy?

Walk past it the way you walk past shelves of things that are not yours. You do not argue with them. You do not price them. Buy your bread. Feel the bag light in the hand that used to carry smoke and matches and mint and shame in winter. That lightness is not absence. It is ownership.

I loved that walk the first time I did it — out of the shop with nothing to hide in the pocket and nobody to hide it from.

Air On Your Face At Break Time

The clock says break and the old voice says step out with paper to rest.

We obeyed that voice in rain and wind. We stood at the wall while talk ran on without us inside. We cupped heat against weather and called it pause, called it breath, called it rest, while the break shrank to managements — light, shield, relight — and the face came back smelling of sour.

Take your break where you want it. Take tea to the window. Take a turn round the block. Feel air move across the mouth without heat laid over it. Feel hands in pockets because they want warmth, not because they must guard anything.

"What do I do with my hands?"

Hold the mug with both. Put them round its heat. Stretch the fingers. Put them in your coat. They will learn in a day what they are for when they are not waiters. Hands were never smokers. You were never a smoker in your hands. You were a payer with busy fingers.

Did a pause ever need to be browned at the edge to count as pause? Did your shoulders ever need bite at the back of the tongue to drop? Did ten minutes ever fail to pass unless you powdered them?

Stay the full ten. Notice how long ten is when it is not cut into puffs. Notice talk does not need you to leave it to enjoy it. Notice work waits the same whether you come back tasting tea or tasting ash, except one of those mouths wants water and the other wants another.

The tug may brush past as an empty little nudge in the afternoon. Let it pass while you sip. It passes whether you argue or not. Each passing thins it. You do not need to mark it. You need only to keep drinking your tea and doing your work and walking to the window.

That is not endurance. That is noticing what is true.

When The Laugh Lifts

Evening will bring the test you already passed — the laugh round a late patio with drinks, a kind hand holding out paper.

We used to believe that scene owned us. Drink loosened the no. Company made refusing feel rude. We said one will not hurt because we wanted the night to keep shining and we had been taught where the shine lived.

Sit in that shine tonight and watch where it lives.

Sun low. Glasses sweat. Story halfway. Someone lights and steps half aside to manage in the wind while the punchline lands without them. You stay. You hear the end. You laugh with a clear mouth and the laugh does not thin.

"Just this once, to belong."

Did you ever belong because of paper, or in spite of stepping out from talk to feed it? Did drink ever need sour laid over it to be kind? If the night is warm and the company is kind, what would heat and bite add that is not already there?

You know the answer because you watched the farewell up close — yellow rim, grey powder, heat creeping to the lip, last third fouler than the first. No polished memory survives that look. The picture tries to polish itself and then remembers the ashtray.

When that picture floats by, do not debate it. Do not weigh. Answer from the chest with the short shout you own.

YIPPEE! I'M FREE!

Say it inside if the table is loud. Say it aloud if you can. Feel how different it sits from one won't hurt. One is a chain rattled to sound like music. The other is a door shut on a room you never need to rent again.

I still say it when drink and laughter lift and paper glints across the table, not because I am tempted, but because I like hearing the truth out loud.

And then get back to the story. Ask for the end you missed all those years at the wall. Taste your drink to its end. Stay till you want to go, not till the pack says you must. Go home with hair that smells of night, not of burning, and sleep with a mouth that does not ask for water at three.

That is the whole of these days. Kettle, queue, work, food, talk, sleep — running straight through without small managements cutting it into pieces. Each plain hour proves what the farewell proved. There is no gap because there is nothing to gap between.

If doubt knocks next week in your own voice — only at Christmas, only when hard — hear it labelled. Trap script. Tired record. You know its landlord. Answer each with the same shout and turn back to what you were doing. A stumble warns. It does not erase unless you let the old credit back in. Keep the belief shut and the days stay yours.

You stepped out once with open eyes. Now live the rooms you stepped into.

SUMMARY
- Morning tea stands complete with no paper beside it, and taste lingers to its end.
- The shop shelf bills and never gave, so walking past is walking past what was never yours.
- A break rests because it is a pause, not because heat was laid over it.
- Night, drink and company shine on their own, and staying inside the story proves it.
- A passing nudge is habit reaching for a shut door, and letting it pass thins it.
