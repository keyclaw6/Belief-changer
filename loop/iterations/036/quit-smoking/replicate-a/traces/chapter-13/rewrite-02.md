Chapter 13
THE DAY RUNS STRAIGHT THROUGH

IN THIS CHAPTER
Cold tiles and kettle steam. The shop queue and the bright shelf. A mug at the window. A plate sat to its end. The wheel and the errand run. A bill on the mat. Talk and drink at night. A plain mouth at bedtime. A week joining up.

*The ordinary hour was always yours, and now you get to live it as yours.*

Cold Tiles At Six

You wake and the hand lifts half an inch toward the bedside table before the eyes are open. It does that because it did that for years. Nothing more.

We all know that half-lift. We slept with paper within reach and woke with the mouth dry and the mind already counting. The day began with managing before it began with living.

You remember that first drag on empty lungs and know it never woke anything.

This morning put both feet down on the cold. Let the tiles bite. Stand a second in the chill and feel the blood come up to meet it. Kettle on. Tap running. Window cracked two fingers. The morning comes in with a thin edge that tells the skin it is morning before tea tells the tongue.

The hand feels odd with nothing to pinch and tap. Of course it does for a day or two. It was a waiter for years — tap, shield, light, carry, hide. Odd is not missing. Odd is the quiet where the waiting used to stand.

Did the kettle ever ask for paper to boil? Did your tongue ever ask for sour to taste tea? Did the light through the blind ever refuse to come until you narrowed your chest?

Pour slow. Cup in both hands. Drink while standing at the sill. Look at the sill. No grey dust in the groove. No glass with sour ends cupped in it. No faint brown rim on the white paint where smoke curled year after year. Wash the cup when you are done and smell the water run off it. That quick lift in the chest is yours reporting in. It was never lent by a tube.

Stand under the shower and let the water run hot on the face. Dress without the small pause on the step. Find keys, find coat, find bag, and notice no patting for a box before the door. The door opens to the same street and the street smells of wet stone and bins and bread from the van.

I stood at that same sink on my second day and laughed at how loud the kettle sounded when no hiss ran beside it.

We called that first dose waking. We called it starting. The mouth turned papery before the tea cooled and the shoulders stayed up and we called the tightness a lift. The fact sits plain now. The morning started without it because the morning never contained it.

Put the cup down. Make the bed. Run water on the face. Notice how long taste sits when no ash is laid over it. Tea to its end. Breath that stays sweet after swallowing. Hands that have nowhere to hide and no one to hide from.

The morning was yours. Paper only billed you for standing in it.

You remember that first drag on empty lungs with feet not yet on the floor, and you know it never woke anything.

Milk, Bread And The Shelf Behind The Till

By midday you will need something from a shop. Milk. Bread. A paper. You will stand in line and hear the till tap and the foil crack behind the glass.

We stood in that line a thousand times and did sums without writing them. Have I enough for tonight. Have I enough for the drive. Have I enough for the morning before the shop opens. We patted the pocket for the box the way a man pats for keys. The box was a day of hunger with a filter on it.

Stand there today and watch with clear eyes.

The man ahead steps aside to count coins twice and checks his coat twice more. The girl at the till slides the bright packs forward without looking at them, the way she slides tickets for a draw no one expects to win. No one at that shelf looks soothed. They look served for a few minutes and then sent back to wait.

“But I always bought mine here. It feels strange to walk past.”

Does the shelf give, or does it bill? Did it ever put milk in your fridge or money in your coat? If twenty years of paying never made the queue shorter, what would one more payment buy?

Buy your bread. Feel the bag light in the hand that used to carry smoke and matches and mint in winter. That lightness is not absence. That is ownership.

Take your change without counting for tomorrow's box. Push the door with your shoulder. Step to the pavement and hear the door fall shut behind talk and beeps. No turning back because the count was wrong. No second queue because the night will run longer than the box.

I loved that walk the first time I did it — out of the shop with nothing rattling in the pocket and no arithmetic running under talk.

We thought the shop was ours because we knew the price and the brand and the face that served. The shop knew our hand and not our name. It took and took and called the taking a habit. A habit you have to feed and hide and pay for while standing apart in the cold is not a habit. It is a tenancy, and you have left the room.

Walk past it the way you walk past shelves of things that are not yours. You do not argue with them. You do not price them. You do not prove anything by looking away hard. You look at your list and get what you came for.

The shelf never closed your hunger. It restocked it.

Ten Minutes With A Mug

The clock says break and the old voice says step out with paper to rest.

We obeyed that voice in rain and wind. We stood at the wall while talk ran on without us inside. We cupped heat against weather and called it pause, called it breath, called it rest, while the ten minutes shrank to small jobs — light, shield, relight — and the face came back tasting of sour.

Take your break where you want it. Take tea to the window. Take a turn round the block. Feel air move across the mouth with no heat laid over it. Feel hands in pockets because they want warmth, not because they must guard anything.

“What do I do with my hands?”

Hold the mug with both. Feel its heat through the clay. Stretch the fingers. Put them in your coat. They will learn in a day what they are for when they are not waiters. Hands were never smokers. Fingers were never in need. They were busy because you paid them to be busy.

Did a pause ever need to be browned at the edge to count as pause? Did your shoulders ever need bite at the back of the tongue to drop? Did ten minutes ever fail to pass unless you powdered them?

Stay the full ten. Notice how long ten is when it is not cut into puffs. Watch the street below. A bus sighs. Sparrows pick at the sill. Tea cools a shade and tastes deeper as it cools. Notice talk does not need you to leave it to enjoy it. Notice work waits the same whether you come back tasting tea or tasting ash, except one of those mouths wants water and the other wants another.

The tug may brush past as a small hollow nudge in the afternoon. Let it pass while you sip. It passes whether you argue or not. Each passing thins it. You do not need to mark it. You need only to keep drinking your tea and doing your work and walking to the window.

That is not endurance. That is noticing what is true.

We thought the break belonged to paper because paper always stood in the break. A dog that always stands in the doorway does not own the house.

The pause was yours. The ten minutes prove it.

The Plate Sitting To Its End

Lunch comes and dinner comes and the old rule says close the meal with ash laid over taste.

We lived by that rule without tasting it. We ate fast to get to the close. We pushed the plate half aside while still chewing and reached. We buried good flavour under hot paper and called the burial a finish. We said food was not food until smoke signed it.

Sit today to the end of the plate.

Eat at the table, not over the sink. Take the time the food asks. Notice the cut edge of bread. Notice butter melting into heat. Notice pepper at the back of the nose. Let each mouthful finish before the next begins. When the plate is empty, sit a minute with the taste still sitting on the tongue, uncovered and sitting there to its end.

“Food feels unfinished.”

Did food ever ask for covering, or did the nudge ask to be quieted? Did the cook put sour in the recipe? If taste was good for twenty minutes, why would it need three minutes of bite to complete it?

Drink water after. Rinse and swallow. Taste stays and then goes the way taste goes — slowly, kindly, leaving the mouth ready for talk. Take coffee if you take it. Drink it to its sediment and set the cup down. No throat clearing to shift the sour. No mint to lay over the lay-over. No stepping out while others still sit with spoons down and stories half told.

I ate a plain stew on my third night and sat dumb at how long thyme sat on the tongue when nothing came after to wipe it.

We credited paper with appetite and with digestion and with sociability after meals. Paper took credit for sitting still. Sitting still was yours. Hunger was yours. Fullness was yours. Company after the plates were cleared was yours.

The meal closes when you push back the chair. Nothing signs it.

The plate proves what the kettle proved. There was never anything on the table but you and food and people.

Wheel, Road And Errands

You will drive somewhere today. To the store. To work. To fetch someone. The car is a small room where we smoked the most because no one watched.

We lit before the key turned. We lit at the lights. We lit on the open stretch with the window cracked two inches and the wind pulling. We said the road needed company. We said traffic needed patience and paper gave it. We ashed into the tray and then into the wind and then brushed the knees and called the brushing normal.

Drive today with both hands for the wheel.

Key in. Mirror checked. Seat back one notch where you like it. Pull out without the small ceremony of tap and search. Notice how much room there is when the right hand stays down. Notice the wind when the window is shut because no ember needs feeding. Notice the lights change and the queue move and your shoulders stay down without a tube to hold them down.

“But the long drive always had smoke in it.”

Did the engine ever ask for it? Did the mile posts count puffs? If forty minutes felt long with paper cutting it into four parts, how does forty minutes feel straight through?

Run your errands in one run. Post office. Grocer. Tank filled. Bags in the boot. No stop to top up the box. No counting to see if the box will last the loop. No parking farther out to finish before going in. Fill the tank and pay and take the receipt with fingers that smell of nothing but cold coin. Walk in tasting the day you drove through, the air still plain in the mouth from start to end.

Listen to talk on the radio all the way through a story. Notice wipers clearing a thin rain. Notice hedges running past uncounted.

I drove fifty miles in my first week and kept waiting for the hour when I would weaken. The hour never came. What came was hedges and sun and talk on the radio and a mouth that stayed plain from start to end.

We thought miles needed marking. Miles need only driving. The hand that held paper every mile can hold the wheel every mile and the wheel is better company because it goes where you steer it.

The road was yours. The errands prove it.

When The Bill Sits On The Mat

Some day this week the phone will ring hard. The voice on the other end will be short. The bill will sit white on the mat. The old voice will say paper steadies.

We know that hour. Argument, phone down, hand already moving while the mind still shakes. We lit and drew and said the shoulders dropped. The papers sat untouched while the nudge quieted for minutes and then called again. We called the quiet calm and paid for the next storm with the same coin.

Meet the hour with both hands empty.

Read the bill twice standing still. Put it on the table. Pour water. Drink it. Sit with the phone in its cradle for a minute before you answer. Let the heat come and go across the face without laying heat over it. Answer plain. Write down what is asked. Do what can be done today and put the rest in a list for tomorrow. Pick up the pen and make the letters plain. Cross one line when done. Put the kettle on again and work beside its steam. The knot in the stomach loosens when the hand moves to work, not when the hand moves to the pocket.

“But I always coped with one.”

Did paper ever lift a paper off the desk? Did it ever answer the phone or mend the quarrel? If the dose truly steadied, would those who use it meet hard days easier than those who do not? Watch with clear eyes. They meet them worse — stepping out, checking gaps, tasting sour over worry, missing the help while at the wall.

If you ever feel ill or worried, speak to your clinician.

You do not need that clause for a nudge. A nudge is a hollow tug that passes in minutes while you sip and work and walk to the window. It is not you. It is habit reaching for a door that is shut. Smile at the reach. Turn back to the list. The tug quiets whether you feed it or not, but only one way ends it.

I took a hard call on day four with tea going cold beside me. My hand rose half an inch toward the old pocket and then stopped because there was nothing to do there. I wrote two names and two sums while the voice still talked. When I put the phone down the problem sat smaller because I had met it. No sour sat over it.

We thought pressure needed a valve of smoke. Pressure needs a hand that writes and a mouth that speaks plain and a chest that stays open to cold air from the window.

The problem was never solved by lighting. The hour proves it.

Staying Inside The Story

Evening will put talk and drink in front of you and the old picture will float by.

We used to believe that scene owned us. Drink loosened the no. Company made refusing feel rude. We said one would not hurt because we wanted the night to keep shining and we had been taught where the shine lived.

Sit in the shine tonight and watch where it lives.

Table set. Glasses sweat. Story halfway. You stay while someone steps half aside to manage in the wind and misses the punchline. You hear the end. You laugh with a clear mouth and the laugh does not thin.

You remember that late patio with drinks and know the shine was never paper.

“Just this once, to belong.”

Did you ever belong because of paper, or in spite of stepping out from talk to feed it? Did drink ever need sour laid over it to be kind? If the night is warm and the company is kind, what would heat and bite add that is not already there?

You know because you watched the farewell up close — yellow rim, grey powder, heat creeping to the lip, last third fouler than the first. No polished memory survives that look. The picture tries to polish itself and then remembers the dish.

Ask for the part you missed all those years at the wall. Let the teller start again because you want to hear it, not because you need time. Taste your drink to its last sip. Feel the chair solid under you. No checking the pocket under the table. No adding sums to laughter.

When that picture floats by, do not debate it. Do not weigh. Answer from the chest with the short shout you own.

YIPPEE! I’M FREE!

Say it inside if the table is loud. Say it aloud in the kitchen if you can. Feel how different it sits from one won’t hurt. One is talk the trap lent you. The other is what you saw with both eyes.

I still say it when laughter lifts and paper glints across a table, not because I am tempted, but because I like hearing the truth out loud.

And then get back to the story. Ask for the end you missed all those years at the wall. Taste your drink to its end. Stay till you want to go, not till the box says you must. Go home with hair that smells of night, not of burning, and sleep with a mouth that does not ask for water at three.

That is the whole proof of these nights. Kettle, queue, work, food, talk, sleep — running straight through without small managements cutting it into pieces. Each plain hour proves what the farewell proved. There is no gap because there is nothing to gap between.

The story was yours. Staying proves it.

Sleep Through And The Next Morning

Night is the last room we feared. We said sleep needed a last dose to come. We said the morning would punish us if we did not pay at night.

We smoked that last one on the step with the cold coming up through shoes and called it settling. The mouth went sour before the head touched the pillow. We woke at three wanting water and called the thirst normal. We woke grey and called the grey tiredness.

Go to bed tonight with a plain mouth.

Brush the teeth slowly. Rinse twice. Notice the paste sharp and then gone. Put the glass down. Lie with the window cracked. Feel the night come in thin and cold across the forehead. Pull the blanket to the chin. Let the day run back without counting — kettle, till, mug, plate, wheel, bill, talk. Each ran without paper beside it. Each ran longer and tasted longer because nothing cut it.

“I will lie awake wanting.”

Will wanting keep you awake, or will watching for wanting keep you awake? Did the dose ever bring sleep, or did it bring sour that sleep had to climb over? If twenty years of last ones never made mornings bright, what would one more last one buy?

Sleep comes the way it comes to those who never paid for it — in waves, with turns, with dreams that clear by morning. If you wake at three, drink water and lie back. The hollow may brush past. Let it pass while you breathe. It passes in minutes whether you argue or not. Turn the pillow. Think of bread in the bag and tea in the cup and the wheel under both hands. Sleep returns because the body wants sleep, not smoke.

I slept broken the second night and woke clear the third and understood the broken night was not punishment. It was the body settling its books after years of false entries.

Wake when you wake. Feet to tiles again. Kettle again. The sill still plain. The cup still yours. Pull the curtain and see the same light without grey in it. The second morning does not ask more than the first. It asks the same — stand, pour, drink, go. Each morning lays its proof beside the last until doubt has nowhere to lodge.

The night was yours. Sleep proves it.

The Week Joining Up

Days join. Tuesday proves Monday was not luck. Thursday proves Tuesday was not mood. Next Monday proves the week was not a trick of a calm spell.

We waited for the test that would break us — the wedding, the funeral, the deadline, the holiday. We thought ordinary days were easy and special days would own us. The trap taught us to fear the calendar because fear kept the box in the pocket just in case.

Live the calendar straight through.

Monday shop. Tuesday drive. Wednesday bill. Thursday talk. Friday late table. Saturday errands with bags in the boot. Sunday roast with taste sitting to its end. Wash up and put plates away and sit with tea while light goes. Each day the same plain proof in different clothes. No day asks for paper because no day ever contained paper. Days contained work and food and people and weather, and paper stood beside them billing.

If doubt knocks next week in your own voice — only at Christmas, only when hard — hear it labelled. Trap talk. Tired lines. You know who lets those rooms. Answer each with the same shout and turn back to what you were doing. A stumble warns. It does not wipe you unless you let the old credit back in. Keep the belief shut and the days stay yours.

You stepped out once with open eyes. Now live the rooms you stepped into.

I met a mate in my second week who lit while still talking and reached while still thinking of something else. I watched his hand move before his mind chose and felt no pang, only recognition. We all lived that reach. I pitied the managing, not the man. Warm to him, harsh to what held him. He asked if I missed it and I told him the truth as I had seen it — yellow, sour, narrowing — and he laughed and said I sounded like a convert. I sounded like someone who had looked at last.

Did his dose make his story funnier? Did the wind that stole his light add to his evening? Did stepping out prove his pleasure or interrupt it?

Ask those questions with your eyes open and the answers come back plain. His evening ran on without him while he fed. Yours runs straight through while you stay. You do not need to preach or to linger by the wall. Say what you saw once, kindly, and turn back to the table where the story waits for its end.

Say what you know when the thought floats by.

YIPPEE! I’M FREE!

Say it the tenth time with the same lift as the first. Not to push anything away. To report what the kettle and the till and the mug and the plate and the wheel and the bill and the table and the bed have all proved — there was never anything to keep.

That is the hand-off. Not a manual. Not a set of steps to memorise. Days lived once with both eyes open, proving in the body what the mind already settled.

SUMMARY
- Morning tea stands complete with no paper beside it, and taste sits to its end.
- The shop shelf bills and never gave, so walking past is walking past what was never yours.
- A break rests because it is a pause, not because heat was laid over it.
- Food closes when the chair pushes back, not when ash is laid over flavour.
- The wheel turns the same with both hands on it, and miles pass straight through.
- A hard hour meets work and plain talk, and its problem sits untouched by smoke.
- Night talk shines on its own, and staying inside the story proves it.
- Sleep comes to a plain mouth and mornings repeat the same proof.
- Days join into weeks, and each plain week hands the next its proof.
