Chapter 2
ARE YOU REALLY CHOOSING THIS

**IN THIS CHAPTER** — the garage step after midnight, the Febreeze bottle and the mints, the hand that lights before you decide, the second called heaven

*You did not choose to need cigarettes; you were drawn into something that took the choosing away.*

Good. You are still reading with an open mind, and you are still smoking as you were. Keep it that way while we talk. I do not want you distracted by missing. I want you to watch yourself clearly for a few pages.

I ask this because I need you to see one thing before we touch anything else. Not health. Not money. Not stress or taste or habit. Something more basic. Whether you are actually choosing this at all.

You say you choose it. I said the same for years. Let us look at what choosing looks like in your own day.

The Smoker Who Hides

Come with me to an ordinary night you know too well.

The house is quiet. Your partner is already in bed. The kids are asleep. You told everyone at dinner you were cutting back. You meant it when you said it. Then the tug comes, that familiar nudge under the ribs, and you find yourself saying you just need to check the car. Or take the rubbish out. Or get a breath of air on the garage step.

It is cold. You close the door softly behind you so it does not click. You cup the light so it does not flare at the window. You smoke quickly, listening for footsteps. You grind the end under your shoe and stare at it for a second too long. Then the second job starts, the job no chooser should ever need. You chew a mint. You wash your hands. You spray Febreeze the hell out of your coat and wave the air with your hand like a guilty teenager. You stand a moment in the cold to let the smell lift off you. Then you slip back inside, smile at nothing, and sit down as if you never left.

We have all done that walk. I have stood on that cold step myself, doing the arithmetic of wind direction and who might open a window upstairs. There is no contempt in me as I describe you, because there was no meanness in you as you did it. You were kind at dinner. You are kind now. You hid because you did not want a lecture or a worried look. You hid because you love those people inside.

But answer me honestly, in your own head, and let the answer sit.

When in your life have you ever hidden to do something you freely chose and purely enjoyed? You do not hide a cup of tea. You do not hide a good book. You do not Febreeze a walk in the park.

A man who is free to eat cake does not eat it in the garage.

That hiding is not a quirk of manners. It is evidence. Choice does not need an alibi. Only a trap makes a decent person sneak out of his own house to feed a paper tube and sneak back in pretending nothing happened.

Look at what that night cost you, not in lungs or years, but in plain self-respect. For a few minutes you lived divided. One self at the table saying I will cut back. Another self on the step making sure no one knows. You were not lying because you are a liar. You were lying because you were caught. You slipped inside the nicotine trap years ago without noticing the door close, and now you live half inside and half outside it.

If this were choice, there would be one of you, whole, smoking in the light and enjoying it. There are two of you, and one of them is ashamed on a cold step. That division tells the truth your mouth is still afraid to say.

The Hand That Moves First

There is another moment I want you to watch, because it happens almost every hour and you have trained yourself not to notice.

The phone goes down after a sharp exchange. Or the meal ends. Or the car stops at the lights. Or the kettle clicks. And before you have decided anything, your hand is already moving. Pocket. Pack. Tap. Light. It is done before thought arrives. Thought comes after to sign the paperwork: oh, I need one, I deserve one, just this one.

Watch that order. Hand first. Reason second.

I know the voice that answers back, because it spoke loudly in me.

“Nobody holds a cigarette to my head. I buy them myself. I like the feel of lighting up. Do not tell me I am not choosing.”

I hear you. I am not calling you weak or stupid. We were not foolish when we started. We were young and curious and surrounded by smokers, and the first puffs tasted foul and made us cough. No child puffs and thinks, this is delicious. We learned to tolerate it until we claimed we liked it. That is not choice on full information. That is being conned on phoney information, then calling the con a preference.

Let me put three plain questions to you, and answer each with what you have actually lived, not what you wish were true.

If you are choosing, why does your hand move before you choose?

If you are free, why do you smoke the ones you do not even want, the ones in rain, in wind, outside hospitals and stations, smoked too fast and tasting of nothing, just to stop the nudge?

If this is pleasure, why do you have to apologise for it with mints and spray and an open window?

You know the honest answers. The hand moves first because something is pulling it. You smoke the unwanted ones because the pull does not ask whether you are enjoying yourself. You apologise because some part of you already knows you are not acting as a free man acts.

It is the other way around from what you tell yourself. You do not light because you decided. You decide because you lit.

The Second You Call Heaven

Now I will speak for that lighting second, because I do not want you to feel I deny what you feel.

There is a second, just as the smoke bites and settles, when you think, ah, there. That is it. Heaven. Home. Taken care of. I know that second. Every smoker knows it. The shoulders drop. The noise dulls. The hand stops twitching.

I am not mocking that feeling and I am not saying you invent it. You feel what you feel. The taken-care-of feeling arrives and you credit the cigarette, in that moment, completely.

Hold that second under a lamp for a moment longer.

How long does your heaven last? Be precise. Not the story about it. The actual sensation. Ten puffs? The length of standing on the step? By the time you wash your hands and sit back down, where is it? By the time the aftertaste coats your mouth and you feel shitty for hiding, what is left of it? You love lighting and hate being a smoker in the same evening. Both are true. That should tell you something vital.

The enjoyment you defend lives only at the moment of lighting. It does not spread across your day like a gift. It does not fix the phone call that upset you. It does not clear the papers on the desk. It does not make you braver or kinder or calmer in any lasting way. It flares and fades, and leaves you exactly where you were, plus a sour mouth and a secret.

Ask yourself: what kind of choice is it that gives heaven for a minute and shame for an hour, and still gets chosen twenty times a day? A free person choosing pleasure would not sign that bargain. A trapped person would, because he is not weighing pleasure at all. He is answering a tug.

Choice would not need a hiding place. That is the verdict of your own garage step.

The Two Hooks That Hold You

So what holds you, if not choice and not pleasure?

Think of it as two hooks working together, both small enough to miss until you name them. I name them now only in passing, so you have words for what you already feel. We will pull each one apart properly as we go.

The first hook is physical and almost laughably slight. After a little while without a dose, your body nudges you. It is never pain. It is never drama. It is an empty, slightly restless, slightly edgy little tug that feels almost like hunger and almost like boredom and almost like nothing at all. Non-smokers feel that kind of edginess all day and think nothing of it. In you it has been trained to mean one thing: light up.

That little physical itch is the voice of the Nipper, a tiny creature that lives only on its next dose and dies quickly without it. He is not strong. He is not clever. He is a starving nipper at your heel, yapping to be fed. Every dose feeds him for a few minutes, then he yaps again. You never satisfy him. You only quiet him briefly and guarantee his next yap.

The second hook is the one that really keeps you. It is the cloud of stories around the yap that tells you what the yap means. It tells you the dose steadies your nerves, marks your break, rewards your work, keeps you company. It tells you stopping would leave a hole. It tells you this one is special, this moment needs it, you choose it. Those stories are the Smokescreen, the thick belief-cloud that turns a trivial tug into a need and a need into an identity.

Do you see how the two work as a pair? The Nipper tugs. The Smokescreen translates. Tug says empty. Translation says you need a cigarette to cope, to enjoy, to think. You light. The tug quiets for minutes. The translation takes the credit. Then the tug returns, and the translation says, you see, you need it again.

We all breathed that cloud in for years. Our parents smoked. Our friends smoked. Films taught us that smoke means thought and calm and grown company. Shops taught us that a pack is a friend in the pocket. That is brainwashing, plain and simple, and I use the word without insult. To be brainwashed is not to be stupid. It is to be exposed, repeatedly, to a con until the con feels like common sense. We were conned, not weak. We were chained by a story, not by character.

Once you see the pair, the mystery of your own behaviour clears. You are not smoking because you decided after fair thought that smoke in your lungs improves food, work, friendship and rest. You are smoking because a small tug demands a dose and a large story tells you the dose is pleasure and help. The story borrows the relief of the tug quieting for a minute and calls it proof.

And a trap that can make you hide, move your hand before you think, and call a minute of quiet heaven is not a habit. It is slavery with good manners.

I want you to feel the weight of that without dread, because there is daylight in it. If choice were real, you would need effort to overturn it. If choice was removed by a con, you need only to see the con. Seeing is what we are doing together, calmly, while you smoke as before. What was taken by misunderstanding can be returned by understanding. That is the way out of the nicotine trap, and it starts the moment you stop defending the bars as if you built them.

Let me leave you with the picture we started with, because pictures hold what arguments only point at.

Tonight you will step out again, perhaps. Cold air. Soft door. Cupped light. Watch yourself from a step behind as if you were watching a friend you love. Watch the quick drag, the listening ear, the mint, the spray, the careful face as you come back in. Then ask the kind question, not the cruel one.

Would a person who is choosing need to do any of that?

You know he would not.

2. DON'T STOP OR CUT DOWN YET
Smoke as normal until your last cigarette.

**SUMMARY**
- Hiding to smoke proves the opposite of choice; no freely chosen pleasure needs mints and spray.
- Your hand moves to the pack before your mind decides, then invents a reason after.
- The heaven you feel lives only in the lighting second, then turns to aftertaste and shame.
- A small physical tug and a large story about what it means keep you lighting, not free decision.
- Seeing the trap clearly is what returns choice, not fighting yourself.
