Chapter 2
ARE YOU REALLY CHOOSING THIS

*You did not choose to need cigarettes; you were drawn into something that took the choosing away.*

Good. You are still reading with an open mind, and you are still smoking as you were. Keep it that way while we talk. I do not want you distracted by missing. I want you to watch yourself clearly for a few pages.

I ask this because I need you to see one thing before we touch anything else. Not health. Not money. Not stress or taste or habit. Something more basic. Whether you are actually choosing this at all.

You say you choose it. I said the same for years. Let us look at what choosing looks like in your own day.

The Smoker Who Hides

Come with me to an ordinary night you know too well.

The house is quiet. Your partner is already in bed. The kids are asleep. You told everyone at dinner you were cutting back. You meant it when you said it. Then the tug comes, that familiar nudge under the ribs, and you find yourself saying you just need to check the car. Or take the rubbish out. Or get a breath of air on the garage step.

It is cold. You close the door softly behind you so it does not click. You cup the light so it does not flare at the window. You smoke quickly, listening for footsteps. You grind the end under your shoe and stare at it for a second too long. Then the second job starts, the job no chooser should ever need. You chew a mint. You wash your hands. You spray Febreeze the hell out of your coat and wave the air with your hand like a guilty teenager. You stand a moment in the cold to let the smell lift off you. Then you slip back inside, smile at nothing, and sit down as if you never left.

We have all done that walk. I have stood on that cold step myself, doing the arithmetic of wind direction and who might open a window upstairs. I have held the butt low by my leg so the glow would not show over the fence. I have breathed into my own sleeve to check myself before I went back in, the way a boy checks. There is no contempt in me as I describe you, because there was no meanness in you as you did it. You were kind at dinner. You are kind now. You hid because you did not want a lecture or a worried look. You hid because you love those people inside.

But answer me honestly, in your own head, and let the answer sit.

When in your life have you ever hidden to do something you freely chose and purely enjoyed? You do not hide a cup of tea. You do not hide a good book. You do not Febreeze a walk in the park.

A man who is free to eat cake does not eat it in the garage.

That hiding is not a quirk of manners. It is evidence. Choice does not need an alibi. Only a trap makes a decent person sneak out of his own house to feed a paper tube and sneak back in pretending nothing happened.

Look at what that night cost you, not in lungs or years, but in plain self-respect. For a few minutes you lived divided. One self at the table saying I will cut back. Another self on the step making sure no one knows. You were not lying because you are a liar. You were lying because you were caught. You slipped inside the nicotine trap years ago without noticing the door close, and now you live half inside and half outside it.

If this were choice, there would be one of you, whole, smoking in the light and enjoying it. There are two of you, and one of them is ashamed on a cold step. That division tells the truth your mouth is still afraid to say.

And it is not only the midnight step. It is the office doorway in the rain while the non-smokers stay warm inside. It is the restaurant table where you watch the clock because you know you will have to excuse yourself between courses. It is the family car where you wind the window down two inches and hold the stick out in the wind and call it fresh air. It is the hotel window opened wide on a winter morning, the bathroom fan switched on, the little performance of spraying and chewing and airing. We tell ourselves these are courtesies. Some of them are courtesies. But underneath the courtesy is a different feeling you know well. The feeling of being managed by something small.

A free pleasure does not organise your evening around windows and weather and witnesses. A free pleasure does not make you lie to people you love about a five-minute errand. Ask yourself what else in your life makes you behave like that. Not the food you love. Not the friends you love. Not the music or the game or the walk you love. Nothing you truly choose makes you sneak.

What Free Choice Would Look Like

Let us be precise about choosing, because the word has gone soft from overuse.

When you choose something you enjoy, there is a simple shape to it. You think of it with pleasure beforehand. You do it with attention while it happens. You finish it without apology after. You would do it in front of people whose opinion matters to you. You do not need to explain it away.

Think of a proper pudding you love. You do not stand in an alley to eat it. You do not tell your wife you have stopped eating pudding and then creep downstairs at midnight to eat it over the sink. You do not chew mints afterwards so no one knows you had pudding. You have it, you enjoy it, you are done. If someone offers you more, you can take it or leave it and you feel the same about yourself either way.

Now put your smoking beside that picture and be honest about the difference.

Do you think of the garage cigarette beforehand with pleasure, or with a nagging pull you want quieted? Do you smoke it with attention, tasting each draw, or quickly, hungrily, half annoyed at the wind and half listening for the door? Do you finish it without apology, or begin at once the mint and the spray and the hand-wash and the careful face?

You know the answer. We all know it.

I know the voice that answers back, because it spoke loudly in me for years.

“I pay for them myself. Nobody forces me. I like holding it. I like the first draw. Do not tell me I am a prisoner.”

I hear you. I am not calling you foolish. I am asking you to look at the shape of your own behaviour and compare it to every other free choice you make. The fact is, we use the word choice to protect ourselves from an uncomfortable feeling. If we say we choose, we do not have to admit we feel stuck. The word is a blanket we pull over the cold step.

Let me ask you the question that pulled the blanket off for me.

If you wanted to stop eating strawberries, would you need a book to show you how? If you truly enjoyed your hobby of evening walks, would you sit reading a book about how to stop going for walks? The mere fact you are holding this book proves something your mouth still argues with. A free chooser does not look for a way out.

You are here because some part of you already senses the truth. You are not choosing in the way you choose your food or your friends or your films. You are answering something. There is a difference, and your hiding knows the difference even when your lips defend the word.

The Hand That Moves First

There is another moment I want you to watch, because it happens almost every hour and you have trained yourself not to notice.

The phone goes down after a sharp exchange. Or the meal ends. Or the car stops at the lights. Or the kettle clicks. And before you have decided anything, your hand is already moving. Pocket. Pack. Tap. Light. It is done before thought arrives. Thought comes after to sign the paperwork: oh, I need one, I deserve one, just this one.

Watch that order. Hand first. Reason second.

Yesterday you smoked cigarettes you did not want. You know you did. The one outside the station in the wind that tasted of nothing and burned too fast. The one in the rain with water running off the end. The one you lit because the bus was late and then stamped out half-smoked when the bus came. The one you lit off the end of the last one without tasting it. Tell me, what chooser lights something he does not want and does not taste and then defends it as pleasure?

We smokers have a strange habit of counting only the good ones and forgetting the other fifteen. We remember the evening stick with a drink and forget the morning cough-stick smoked on an empty throat. We remember the break in the sun and forget the doorway in the sleet. If smoking were choice, we would smoke only the good ones. We smoke all of them, good and bad, wanted and unwanted, tasty and foul, because the pull does not ask whether you are enjoying yourself.

And notice how quickly the mind invents a story after the hand has moved. If the phone call was tense, we say it was for nerves. If the meal just ended, we say it was to round it off. If we are waiting, we say it was for boredom. If we are with friends, we say it was for company. The story changes every time but the movement is always the same. Pocket. Pack. Tap. Light.

Let me put three plain questions to you, and answer each with what you have actually lived, not what you wish were true.

If you are choosing, why does your hand move before you choose?

If you are free, why do you smoke the ones you do not even want, the ones in rain, in wind, outside hospitals and stations, smoked too fast and tasting of nothing, just to stop the nudge?

If this is pleasure, why do you have to apologise for it with mints and spray and an open window?

You know the honest answers. The hand moves first because something is pulling it. You smoke the unwanted ones because the pull does not ask whether you are enjoying yourself. You apologise because some part of you already knows you are not acting as a free man acts.

It is the other way around from what you tell yourself. You do not light because you decided. You decide because you lit.

Stay with that reversal for a moment, because it changes everything. We thought the thought caused the act. I feel like one, so I have one. The truth in your own day is the opposite. The hand acts, the mouth explains. You are not thinking your way to a cigarette. You are smoking your way to a thought that excuses it. Once you see that order, you cannot unsee it. Tomorrow morning watch it happen in real time. Kettle clicks. Hand moves. Mind says, ah, morning stick, my little moment. The moment did not call the hand. The hand called the moment.

The Second You Call Heaven

Now I will speak for that lighting second, because I do not want you to feel I deny what you feel.

There is a second, just as the smoke bites and settles, when you think, ah, there. That is it. Heaven. Home. Taken care of. I know that second. Every smoker knows it. The shoulders drop. The noise dulls. The hand stops twitching. If anything stressful happens in my life, a cigarette takes care of it — that is how we put it to ourselves, and in that second we mean it completely.

I am not mocking that feeling and I am not saying you invent it. You feel what you feel. The taken-care-of feeling arrives and you credit the cigarette, in that moment, completely. I felt it a thousand times. I would have sworn in that second that the dose had steadied me.

Hold that second under a lamp for a moment longer.

How long does your heaven last? Be precise. Not the story about it. The actual sensation. Ten puffs? The length of standing on the step? By the time you wash your hands and sit back down, where is it? By the time the aftertaste coats your mouth and you feel shitty for hiding, what is left of it? You love lighting and hate being a smoker in the same evening. Both are true. I have heard smokers say it in their own plain words, that the moment I light up I am in heaven, but I feel shitty afterwards, that whilst I love smoking I hate being a smoker. That should tell you something vital.

The enjoyment you defend lives only at the moment of lighting. It does not spread across your day like a gift. It does not fix the phone call that upset you. It does not clear the papers on the desk. It does not make you braver or kinder or calmer in any lasting way. It flares and fades, and leaves you exactly where you were, plus a sour mouth and a secret.

Think of the shape of a true pleasure. A good meal leaves you satisfied for hours. A good rest leaves you stronger for the afternoon. A laugh with friends warms the evening after. What does your heaven leave an hour later? Not satisfaction. A desire to repeat it. Not strength. The same tug, back again, asking for another dose. A pleasure that leaves you emptier than it found you is not acting like pleasure in any other part of your life.

Ask yourself: what kind of choice is it that gives heaven for a minute and shame for an hour, and still gets chosen twenty times a day? A free person choosing pleasure would not sign that bargain. A trapped person would, because he is not weighing pleasure at all. He is answering a tug.

I want you to notice, too, how narrow the heaven is. It is never the whole cigarette. It is the first two draws, the bite and the settle, the ah-there. The middle of the stick is smoked without thought. The end is bitter and hot and sucked too far down. If you smoke twenty a day, you get twenty brief flares of quiet and twenty long tails of ash and aftertaste and faint self-disgust. We remember the flare and forget the tail. Choice would weigh the whole. The trap teaches you to remember only the flare.

Choice would not need a hiding place. That is the verdict of your own garage step.

You Were Conned, Not Weak

At this point a worried voice often speaks up. I heard it in myself.

“Are you saying I am weak? Are you saying I have no will of my own?”

No. I am saying the opposite, and I say it flatly because you need to hear it clean. You were conned, not weak.

Think back to the start, honestly. No one begins smoking because a clear-headed adult weighs the taste and decides smoke in the lungs is delicious. We began because friends did, because it looked grown, because someone offered on a night out, because curiosity and belonging pulled harder than sense. The first puffs tasted foul. We coughed. Our eyes watered. Our throat burned. That was your body telling the truth on day one. No child thinks, this is lovely, I choose this.

We learned to tolerate it until we claimed we liked it. Puff by puff, we trained the throat not to cough and the mind to call tolerance pleasure. That is not choice on full information. That is being conned on phoney information, then calling the con a preference.

We breathed in more than smoke in those years. We breathed in the stories. Our parents smoked on steps and in cars. Our friends smoked to mark a break. Films taught us that smoke means thought and calm and grown company. Shops taught us that a pack is a friend in the pocket. Everywhere we looked, someone lit up after food, after work, after love, after argument, until the mind joined the dots that were never joined in nature. That is brainwashing, plain and simple, and I use the word without insult. To be brainwashed is not to be stupid. It is to be exposed, repeatedly, to a con until the con feels like common sense. We were conned, not weak. We were chained by a story, not by character.

Do you see how kind that truth is? If you were weak, you would need to become strong to get out. If you were conned, you need only to see the con. Weakness would require effort and struggle. A con requires only clear eyes. I am not asking you to become a different person. I am asking you to see what was done to the person you already are.

We smokers share the same history here. We did not choose to need. We chose a single experiment on false evidence, and the experiment removed the choosing. It happens to bright people and steady people and careful people every day. The trap does not check your character before it closes. It closes on anyone who feeds it twice.

That is why there is no shame in admitting you feel stuck. Shame belongs to a choice freely made and badly used. You did not freely make this. You were a young person, or a stressed person, or a lonely person on a night out, who accepted a paper tube on the assurance of everyone around you that it helps and pleases. You trusted the assurance. The assurance was false. A person deceived is not a person disgraced.

And look how the con keeps itself alive now. It tells you that admitting the trap would prove you are weak, so you defend the bars to prove you are strong. I did that for years. I polished my bars. I called my prison my cottage. I told non-smokers they did not understand pleasure. All the while my hand moved before my mind, and my midnight step grew colder. The strongest thing I ever did was not defending the trap. It was saying, plainly, I was conned, and I can see it now.

The Two Hooks That Hold You

So what holds you, if not choice and not pleasure?

Think of it as two hooks working together, both small enough to miss until you name them. I name them now only in passing, so you have words for what you already feel. We will pull each one apart properly as we go.

The first hook is physical and almost laughably slight. After a little while without a dose, your body nudges you. It is never pain. It is never drama. It is an empty, slightly restless, slightly edgy little tug that feels almost like hunger and almost like boredom and almost like nothing at all. Non-smokers feel that kind of edginess all day and think nothing of it. In you it has been trained to mean one thing: light up.

That little physical itch is the voice of the Nipper, a tiny creature that lives only on its next dose and dies quickly without it. He is not strong. He is not clever. He is a starving nipper at your heel, yapping to be fed. Every dose feeds him for a few minutes, then he yaps again. You never satisfy him. You only quiet him briefly and guarantee his next yap.

The second hook is the one that really keeps you. It is the cloud of stories around the yap that tells you what the yap means. It tells you the dose steadies your nerves, marks your break, rewards your work, keeps you company. It tells you stopping would leave a hole. It tells you this one is special, this moment needs it, you choose it. Those stories are the Smokescreen, the thick belief-cloud that turns a trivial tug into a need and a need into an identity.

Do you see how the two work as a pair? The Nipper tugs. The Smokescreen translates. Tug says empty. Translation says you need a cigarette to cope, to enjoy, to think. You light. The tug quiets for minutes. The translation takes the credit. Then the tug returns, and the translation says, you see, you need it again.

We all breathed that cloud in for years. Our parents smoked. Our friends smoked. Films taught us that smoke means thought and calm and grown company. Shops taught us that a pack is a friend in the pocket. That is brainwashing, plain and simple, and I use the word without insult. To be brainwashed is not to be stupid. It is to be exposed, repeatedly, to a con until the con feels like common sense. We were conned, not weak. We were chained by a story, not by character.

Once you see the pair, the mystery of your own behaviour clears. You are not smoking because you decided after fair thought that smoke in your lungs improves food, work, friendship and rest. You are smoking because a small tug demands a dose and a large story tells you the dose is pleasure and help. The story borrows the relief of the tug quieting for a minute and calls it proof.

And a trap that can make you hide, move your hand before you think, and call a minute of quiet heaven is not a habit. It is slavery with good manners.

I want you to feel the weight of that without dread, because there is daylight in it. If choice were real, you would need effort to overturn it. If choice was removed by a con, you need only to see the con. Seeing is what we are doing together, calmly, while you smoke as before. What was taken by misunderstanding can be returned by understanding. That is the way out of the nicotine trap, and it starts the moment you stop defending the bars as if you built them.

Keep smoking while we talk. Watch the tug when it comes today. Notice the translation that follows it. Notice the hand. Notice the heaven that flares and dies. You do not have to fight any of it. You only have to see it. A con seen clearly loses its power to pose as choice.

Let me leave you with the picture we started with, because pictures hold what arguments only point at.

Tonight you will step out again, perhaps. Cold air. Soft door. Cupped light. Watch yourself from a step behind as if you were watching a friend you love. Watch the quick drag, the listening ear, the mint, the spray, the careful face as you come back in. Then ask the kind question, not the cruel one.

Would a person who is choosing need to do any of that?

You know he would not.

2. DON'T STOP OR CUT DOWN YET
Smoke as normal until your last cigarette.

**SUMMARY**
- Hiding to smoke proves the opposite of choice; no freely chosen pleasure needs mints and spray.
- Your hand moves to the pack before your mind decides, then invents a reason after.
- The heaven you feel lives only in the lighting second, then turns to aftertaste and shame.
- A small physical tug and a large story about what it means keep you lighting, not free decision.
- Seeing the trap clearly is what returns choice, not fighting yourself.
