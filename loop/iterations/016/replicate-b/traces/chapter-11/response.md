Chapter 11
MYTHS ON A PLATE — Q&A

IN THIS CHAPTER — the drawer you call control, the calendar square marked tomorrow, the bottle labelled natural, the switchboard with one cord still left in, the three o'clock rollercoaster

*Keeping a little of the trap keeps all of the trap.*

### THE POCKET YOU KEEP CHECKING

We have taken the big stories apart. The fuel story — gone. The treat story — gone. The sweetest evening — taken back. The fear itself — seen for what it is, two ropes held by one hand.

What is left is smaller, cleverer, and more persistent because it does not sound like a story at all. It sounds like common sense. It sounds like you being reasonable.

You will hear it after this book is closed, in your own voice, as if you thought of it yourself:

Just this once. Just the special ones. Just not in the house. Just the natural kind. Just tomorrow. Just a little less.

I want to take each of those lines now, while we are together and warm, and answer it once, flat and completely, so that when it comes back later it arrives already labelled. Not as your reasoning. As the con speaking through you. The con that fed you is the con that wants to keep one cord plugged in, because one cord is enough to keep the whole appliance humming.

Picture the primitive wanting system as a switchboard with many power cords. You have pulled most of them this week - the fuel cord, the reward cord, the sociability cord. The board still looks lit. One cord left in the wall will keep the light on. That is why cutting down fails. That is why keeping the special ones fails. It is not about the amount. It is about whether any cord remains to translate that faint empty feeling as feed me.

And that empty feeling is real. I am not telling you you will feel nothing. The mild dip after you stop feeding BAD SUGAR can be there for a few days, strongest at the usual times - the desk at three, the sofa, the aisle. It is small, it is brief, and it starves if you do not feed it. Keep that in mind while we answer the myths, because none of them removes the dip. They only make you feed it again.

If you have diabetes, take prescribed medication, have a history of disordered eating, or a medical condition affected by what you eat, talk to your clinician before changing what you eat — I want you free with your body looked after too.

Let us do this quickly, as you would want it done. Your own words first, then the answer. No hedging. No second chance for the trap to sound reasonable.

### "JUST A TREAT - ONCE A WEEK IS FINE. I CAN CUT DOWN AND KEEP THE SPECIAL ONES."

You say it as moderation. Hear what it does inside your head.

The moment you make BAD SUGAR forbidden-but-sometimes, you make it precious. Intermittent access drives bingeing - in the lab the schedule itself drives the volume, not hunger. Allowed all the time, animals do not show the same pattern; allowed now and then, they binge. We see the same human echo every January and every Monday: cut down, be good, then one bite becomes the box. You do not get free by making the dose more valuable between hits. You get free by unplugging every cord.

And the special ones are the trap in its smartest clothes. Keep one exception and you keep the whole belief intact: that BAD SUGAR still has something you need. One exception means you are still sitting on the rollercoaster you pay to ride and then pay to get off. You lift, you drop, you blame the afternoon and feed that drop with the very thing that created it. Cutting down is not stepping off. It is staying on and calling the dips discipline.

Ask yourself: if there is no real benefit to doing it often, what point is there in doing it occasionally?

### "I DON'T KEEP IT IN THE HOUSE SO I'M IN CONTROL."

This is the control myth, and it feels true because the kitchen looks tidy.

We did this, you and I. We cleared the cupboard. We felt virtuous at the shop. Then we thought about the shop all week. We told friends I don't keep it in the house with a little pride, and we ate twice as much when we were out - at the office, at the cinema, at the petrol station - because the rule had made every encounter a decision and every decision a reminder.

Not keeping it in the house does not remove the want. It relocates it. The cue still fires - the wrapper, the smell, the 3 p.m. hum of the vending machine - and the wanting circuit fires before hunger does. You white-knuckle the kitchen and binge at the party and call that control.

Real control is not moving the packets. It is seeing there is nothing in the packet you want. A person who does not want to drink floor cleaner does not need to hide the bottle.

Do you want a life where you must police your own cupboards for the rest of your years, or a life where the cupboard is simply a cupboard?

### "NATURAL SUGAR IS FINE - I NEED SUGAR FOR MY BRAIN. IT'S HARMLESS."

This one borrows a lab coat.

The fact is your body makes its own glucose from protein and glycerol. You do not need to import BAD SUGAR to think, to work, to be kind, to be quick. That sugar is not an essential import. It is an engineered add-on that spikes and crashes and bypasses the appetite controls that real food respects. Calling it natural because it came from a plant last year is like calling a cigarette natural because tobacco grows in a field.

And harmless is a word the trap taught you while you were on the gentlest part of the slope. The fact is a high intake of added sugar carries a real bodily load at population level - liver overload, pressure, inflammation, the slow blunting that makes larger family-size portions feel normal and wrappers more exciting before the bite. In your mouth the mechanism is simple and mechanical: free sugars become plaque acid and plaque acid decays teeth. I tell you that not to frighten you into change - do not change from fear - but to place harm where it belongs: not in some distant future, but in the small daily taxes you already pay and already feel - the sticky mouth, the heavy eyelids, the fast drop after the short lift.

There is also honest disagreement among scientists about whether the word addictive fits BAD SUGAR in the clinical sense, or whether the binge pattern reflects the way we restrict and then allow it. Both camps grant the same thing we have lived: under an on-off schedule, people binge. That honesty is not a reason to stay trapped while experts debate. It is a reason to stop arguing about the label and look at your own evenings: I just can't stop once I start. The schedule explains that. Remove the schedule entirely and the behaviour has nowhere to go.

You do not need BAD SUGAR for your brain. Your brain has been making its own clean supply since before you ever tasted the first sweet. It will go on doing so, wonderfully well, when you stop importing the spike.

### "I'LL QUIT TOMORROW. I'LL WEAN OFF SLOWLY. I'LL SWITCH TO THE LESS-BAD VERSION."

Three costumes, one cord.

Tomorrow is the trap's favourite calendar square because tomorrow never arrives. You picture a kinder future self with more time, less stress, a perfect Monday, and you lend today’s dose to that future self as if it were payment for later freedom. But each today dose creates tomorrow’s low and tomorrow’s excuse. The lifetime cost is not abstract - it is every single day forever doing something you already know gives you nothing and makes you feel worse an hour later. If it were harmless you would not be reading me now.

Weaning off slowly sounds responsible. The evidence for weaning is genuinely split - short experiments in young people point opposite ways - and that split matters: it means there is no clean, proven gentle ramp that makes the mild hump smaller. What we do know, from the same schedule work that shows intermittent access drives bingeing, is that spacing hits makes each hit more exciting and more memorably relieving. Weaning keeps you in the anticipation loop longer, thinking about BAD SUGAR between every smaller hit. Stopping is a cliff jump. You can jump off as long as you do not fall more than a few metres is not a sensible plan. You step off. Totality is kinder than tapering because it ends the negotiation.

Switching to the less-bad version is the same negotiation in new packaging. The sweet still teaches your mouth and your brain that the answer to the empty feeling is a sweet hit, and the cue still fires at the wrapper. You keep the translation alive: dip means dose. While that translation lives, freedom does not.

The other way around is simpler and truer: the lift you feel after the less-bad version is not proof you needed it. It is proof you were already a little low from the last one.

### WHY ONE CORD IS ENOUGH

You can see now why I put the switchboard image before the answers.

Each myth is an attempt to leave one cord plugged in and call that balance. Keep the weekend treat and you keep the belief that weekends need it. Keep the house rule and you keep the belief that you are someone who must be guarded from packets. Keep the natural label and you keep the belief that your body depends on imported sweetness. Keep tomorrow and you keep the belief that today is not the day you get to be free.

And the trap loves a single cord because the wanting circuit is built to be cue-driven. A sight or smell can fire want before hunger arrives, before you have decided anything. That hyper-reactivity to wrappers and smells is not character. It is a learned cue habit that fades when you stop feeding the story that gives it meaning. While any myth remains, the cue has a story to land on. When all cords are pulled, the cue fires into empty air and goes quiet.

There is also the rollercoaster you already know by heart. The office at three: the hum, the sticky mouth, the heavy lids, the quick sweet that lifts you for twenty minutes and then drops you faster than you climbed, which you then read as proof you need another lift. The rollercoaster does not prove you need sugar to get through the afternoon. It proves you are on a ride you pay to ride and then pay again to get off. Step off and the ground is level. Level is not deprivation. Level is normal, and normal is where your energy has been waiting to settle all along.

I will not promise you that you will never feel a flicker again. Some people feel little or nothing; some feel that mild empty feeling for a few days, strongest at the habitual times. That is not a sentence and it is not failure. It is the Imp dying, and it is brief. Do not give that mild, passing feeling a grand name. Give it the name that makes you glad: shrinking.

### NO ROUTE BACK

We have closed the routes before you reach for them, on purpose.

When the thought comes - and it will come, quietly, in your own dialect, perhaps tonight by the shop, perhaps on a tired Thursday - "just a treat, once a week is fine" - you will hear it for what it is: the con trying to plug one cord back in.

When it says "I don't keep it in the house, so I'm in control," you will hear tidy-cupboard pride and know control is not moving packets but wanting none.

When it says "I need it for my brain, the natural kind is harmless," you will hear the lab coat it borrowed and answer with your own body, which has made its own glucose beautifully since your first day and will go on doing so.

When it says "tomorrow, slowly, the less-bad version," you will hear the calendar square and know tomorrow is how the trap keeps today.

There is nothing clever left to try on this plate. Every clever way is the trap trying to keep itself alive by sounding like you being sensible.

You are not sensible to keep a little of the poison that makes you feel worse and calls it a treat. You are sensible to see there is no benefit left to moderate.

We fell into this by brainwashing, not by weakness. We stay in it by believing one small myth at a time. Pull the last cord with the others. Do not leave a back door open and call it balance.

You do not need to argue harder. You need to see there is nothing left to argue for.

### SUMMARY

* Cutting down and keeping special ones does not tame the trap; the intermittent pattern makes each hit more precious and the next binge more likely.
* Not keeping packets in the house does not create control; it moves the wanting to the next shop or occasion while the cue still fires.
* BAD SUGAR is not an essential import for my brain; my body makes the glucose it needs and the natural label does not remove the spike and crash.
* Harmless is not true; the daily taxes I already feel are the bodily load, and experts may disagree on the addiction label while agreeing the binge pattern lives in the on-off schedule.
* Quitting tomorrow, weaning slowly, or switching to a less-bad version all keep one belief cord plugged in, which keeps the whole trap alive; totality is the kinder and only stable freedom.
* The mild, brief empty feeling in the next days is not proof I need a fix; it is a small, starving feeling that passes and is glad to be felt shrinking.
