Chapter 6
HUNGER YOU CAN TRUST

IN THIS CHAPTER — the kitchen at midday, an empty table waiting, a bowl of warm food, the first mouthful after true hunger, the quiet pause where enough arrives

*Hunger is not an emergency — it is a friendly signal, and real food answers it completely.*

### WHEN HUNGER STOPPED FEELING LIKE HUNGER

We grazed so long we forgot what hunger is.

We ate before we were hungry. We ate because the clock said twelve, because the packet was open, because the afternoon felt flat, because the sweet called from the cupboard. We taught our bodies to live with a low hum of fullness that never cleared and never satisfied. Then we called that hum normal and decided hunger itself must be the problem — an emergency that needs a quick and sweet fix, right now, or something bad will happen.

I know that flutter. The slight hollow that makes you think, I need something now. The voice that says if I do not have something quick my energy will crash, I will not cope, I will get shaky, irritable, useless. We obeyed it with a dose of BAD SUGAR and the flutter went quiet for a little while, so we believed BAD SUGAR had solved it.

It had not solved it. It had fed it.

A child who eats when hungry does not feel emergency. Hunger arrives as a clean, mild, friendly signal — a little empty, a little interested, a thought that food would be marvellous. It is not panic. It is not pain. It is your instinct knocking politely to say the kitchen is open. You answer with real food, you feel the quiet click of satisfaction, and you forget about food again until the knock returns. We were built for that rhythm. We replaced it with grazing and then blamed our hunger for being broken.

You have already seen two great cords pulled. You know BAD SUGAR was not fuel and you know it was not a genuine pleasure or treat. That leaves the question that sits quietly behind them: if I no longer feed that loop, what will I actually eat, and will I be left hungry?

Watch and see.

### A PLATE WHEN YOU ARE HUNGRY

I want you to remember a simple meal. Not a feast, not a plan, not a perfect plate from a book. A simple meal eaten when you were truly hungry.

It is midday. The kitchen is not dressed up. A bowl is warm in your hands. Perhaps it is soup with vegetables still bright, or chicken and rice, or beans and greens with olive oil and a little salt, or fruit that actually smells like fruit. There is colour. There is steam. You have waited until your body asked.

You take the first mouthful. Notice what happens before thought gets in.

There is taste. Real taste, not the white blast that wipes everything else out. The tongue wakes up. Salt is salt. Sweet is gentle and stays where it belongs — in the pear, in the pepper, in the milk. You chew and the food is interesting all the way through the bite. Your body leans in a little. That is instinct doing its job. It knows this.

We eat with BAD SUGAR whispering and we eat without it and the difference is unmistakable once you watch for it.

With BAD SUGAR in the day, eating never quite starts or stops. You finish and ten minutes later the talk starts again — something sweet, something more. The mouth is sticky. The mind is already hunting. You call it hunger but it is not hunger. It is the faint tug from the last dose, still asking to be fed.

Without BAD SUGAR running the show, eating has a shape. Hunger arrives, friendly and clear. You eat. Somewhere in the middle there is a pause you had forgotten. It is not will, it is not counting, it is not being good. It is a quiet arrival — enough. The bowl is half done and the body says, that is satisfying, thank you. You stop and you feel clean and steady, not stuffed and not deprived. An hour later you are not thinking about food at all. You are thinking about your afternoon.

I sat with a plate like that after years of grazing and was startled by how marvellous ordinary food had become. The vegetables were not deprivation. They were not a punishment for being healthy. They were what I had wanted while I was eating packets that kept me chasing. The satisfaction was not thin. It was full. I had not given anything sweet away. I had recovered the ability to taste sweet where it lives.

You once told yourself, I would have something quick and sweet but now know that will only give a short term lift which will inevitably be followed by a fast drop. You were right about the roller coaster. What you had not yet felt is the other side of that sentence: when you answer real hunger with real food, there is no lift and crash. There is a steady return to comfortable. The quick fix promises energy and delivers restlessness. Real food does not promise energy. It gives it, quietly, without the bill.

Is your mouth watering a little now thinking of that bowl? That is not weakness. That is your instinct recognising its favourite.

This is the positive authority you already own. Not a rule, not a slogan, not a new identity to build. Your body knows hunger. Your instinct knows satisfaction. Real food is the answer they have been waiting to give. You do not need to learn them. You need to let them speak again by eating when hungry and stopping when satisfied, while letting BAD SUGAR sit out.

We changed nothing else in our lives to feel this. We simply watched eating happen without the confectionery gatecrasher in the middle of it.

### YOUR BODY ALREADY KNOWS HOW

Perhaps a worry speaks now, in the reasonable voice we use when we are stalling.

But do I not need sugar? Is it not fuel for my brain?

The fact is your body makes its own glucose from protein and glycerol, so going without BAD SUGAR is not deprivation. Your body has its own supply and it is good at using it. This does not mean you should live on meat or cut out wholesome carbs — goodness, no — fruit and vegetables and plain whole foods stay on the menu and are welcome. It means you are not removing something essential when you let BAD SUGAR go. You are removing something engineered to spike and crash. If you have diabetes, take prescribed medication, have a history of eating distress, or a condition affected by what you eat, talk to your clinician before changing what you eat — this book is not medical advice.

Hear what that means in ordinary life. Vegetables are not the price you pay to be free. They are not the sad alternative. They are what your appetite is for. BAD SUGAR had been blocking the taste the way a loud radio blocks conversation. Turn it off and you do not need to strain to hear. The conversation returns.

And what about the first few days without the grazing? Another quiet fear: will I feel awful?

The fact is any faint, unsettled feeling peaks in two to five days then passes — a brief hump, not a lifelong fight — and for many it is mild or barely noticed at all. Human reports on this are still preliminary and varied, so I will not promise you will feel nothing and I will not promise you will feel strong discomfort. Most people describe it as a light restlessness, a little empty, a little more aware of the cupboard, and then it fades if not refed. That is not a sentence. If you feed that faint tug with another dose, it resets. If you let real food and a little time do their work, it starves. It is already dying.

you have nothing to lose and everything to gain

That line is true here in the most practical way. You lose no nourishment, no energy, no essential fuel. You gain the return of clean hunger and the satisfaction that actually ends.

### THE TASTE THAT WAS WAITING

There is a small revelation that comes when you let a real meal do its work, and I want you to watch for it because it will prove everything to you better than any argument I can make.

It is the second mouthful that tells you.

The first mouthful is polite. The second mouthful is honest. With BAD SUGAR in your system, the second mouthful is already a little less bright than the first, and the fourth is chase, not taste. With real food after real hunger, the second mouthful is more interesting than the first. The flavour opens. You taste the edge of salt, the sweetness that lives naturally in the food, the way your mouth waters in the right places. Your instinct leans in. You are not willing yourself to enjoy vegetables. You are enjoying them because you can finally taste them.

That is what was meant by the vegetables-are-deprivation lie. We told ourselves plain food was bleak so we could justify the packet. What was bleak was eating sweet food that never satisfied and left us hunting again. Plain food eaten when hungry is not bleak. It is favourite. It is what your body asks for when the radio is off.

You will notice another thing in that quiet pause where enough arrives. The mind is not arguing. No tug-of-war about whether you may have more, whether you should, whether you have been good enough to deserve it. Enough is not a decision you make. It is a signal you receive. You put the spoon down, you feel relief and steadiness, and food leaves your thoughts without a scold. That steadiness lasts. It does not spike and hunt an hour later. You carry it into your afternoon and you get on with your work and your people without the hunt.

We lived opposite for years and called it normal. We thought hunger was an emergency that needed a quick fix. We thought vegetables were what you endure while wishing for the real treat. Watch one ordinary meal eaten when hungry — not according to the clock, not to be good — and the belief reverses on its own. Hunger is a friendly knock. Real food is the answer. The packet was the interruption.

There is nothing to white-knuckle here. You are not keeping BAD SUGAR out by effort. You are letting your appetite in by attention. In the next chapter I will show you why the faint tug felt so big and the belief that fed it felt so true — and why seeing that clearly makes everything you have just felt with that bowl make perfect sense.

For now, live this one piece and let it prove itself.

Eat when you are hungry. Taste what is there. Notice when enough arrives and rejoice that you can feel it again. That is freedom arriving as relief, not as sacrifice, and it was yours all along.

### SUMMARY

* Hunger is not an emergency that needs a quick fix; it is a mild, friendly signal that comes and goes, and answering it with real food brings a clean, lasting satisfaction.
* Real food eaten when hungry tastes marvellous and carries its own gentle sweetness; vegetables and plain whole foods are not deprivation when the radio of BAD SUGAR is off.
* Eating has a natural shape — hunger, eating, the quiet arrival of enough, then forgetting about food — and you can feel that shape return in one ordinary meal.
* Your body makes its own glucose, so letting BAD SUGAR go does not deprive you; any faint unsettled feeling is brief, peaks in a few days then passes, and is often mild.
