Chapter 3
WHAT DOES IT DO FOR YOU?

IN THIS CHAPTER — the fridge door with a taped menu, the dinner table with an uninvited guest who eats off your plate, the bright supermarket aisle humming like a pharmacy, the small whisper behind the ear and the voice that sells it

*BAD SUGAR does nothing for you — it does everything to you.*

### THE WRONG QUESTION

We have been asking the wrong question.

We ask, does BAD SUGAR do me more harm than good? We weigh the harm on one side — weight, teeth, slump, money, shame — and we weigh what we call the good on the other — taste, comfort, energy, celebration — and we try to balance the scales. Harm versus good. Cost versus benefit. That is the question the trap taught you to ask, and as long as you ask it, you keep your hand in the bowl.

The fact is, the question is a con. It smuggles in the answer it wants. It assumes there is a good.

I want you to change the axis. Not, does it do more harm than good. But, what good is there at all?

Imagine someone offered you a pill that did plenty TO your body while claiming to do something FOR your mind. You would not ask whether the harm outweighs the benefit. You would ask a simpler thing: what benefit? Show me one. Just one, clean, that belongs to the pill and not to everything else that happened around it.

That is the test I want you to bring to BAD SUGAR. Not the ledger. The search. We will lay every reason you have ever given on the table, not to judge you — we all gave the same reasons — but to make each reason justify itself. You will see very quickly that the behaviour is doing plenty TO you, and nothing FOR you.

I know this is hard to accept at first. Of course it is. If you believed BAD SUGAR gave you nothing, you would not be reaching for it. That belief — that it is a genuine pleasure, a treat, a lift, a reward — is not a free thought. It is the work of years of kindness and advertising and habit, all repeating the same line until it sounded like your own voice. We need to hear that voice clearly so we can hear whose it really is.

### THE MENU ON THE FRIDGE

Let me put your menu on the fridge, in your own words, exactly as you say it to yourself and to me. I have heard each of these a thousand times, in kitchens and offices and cinemas. There is no shame in them. They are the lines the trap wrote for us. Read them and recognise your own handwriting.

"I need sugar to get me through / to get me through the afternoon"

"It gives me energy / helps me concentrate / it's fuel"

"I deserve a treat / I've been good this week / treat when you deserve one"

"It's my reward / celebrating, rewarding myself, going to the cinema, etc with chocolate"

"It's sociable / it makes the meal / the occasion wouldn't be the same"

"Just a treat / once a week is fine / I can cut down / keep the special ones"

"I don't keep it in the house so I'm in control"

"Natural sugar is fine / I need sugar for my brain / it's harmless"

"I'll quit tomorrow / I'll wean off slowly / I'll switch to the less-bad version"

"I just can't stop once I start / one bite becomes the box"

Ten lines. One voice.

We have fed ourselves each of them after the dose, to explain why we took it. Not one of them was our reason before we ever took the first sweet as a child. Did you fear a low brain fuel when you had your first chocolate? Did you need sociability at age six? You did not. You took it because it was offered as love and you believed the offer. The explanations came later, fitted neatly backwards.

You will notice I am not going to argue all ten in this chapter. Each has its own owner, its own ground to be stripped clean — the fuel idea lives on your desk at three o'clock, the treat idea lives in the cinema queue and at the birthday table. We will meet each on its own ground and reassign the credit to where it actually belongs. That way nothing is hurried and nothing is left half-believed.

Only one of those lines belongs to this room, because it is the only one that can keep the other nine looking respectable. The sociable line. The idea that BAD SUGAR makes the meal. If that one falls, the rest wobble, because if it cannot even make a meal better — the simplest, most human pleasure — what can it do for you?

So let me ask you plainly, as one friend to another. If BAD SUGAR were truly doing something FOR you, you could name it now. One clean benefit that is yours only when BAD SUGAR is present, that you cannot have without it, that outweighs nothing because it does not need to. Not a relief from a craving. Not a pause in restlessness. A genuine gain. Can you name it?

You say, I like the taste. Then why do we add sweetness to things that already taste of something? The apple is sweet. The roast chicken is savoury and satisfying. We do not add sugar because the meal lacks taste; we add it and then claim it supplied the taste. You say, it helps me relax. Then why do you feel more on edge an hour after? You say, it is social. Then why does the same table with the same people, laughing at the same story, feel just as warm when BAD SUGAR is not on it?

Hold that discomfort lightly. It is not failure. It is the old belief being touched for the first time.

What you have called a benefit is not a benefit at all. We have a phrase for the thing we think we would lose if we stopped. For years we have whispered it to ourselves without ever testing it. We tell ourselves BAD SUGAR is a genuine pleasure or treat. Say it inside your head and hear how finished it sounds, how it closes the question. That little dyad — pleasure or treat — has been doing all the work. It names the illusion as if it were a fact. Once you see that, you can test it. Is it a genuine pleasure or treat, or is it the relief of feeding a whisper that should not have been there in the first place?

That phrase is now your test. Every time the thought arises — go on, you deserve it; it makes this moment — replace the story with the test: is this a genuine pleasure or treat, or is the Imp being fed? You will hear the answer very quickly. The pleasure was the moment. The treat was the company. BAD SUGAR was only the tax you paid to get to them.

### THE GATECRASHER AT THE TABLE

Let me show you the guest who stole the credit.

Picture a table you love. It might be Sunday lunch, it might be a friend's kitchen at midnight when everyone is too tired to be polite, it might be a film with the lights down and the bowl in the middle. The food is good. The people are warm. The story is rolling. You are hungry, you eat together, you laugh. This is one of the best things a human body does.

Now watch who walks in without knocking.

BAD SUGAR is a terrible gatecrasher. Carbs are inherently lonely — a plain cracker, a white roll, a bowl of pasta alone on a plate — they call for company because on their own they are thin, dry, and forgettable. They invite butter, then cheese, then something sweet to finish, then a moreish sweet to finish the sweet. One taste calls the next like a party that never ends. BAD SUGAR does not wait to be invited. It arrives with a bottle it brought for itself, drinks it on your carpet, puts its feet on your sofa, and then thanks you for a wonderful evening as if it hosted it.

We all know this guest. You cook a proper meal, real food, seasoned, satisfying. You eat until that quiet pause arrives — the clean, settled signal that says enough. Then the gatecrasher leans in and whispers, you need something sweet to finish. Not because the meal was not a treat. Because the gatecrasher needs a reason to be let in.

And because he is loud and sweet and bright-packaged, you remember him. You say, the meal was lovely because we had pudding. You say, the occasion wouldn't be the same without it.

I want you to isolate the variable, as you would in any honest experiment.

Think of the same evening, same people, same film, same laughter — but the bowl in the middle is not sweets. It is salty popcorn you actually like, or fresh berries, or nothing at all and your hands are empty and warm around a mug. Does the film become bleak? Does the love in the room leak out through the floor? Or does everything that made it sweet remain — the story, the nearness, the jokes — and the sticky mouth and the heavy eyelids after the sweets are simply missing?

It is the other way around. The evening was sweet. BAD SUGAR was sneaking a ride.

I lived this like you did. I once believed a family film night without chocolate was a lesser night, a de-tuned version of love. When I watched cleanly, I saw the trick. The excitement was the children climbing onto the sofa, the shared blanket, the gasp at the same scene. The sweets added nothing to that excitement; they interrupted it every few minutes with a reach, a chew, a rustle, a small negotiation about who had more. When I stopped giving the credit to the gatecrasher, the evening did not dim. It brightened. The taste of real food came back, subtle and clean. The satiety stayed.

You fear that without the gatecrasher the party drowns. The reality is the gatecrasher drowned the party and told you it saved it.

So when you hear that line — "It's sociable / it makes the meal / the occasion wouldn't be the same" — hear it for what it is: the gatecrasher thanking himself for your hospitality. He made nothing. He took.

### THE VOICE THAT SELLS THE ILLUSION

If the gatecrasher is the act, the voice that sells the ticket lives one floor up.

You have felt a whisper in the last few hours since we started talking. A mild, empty, slightly insecure, slightly uptight tug, a mouth that wants something, a hand that wants to reach. We named that whisper in the last chapter. It is tiny. It is the Sugar Imp, a small parasite, already dying the moment you stop feeding it. It has no power to command you. It can only murmur.

Then there is the voice that interprets the murmur. It says, this means you need energy. This means you deserve a treat — celebrating, rewarding myself, going to the cinema, etc with chocolate is how you love yourself after a hard week. This means without a fix you will not cope. It turns the Imp's little complaint into a story about pleasure and necessity. That whole belief-system, that kindly, well-dressed con artist that has been with you since childhood, furnishing every justification with perfect manners, is the Sweet Con. Once you hear it as a separate voice, you hear how hard it has been working for BAD SUGAR and how little it has ever worked for you.

See the split. The Imp is physical and trivial. The Con is mental and dominant. We have spent years terrified of the wrong one, guarding against a mild dip as if it were a monster that would overwhelm us, while the Con sat above it whispering, feed it now and you will feel better.

The Con's masterpiece is that phrase we pinned earlier — a genuine pleasure or treat. It repeats it so softly you stop hearing it as a claim and start hearing it as a fact. I had been good this week, you say, so I deserve a treat. Who told you that goodness is paid in BAD SUGAR, and that the coin you pay yourself with is the very thing that leaves you sticky and restless an hour later? I need sugar to get me through, you say. Who taught you that the lift that is followed by a fast drop is fuel? The Con did. It learned its script from parents who meant love, from bright packets that meant profit, from a culture that repeats that sugar is joy until you hear joy and think sugar.

There is warmth in this, not shame. We were not weak. We were outgunned. The trap was built by armies of food engineers who work to find the bliss point and the perfect chew, and by variable access — treat, forbid, allow, forbid — that makes each dose more precious than steady abundance ever would. You were a person trying to get through a day; they were a system trying to keep you grazing every day for the rest of your life.

When you hear the Con next — and you will, perhaps tonight — do not argue with it on its terms. Do not try to prove you are strong. Simply label it: that is the Sweet Con's script, not my reasoning. The moment you label it, it shrinks. You see it feeding the Imp and then taking the credit for the brief quiet that follows, as if it had soothed you. It did not soothe you. It fed the thing it pretends to cure.

### PLENTY TO YOU, NOTHING FOR YOU

We are ready for the verdict, but let me first give you two hard truths at full volume, then lift the fear off them in the same breath.

The fact is, the world body that sets sugar guidance says adults should keep free sugars below a tenth of daily calories — about fifty grams — and that halving that again brings extra benefit, while the normal shop-bought day most of us live puts us far above both. Normal intake is not natural. Normal is the trap's portion. That is not a prescription for you and I will not tell you a number to eat. If you live with diabetes, take prescribed medication, have a history of troubled eating, or have any medical condition where food changes need a clinician, talk to your clinician before you change what you eat. For the general reader, the point is simpler: what we call a normal sweet life is already an excess by every measured yardstick, and we feel it as normal only because the trap moved the yardstick.

The fact is also this. Free sugars feed the bacteria that live on teeth. Those bacteria make acid. Acid softens enamel. Over months that softening becomes decay. Fluoride and brushing sit in the middle of that chain and blunt it, so sugar alone is not a simple sentence and you are not doomed by yesterday's sweets, but the mechanism is flat and physical: BAD SUGAR does harm while claiming to be harmless. You do not need to quit from fear about this. Fear is not the reason to escape. The reason to escape is that there is nothing being taken from you when you do. Hear the fact, then feel the relief with it: you are not leaving a pleasure to avoid a threat. You are leaving a con that was doing plenty TO you while pretending to do something FOR you.

I want you to feel the weight off your shoulders there. I know the health facts are frightening if left to sit as a threat. I do not want you to stop because you are frightened. I want you to stop because you see you are not giving up a genuine pleasure or treat at all — and because when you see that, the fear loses its job. The relief is available now, not after a future test result.

Run the test one more time, cleanly, without the Con narrating.

What does BAD SUGAR do FOR you, that you could not have without it? Does it give you energy, when the quick sweet you use to get through is followed by the very dip you then blame on needing more? Does it give you a true treat, when the celebration you paid for with chocolate is bright because of the people and the story, and the chocolate merely hitched along? Does it make the meal, when the meal was already satisfying before the gatecrasher asked to be let in?

Search honestly. Not one of the ten lines survives as a genuine gain that belongs to BAD SUGAR alone. What survives is this, and it is the whole inversion in one sentence:

BAD SUGAR does plenty TO you. It does nothing FOR you.

We have been grateful to the rescuer who was secretly the perpetrator, grateful for the brief quiet after the dose as if it were a gift, when the dose itself had created the noise. Once that is seen, it cannot be unseen.

So keep the menu where you can see it. When a line floats up tomorrow — I deserve a treat, it's sociable, just a little can't hurt, I'll quit tomorrow — meet it with the test that now lives in you: is this a genuine pleasure or treat, or is it the Sweet Con feeding the Sugar Imp and taking the credit? And hear the reply as a small, cheerful, external fact, not an inner struggle. That is the Con talking. Not you. You already know what the Con does TO you. You already know it does nothing FOR you.

You did not fail to enjoy life. You were conned out of tasting it. The task ahead is not to resist a pleasure. It is to stop crediting a gatecrasher for a party that was yours all along.

### SUMMARY

* You stopped weighing harm versus benefit and asked what good BAD SUGAR does at all — and found no genuine gain that belongs to it alone.
* The ten familiar justifications in your own words justify themselves on the trap's script; the sociable line fails because the meal and the occasion are warm without BAD SUGAR — it was sneaking a ride.
* The tiny whisper is the Imp; the loud story that calls it pleasure, treat, fuel or reward is the Sweet Con feeding the Imp and taking the credit.
* World guidance puts ordinary intake far above the less-than-a-tenth ceiling, and free sugars drive plaque acid toward decay with fluoride and brushing in between — facts that inform without being your reason to quit.
* You now test any urge with one question — is this a genuine pleasure or treat, or the Con at work — and see that BAD SUGAR does plenty TO you and nothing FOR you.
