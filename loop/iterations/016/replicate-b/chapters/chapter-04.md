Chapter 4
THE ENERGY LIE

IN THIS CHAPTER — the 3 p.m. desk with the cold coffee ring, the vending machine humming down the corridor, the sticky mouth and heavy eyelids after the hit, the rollercoaster you pay to ride and then pay to get off

*The lift is not energy — it is the brief quiet after the last dose created the noise.*

### THE THREE O'CLOCK VOICE

At ten past three you know the voice before you hear the words.

We have all lived this hour. The screen has gone milky. The shoulders have dropped an inch. Your mouth is a little dry. There is a thin, restless itch behind the eyes, a heaviness that says you are empty though you ate an hour ago. And then the thought arrives, in your own dialect, helpful and reasonable:

"I need something quick and sweet to get me through."

If you work in an office you know the choreography. The chair pushes back. The small walk that is not really about stretching your legs. The corridor hum. The bright door with the pictures of chocolate and crisps. You do not decide to want it. You feel pulled toward it. You tell yourself, it gives me energy, it helps me concentrate, it's fuel. Without it you will not get through the afternoon.

I know that feeling because I lived it exactly as you do. I kept a drawer that was not a drawer but a pharmacy. Biscuits for meetings. Sweets for the drive. A bar for the three o'clock slump. I told myself I was topping up the tank. I told myself I was being practical. What I was really doing was feeding something and then thanking it for letting me feel normal for twenty minutes.

Let me ask you flatly, as your friend, not your judge. When did you decide that a sweet was fuel?

Did you decide it before you ever had a sweet, as a child, watching a plate of real food? Or did you learn it after years of eating BAD SUGAR, noticing that after the sweet you felt a little less bad than you had a minute before, and then calling that less-bad feeling energy?

We never thought it over when we found it. We found the sweet where the adults left it, we ate it, we felt the brief softening, and the adults — who meant love and had been taught the same line — said, sugar gives you energy. The bright packets said the same louder. We believed it because relief felt like proof.

The fact is, the lift you call fuel carries a real bodily load. At population level, a pattern of high added sugar is linked with the liver being forced to turn the extra into fat, with pressure rising, with a low simmer of inflammation that dulls the body's own signals for when it has had enough. This is not a prediction about you — the link is about patterns across many people, not a sentence on your next test — and you do not need to stop from fear of it. I tell you in the same breath as the relief: you are not stopping to dodge a future fright, you are stopping because there is nothing real being taken from you when you do. The load is simply what the lift was charging you while it called itself free.

You fear the price of stopping. The price was the sweet itself.

### THE ROLLERCOASTER IN THE CORRIDOR

Let me take you to the corridor properly, because your body knows more than your story.

It is 3:07. The vending machine is humming. You have that quick, tight search in the chest — not hunger as a mild, friendly signal that says it is time to eat real food, but a jittery uptight wanting that says now, quick, sweet, now. You choose. You tear the wrapper. You eat. For three or four mouthfuls the world comes back. The shoulders lift. The brain feels lit. This, you say, is energy.

Watch what happens next, without the Con narrating.

At 3:28 the mouth is sticky. The teeth feel coated. The tongue is looking for water. At 3:41 the eyelids are heavy. The very fog you took the sweet to clear is back, a little thicker. You sigh. You think, I crash in the afternoon, I have low energy, I need another top-up to get through. And the machine is still humming.

This is the rollercoaster you have been paying to ride. You queue, you pay, you are yanked up the incline — that is the lift — and then you are dropped. The lift and the drop are not two separate facts about your constitution. They are one ticket.

I want you to hear how many of us describe this in the same words, because those words are not theory, they are your own lived pattern: I would have something quick and sweet but now know that will only give a short term lift which will inevitably be followed by a fast drop. That sentence is not physiology. It is a woman at her desk noticing what happened every day and finally naming it cleanly. The short term lift then fast drop is not fuel misbehaving. It is fuel revealing itself as a loan with interest collected an hour later.

The fact is also this, and I will tell it straight and then remove the fear in the same breath. After a sugary hit your body releases insulin to do its job — that is the lift working — and that surge can produce a dip afterward while you feel the slump. In healthy people that everyday slump usually happens without any measured low sugar at all, and a true clinical low is rare. If you live with diabetes, take prescribed medication, have a history of troubled eating, or have any medical condition where food changes need a clinician, talk to your clinician before you change what you eat. For the general reader the point is simpler: the dip you feel is real enough to notice, and it follows the dose, but it is not proof you need more sugar. It is proof the dose created the need it then offered to relieve.

You were not low and then rescued. You were level and then pushed down, then sold the ladder back.

I tell you this not so you will be frightened of the dip, but so you will stop bowing to it. An hour after the sweet you are not deficient. You are withdrawing from the sweet. The difference is everything. One asks for another dose. The other asks for nothing — just a little time while the tiny Imp that makes that empty, slightly insecure, slightly uptight feeling starves harmlessly the moment you stop feeding it.

We told ourselves our afternoons needed fuel. What they needed was to be left alone.

### IT'S THE OTHER WAY AROUND

Isolate the variable, as you would in any honest experiment.

Think of a day when you ate real food when you were properly hungry — not grazing, not picking, not eating because the clock said lunch, but eating because your body asked — and you ate until that calm, clean pause that says enough. How was three o'clock that day? Not buzzing. Not glittering. Level. Clear. A little tired if you slept poorly, because bodies do tire, but not that uptight, scrabbbling, now-now-now tired.

Think now of the day you started with a sweet breakfast, a sweet coffee, a biscuit at eleven because you deserved it. How was three o'clock that day? Heavy. Edgy. The machine very loud.

Same you. Same job. Same sleep. Only the early doses changed. Yet on the second day you concluded, I need sugar to concentrate. It is the other way around.

The sweet does not give you concentration. It lends you the feeling of concentration for twelve minutes by silencing the jitter it left last time, then collects the jitter back with interest. You never rise above the normal of a person who never ate it. You bob below it and call the bob back up a lift.

We credit BAD SUGAR with what the morning, the meal, the company, the work itself gave us. You say, it helps me focus. Then why does the focus arrive even sharper and steadier on days without the rollercoaster? You say, it is fuel. Then why does real food — soup, bread, fish, potatoes, fruit, vegetables, the food your body recognises — leave you satisfied without the sticky mouth and the next-hour hunt? You say, I would not get through the afternoon without it. Yet you get through every morning before the first sweet without it, and children get through whole afternoons of maths and running and laughing on real food without it.

You believed BAD SUGAR was a genuine pleasure or treat that powered you through, and that without it you would be deprived of the very lift you needed — that is the story that kept the walk to the machine feeling sensible.

Strip the credit and look again.

The energy was yours. BAD SUGAR was sneaking a ride. The work got done because you did it. The film was watched because you were watching. The afternoon passed because afternoons pass. The sweet hitched along and then asked to be thanked for the journey.

Once you see the loan, you cannot unsee it. Every "lift" reveals its price tag.

There is a kinder truth underneath that I want you to feel as relief, not as a new rule. Your body makes glucose itself from protein and other stores when it needs it. You are not depriving it when you stop feeding it BAD SUGAR. You are leaving it to do what it already knows how to do, undistracted. Hunger returns as a mild, friendly signal. Satisfaction arrives cleanly. Taste comes back. No heroic replacement system is required. The baseline that felt lost is not lost. It was being drowned out.

You do not need to brace for three o'clock. You need to see it plain. At 3:07 the Imp will murmur — a faint tug, an old habit of hand and mouth — and the Con will translate that tug instantly: you need energy, get something quick and sweet. Label it as you hear it. That is the Imp dying, not you fading. That is the Con feeding the Imp and taking the credit for the quiet that follows. You have felt that quiet a thousand times and called it fuel. It was only the brief silence after you fed the noise you did not need.

You are not giving up energy. You are giving up the thing that has been borrowing it.

NEVER THINK I CAN'T HAVE BAD SUGAR — THINK GREAT, I'M FREE
There is nothing to give up.

### SUMMARY

* You stopped believing BAD SUGAR is fuel and saw the afternoon lift as a brief loan that creates the crash it claims to fix.
* The 3 p.m. walk to the machine, the sticky mouth and heavy eyelids afterward, and the quick sweet followed by a fast drop are one ticket — the lift and the dip belong together.
* The lift carries a real bodily load at population level and the dip is usually not a clinical low — both facts inform without being your reason to change, and without being a diagnosis.
* Your body makes its own glucose and can be trusted to signal hunger and satisfaction when you eat real food and let it settle.
* The test that now lives in you is simple: when the voice says you need sugar to get through, you no longer hear fuel — you hear the Imp starving and the Con taking the credit — and you answer with relief, not want.
