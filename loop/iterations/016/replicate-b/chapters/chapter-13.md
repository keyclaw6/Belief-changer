Chapter 13
THE LAST ORDINARY DOSE

IN THIS CHAPTER — the kitchen at dusk, the last packet on the table, the ugly attention, the spoken vow, the immediate congratulation, the two doors that stay shut

*You do not become free — you see you already are, and the last dose is where you notice it.*

### THE GATE YOU ARE ALREADY STANDING AT

We have talked about readiness as if it were a feeling you must wait for.

It is not.

You are at the gates of the prison. Behind you is the noise you lived in for years — the drawer, the hum at the end of the corridor, the sticky mouth and heavy lids, the box that emptied when you meant to take one biscuit. In front of you are the fruited plains of freedom. The gate is not locked. It never was. The Sweet Con held it shut with a story, and the Sugar Imp gnawed a little to make the story feel true.

If you feel quiet, a little ordinary, a little unsure whether this will work for you — you are ready.

I have seen a thousand readers stand exactly where you stand now. They did not feel brave. They felt normal. They had closed the escape routes — just a treat, just the special ones, just tomorrow, just wean off — and found those lines were not theirs. They had seen that BAD SUGAR does nothing FOR them and plenty TO them. They had watched the lift reveal itself as relief from a low the previous dose created, never above the level of a person who never ate it.

The seeing is done. What remains is not more learning. What remains is a threshold you cross once.

This is not a test of character. You do not need a perfect day or a perfect mood. You do not need steel. You need the willingness to take one ordinary dose with your eyes open and then make a simple, solemn promise and let freedom be conferred at that instant.

If you have diabetes, take prescribed medication, have a history of disordered eating, or any condition affected by what you eat, talk to your clinician before you change what you eat.

And if you still hear one small cord humming — if some part of you still says keep one special, keep the house clean but the shop allowed — do not bulldoze it with grit. Go back and re-read the章节 that kills that cord. Belief must change first. When it has, this chapter feels easy.

You have nothing to lose by crossing now with an open mind — you have nothing to lose and everything to gain — and everything to gain by finding you were already free.

### THE LAST ORDINARY DOSE — TAKEN WITH ATTENTION

We are not going to make a laboratory of this.

I am not asking you to buy a special bar to study. I am not asking you to taste it slowly for science. This method does not need a staged dose.

Take your last ordinary dose exactly as you would have taken it yesterday, in its ordinary place, at its ordinary time. The packet that is already in the cupboard. The bowl you would have filled. The tea you would have sugared. The petrol-station sweet on the way home.

Take it with one difference: attention on the ugliness.

Not the romance. The fact.

Look at it before you eat it. The bright packaging that sells itself as a treat while it is a dose in tasty wrapping. The way the packet is designed to make you reach without thinking. Feel the faint empty gnaw that called for it — that mild, slightly insecure, slightly uptight feeling that the Sugar Imp uses as a mouthpiece. Hear the translation the Sweet Con adds: you need this, you deserve this, this is pleasure, this will get you through.

Now eat it. Notice what actually happens.

The first bite is loud and sweet and gone in seconds. The second is less. By the third you are not tasting pleasure. You are feeding a nagging. Chew it and find the stick on the teeth, the film on the tongue, the sugary perfume that turns sticky at the back of the throat. Notice the faint nausea under the sweetness if you keep chewing honestly. Notice the twenty-minute lift that is not lift at all but the quieting of the gnaw the previous dose left — you are only climbing back toward normal, never above it.

Then notice what follows while you watch.

The mouth turns dry. The lids get heavy. The head goes a little dull. The mood dips a notch and the trap whispers that this dip proves you need another hit. You see it now the other way around. The dose created the low. The rescue is the perpetrator. The fact is, BAD SUGAR never gave you energy. It borrowed relief from tomorrow and charged interest today.

Do this once with attention — one ordinary dose, fully noticed — and the illusion loses its shine. You are not depriving yourself of a genuine pleasure or treat. You are watching a con complete its loop in real time and seeing it plain.

When you have finished that ordinary dose, you are done.

Not reducing. Not tapering. Not saving one special for later. Done, because you have seen there is nothing in it you want.

### THE SOLEMN VOW — FREEDOM CONFERRED NOW

I promised you escape would feel like escape, not sacrifice.

It does, because the freedom is not earned over weeks. It is conferred the moment the decision is made and meant. The body will catch up. The mind decides now.

So here is your moment. Stand with me at the gate. Look back at the trap you have lived in and forward to the ordinary life that will prove it is gone.

Make the vow out loud. Speak it. A vow that is mumbled stays a thought. A vow that is spoken becomes who you are.

TAKE YOUR LAST ORDINARY DOSE AND MAKE THE SOLEMN VOW
Do not wait to be free — you already are.

Say it plain, as you mean it:

"I will never eat BAD SUGAR again. Not as a treat, not as a reward, not as fuel. I am free."

Say it again if you want to hear the smile in your own voice. There is no trick. You have not given up sweetness. You have stopped feeding the thing that blocked it. The Sugar Imp is already starving. The Sweet Con loses its voice the instant you stop answering it.

I congratulate you now. Not tomorrow. Now.

You have just taken the last ordinary dose you will ever need to take. Let yourself feel that. Not dread. Relief. The light, clean relief of putting down something heavy you had carried long after you forgot you were carrying it.

You stopped easily, immediately and permanently. That is not a boast for later. That is what happened the moment you meant the vow. The easy part is not ahead of you. It is what you have just done.

Whenever BAD SUGAR crosses your mind — in a shop, at a table, on a sofa — you will not mourn a lost friend. You will think one joyous line and mean it:

FANTASTIC! I'M FREE!

Say it now. Out loud. Let it be foolish and loud and true. FANTASTIC! I'M FREE! That is the thought you keep for the rest of your life. The packet, the bowl, the bright aisle — they fire the old cue and the cue now fires joy. The thought does not suppress. It reframes. Every ping becomes a peal of relief that you no longer do it.

### TWO DOORS THAT MUST STAY SHUT

There are only two ways back into the trap. Both arrive as quiet, reasonable thoughts in your own voice. Both are the Sweet Con, not you.

Pre-hear them now so they arrive pre-labelled when they come.

The first door is the bad day.

"I deserve a treat. I have had a terrible day. This will get me through. Just this once to feel better."

Look at it with your new eyes. The dose will not fix the day. The fact is, the treat predicts low mood rather than relieves it. You get twenty minutes of quiet gnawing going quiet, then the heavy lids and the sticky mouth and the flat hour after where you feel worse and call the worse proof you needed it. You are paying yourself with the cause. A bad day is the worst reason to feed the Imp, because you are hiring your pain to argue for the thing that deepens it.

When that door knocks — and it will, on a Tuesday you did not plan — do not argue. Do not white-knuckle. See it: that's the Sweet Con offering rescue by the perpetrator. Think: FANTASTIC! I'M FREE! and get on with comforting yourself the way a free person does — with rest, with water, with real food you like when you are hungry, with company, with the story, with the evening itself. The evening was always the point. BAD SUGAR was only sneaking a ride.

The second door is the small exception.

"Just one cannot hurt. Just the natural one. Just the special one from the good shop. Just a taste so I don't feel deprived."

Totality is not harder than moderation. Totality is the only thing that ends the negotiation. One intentional dose revives the belief, and the belief rebuilds the trap. Your body can cope with an accidental blip — a spoonful in a sauce you did not know about — but your mind cannot allow a deliberate exception. Guard the belief, not the bite. The buffer is there for accidents, not for driving erratically. An intentional just one is not a lapse. It is reopening the decision and handing the Sugar Imp its next meal.

The Imp's life is mild but well-defined. It will make a faint empty noise for a little while if you listen. That noise is not a command. It is a death throe to rejoice in. Each ping unanswered is the little creature starving a little more. Welcome it.

When that second door slides open — "just one" in your softest voice — answer it the same way. See it: that's the con keeping one cord plugged in so the whole switchboard stays lit. Think: FANTASTIC! I'M FREE! and pity the trap, not yourself.

You will not use willpower to hold these doors. Willpower is what we used when belief had not changed. You will not need to resist a genuine pleasure or treat you have seen is not one.

You need one more instruction for the days ahead. It is not a struggle. It is a decision about a decision.

NEVER REOPEN THE DECISION
The tug-of-war ends the moment you stop pulling.

Do not test the vow. Do not weigh it again in the afternoon. Do not ask was that the right dose to end on. The tug-of-war of fear had two ropes and both belonged to the trap — fear you will fail and fear of who you will be without it. Put down both ropes and the war is over. A decision never reopened cannot be lost.

### WHAT THE NEXT FEW DAYS FEEL LIKE, TOLD HONESTLY

I will not insult you by promising you will feel nothing at all.

Some people feel almost nothing. Some feel a mild empty feeling at the habitual times — three o'clock, the sofa, the aisle — a little gnawing, a little restlessness, a little dull head. The fact is, that dip is mild but well-defined, and if you notice it most it tends to cluster two to five days after the last intentional doses and then it thins. It is not a detox. It is not a sentence. It is the small, passing low that follows the last dose, already dying if not fed.

Do not give that feeling a grand name. Call it what it is: the Sugar Imp's death throes. Be glad to feel it leaving. Drink water. Eat real food when you are hungry and notice the clean pause where enough arrives. Move. Breathe. Do your ordinary things. The dip fixes itself when you do not fix it with a hit. The more you welcome the dying noise, the sooner it passes. You do not need to change everything else in your life for this to happen. Your body makes the glucose it needs; you are not depriving it. You are removing the spike that kept drowsing it.

There is no superpower at the end of those days. There is something quieter and better. Hunger returns as a mild friendly signal, not an emergency alarm. Taste returns, not heightened into a trick but simply clean — real food tasting like itself and sweeter than you remember because BAD SUGAR had been blocking the taste. Satisfaction arrives with a pause you had forgotten, where you put the fork down because you want to, not because you are being good.

You will not have to keep telling yourself you are free. You will know it in the small proofs: waking without the hunt, paying for bread and milk without being pulled, eating with your family and finding the warmth exactly where it always was — in the people and the story and the room.

### A NEW WAY TO SEE THE AISLE

On your next ordinary day you will walk past the bright packets and see them differently.

Not with envy. With pity.

You will see the person with a trolley full of doses and you will not think they are indulging. You will see someone still inside the Sugar Trap, still being told that BAD SUGAR is a genuine pleasure or treat, still paying to ride the rollercoaster and then paying to get off it. You were that person. You know the load behind the bright colours — the heavy lids, the sticky regret, the next hunt.

Let the sight reinforce you. Not as superiority. As relief. You are not being deprived. They are. You are walking out with your bread and milk and your evening intact. That is freedom's quiet bonus. You do not need to preach. Do not evangelise in shops or at tables. Visible ease does the recruiting. Your freedom is not an argument. It is the proof.

And if you slip — a biscuit at a meeting taken without thinking, half eaten before you notice — do not turn a blip into a verdict. Put it down. Forgive it instantly. Guard the belief and the blip revives nothing. A rumble strip is not a crash. The only thing that can rebuild the trap is inviting the Sweet Con's story back: "I have blown it, so I may as well carry on." You have not. You had a blip. Your vow stands. Keep it standing.

You took the last ordinary dose. You made the solemn vow. You are free now, for the rest of your life — and for the rest of your life means something marvellous now, not something to fear.

Whenever the thought comes, think FANTASTIC! I'M FREE! and rejoice at a dead enemy.

Get on with enjoying your life.
