Chapter 5
THE TREAT THAT ISN'T

IN THIS CHAPTER — the cinema queue with the bright jars, the birthday cake with the candles still smoking, the corner shop after being good all week, the quiet switchboard humming behind the curtain

*You were not rewarding yourself — you were paying yourself with the very thing that made you need a reward.*

### I DESERVE THIS

You know the sentence before you say it. It arrives complete, reasonable, almost kind.

"I've been good this week."

"I deserve a treat."

"I'm celebrating, rewarding myself, going to the cinema with chocolate — I've earned it."

We have all said it in the same dialect. The week has been tight. Work has pulled. Someone was ill, someone was demanding, you kept the plates spinning. On Friday evening you walk to the shop not because you are hungry — you ate an hour ago — but because you want to mark that you survived. You stand in front of the bright wall and choose. The bar, the packet, the tub. The hand knows where to go before the mind has finished the sentence.

I stood there exactly as you do. I told myself the sweet was love I was giving to myself. A thank you. A little transfer of kindness for effort. If anyone had asked me if that made sense I would have said, of course — everyone knows a treat is a reward.

Let me ask you as your friend, without a shadow of judgement, because I believed this with my whole chest for years. When did you decide that BAD SUGAR was love?

As a small child you did not decide it. You learned it. You were given a sweet when you were brave at the dentist, when you finished your peas, when you stopped crying. You saw adults you adored hand sweets to children they adored and call it a treat. Advertising, which spends billions to teach a single lesson, put the same scene on every screen: smiling mouths, warm light, the voice that says you deserve this, you are worth this, this is love in foil. Family did it with tenderness. The market did it for profit. Both installed the same line: sweet equals love, sweet equals reward.

We were conned, not foolish. The Sweet Con fed us phoney information and then let the Sugar Trap do the rest.

Watch the trick cleanly. BAD SUGAR does not relieve the hardness of your week. It borrows from next week to paint over this one. You eat the dose. For a few minutes there is a brief softening — the jitter the last dose left goes quiet — and you call that quiet love. Then the dip returns, a little lower, a little more restless, and the thought returns with it: you need another lift to feel okay. That is not a reward for being good. It is a tax on being good. You are paying yourself with the cause of the feeling you wanted to soothe.

Think of the shop after being good all week. You were good — you cooked, you worked, you held things together. You deserved something marvellous. What did you hand yourself? A hit that guarantees you will feel less marvellous an hour later, and a little more in need of another handout tomorrow. If a friend thanked you for a hard week by taking ten pounds from your purse and handing you back three with a ribbon on it, you would not call it a gift.

The fact is worth saying plainly, and I will lift the fear off it in the same breath. In one long study that followed thousands of men for years, men who ate more sweets had higher odds of low mood later — and the reverse was checked and not found. That is an association about patterns across many people over time, in men only, not proof that sugar causes low mood in you, and not a sentence hanging over you. I do not tell you so you will stop from fear of feeling low. I tell you because the story we were sold runs the other way. The sweet does not rescue the mood. The pattern predicts the mood dips after the sweet, more often than the other way around. The rescuer is the perpetrator again.

You are not stopping to dodge dread. You are stopping because the reward was never a reward.

### LOVE ON A PLATE

We tell ourselves another story that feels almost sacred, so we handle it gently and firmly at once.

Chocolate is love. Cake is celebration. Without them the evening would be less warm.

Is that true? Let's put the evening back together without the Con narrating.

You remember a birthday. Not the sugar — the room. The kitchen a little crowded. Someone has blown up too many balloons. The cake comes out and the candles are lit and everyone leans in. The children shout. You sing badly and loudly. The small one closes their eyes and wishes. Where was the love in that minute? In the icing, or in the faces?

You remember the cinema queue. The carpet with the swirls. The big jars humming with colour. The person in front of you paying while you decide. The film starts and the room darkens and your shoulder finds the person next to you. The bowl is in the middle and hands go in and out. When you laugh, do you laugh at the sweet or at the joke? When you feel close, is it the chocolate that made you close, or the darkness and the story and the hand you get to hold without having to explain why?

We were taught to credit BAD SUGAR with what the moment gave us. BAD SUGAR gatecrashed the party and then claimed it made the party. Carbs are lonely — they invite company — and BAD SUGAR is the gatecrasher who walks into every meal, every celebration, every sofa evening, puts its arm around the real hosts, and poses for the photograph. Afterwards we say, it wouldn't be the same without it.

It would be the same. Better, because you would taste it properly and remember it without the sticky mouth and the hunt that follows.

I ate anniversary cakes and cinema sweets and told myself I was making the night. What I was doing was renting the night from a dealer and then thanking the dealer for the view. The warmth was yours. The laughter was yours. The love was yours. BAD SUGAR was only ever sneaking a ride.

And when we call it love, we make the trap feel tender. That is the Con at its kindest and cruellest. It takes a child's memory — being handed sweetness by someone who loved you — and turns it into an instruction: to love yourself now, do the same. To love your children now, do the same. It is brainwashing with a soft voice. I honour the love that handed you the sweet. I am vicious only to the lie that the sweet was the love.

Once you see who was really hosting the evening, you cannot unsee it. The people were the treat. The story was the treat. The quiet pride in being good all week was the treat. BAD SUGAR had only pasted its label over them.

You believed BAD SUGAR was a genuine pleasure or treat you deserved and without it the reward would be missing — that is the script that kept the shop visit feeling like kindness.

The script is false. The kindness was missing the sugar. The sugar was missing the kindness.

### WHY THE SECOND BITE NEVER TASTES LIKE THE FIRST

If BAD SUGAR were a genuine pleasure or treat, the pleasure would be reliable. We would take a dose and feel good, and the next dose would feel as good, and the tenth would feel as good as the first.

Your life tells you the opposite. Does it not?

The first square is bright. The second is a little less. By the fourth you are no longer tasting; you are chasing. The packet that once made an evening now makes a corner of an evening. The family size replaces the small bar, not because you have become greedy but because the same hit no longer touches the sides.

There is a reason for this that is worth naming without frightening you.

Imaging work suggests the wanting circuit gets crowded and a little blunted with repeated hits — needing more to feel a little, wanting more before you have even tasted — and at the same time it gets hyper-reactive to cues: the wrapper, the smell by the till, the jar in the queue. This picture is mixed and is consistent with scans more than proven by them, and it does not mean you have damaged your brain. It means the system that learns to want is noisy, and BAD SUGAR is good at making it noisy. Enjoyment fades while wanting spikes. That is why you can feel less pleasure and more pull at the same time. It is why the box empties after you meant to have one.

You have lived this. One bite becomes the box. You do not open the box because the first bite was so marvellous you want to prolong it. You open the box because the first bite re-lit the want it had briefly quieted. Each dose re-triggers the loop it claims to relieve. The wanting is not proof the sweet was wonderful. It is proof the sweet woke the wanting again.

We call that a treat. A treat does not behave like that. Real food behaves differently. You eat when you are hungry, you feel the clean pause where enough arrives, you stop and you do not think about food again until hunger returns as a mild, friendly signal. BAD SUGAR never gives you that pause. It gives you a chase. The fact the chase intensifies is not your weakness. It is the design of the trap.

It's the other way around.

You thought you needed a treat because you had been good and felt flat, and the treat would lift you. The treat made the flatness deeper and called that deeper flatness proof you need another treat. The love you thought you were giving yourself was the very thing making you feel un-loved an hour later. The reward did not follow being good. It followed the last dose.

### THE SWITCHBOARD WITH ONE CORD STILL IN

Let me show you why one is never one, and why being good all week does not cash out as a sweet on Friday — no matter how loudly the Con says treat when you deserve one.

Picture the old switchboard in the back room of your primitive brain. Not a single switch but a dusty appliance with a hundred power cords hanging out the back. Each cord is a belief that keeps the Trap lit: I need it to get through. It gives me energy. It is sociable. It is harmless. It is natural. It is my reward. It is love. I can have just one.

We have been pulling those cords, you and I, one by one. In the last chapter we pulled the energy cord and felt the relief of seeing the rollercoaster for what it was. In this chapter we are pulling the two cords that look most like love: I deserve a treat and it's my reward. Celebrating with chocolate and going to the shop after being good.

Here is what happens when you leave one cord in.

You pull ninety-nine. The board still hums. The light stays on. The Imp — that small, starving creature that makes the faint empty, slightly uptight tug — still gets its whisper of power, because the Sweet Con still has a feed. One cord is enough to keep the whole illusion alive. That is why just a treat, once a week is fine keeps the Trap alive as surely as a daily habit. The frequency is not the point. The belief is the point. If you believe BAD SUGAR is a genuine pleasure or treat even once, the Trap has somewhere to plug in, and the next cue — the birthday, the cinema, the Friday — will fire want before hunger has even had a thought.

This is not a metaphor for willpower. The switchboard is not a test of strength. It is a test of whether the lie is still believed. Leave the treat cord plugged and the evening will prove it to you. You will sit with the bowl in the middle and tell yourself this is the exception, and the exception will feel like a bashful permission while you eat and like a need again an hour later. You will not have failed. You will have left the power on.

We were taught to think moderation proves control. It proves the opposite. Intermittent access makes the hit more precious. The special one is the most powerful dose of all because it carries all the romance. That is why the one-bite-becomes-the-box pattern lives in moderation, not in spite of it. You do not need a stronger will to resist the box. You need to see the first bite is the box. The cord is the cause.

I want you to feel how much lighter this is than the story the Con told you. You are not being asked to give up love, or celebration, or kindness to yourself after a hard week. You are being shown they were never inside the packet. The packet was borrowing their light. Pull the last cord and the light does not go out. It comes on properly for the first time. The cake remains, but you taste it as food among friends, not as a fix that decides the night. The cinema remains — the dark, the joke, the shoulder — and you discover the evening does not need a tax to be sweet. Real sweetness returns because BAD SUGAR had been blocking the taste.

So hear the two sentences clearly and let the second replace the first the moment it arrives.

The old script says: I've been good, I deserve a sweet.

The true sentence says: I was good, and the good was the treat — BAD SUGAR would only turn the treat into a need.

You do not need to argue with the old sentence and you do not need to fight it. You simply see it for what it is — the Sweet Con speaking, not you — and you rejoice that you no longer need to pay yourself with your prison currency.

There is nothing tender being taken from you. There is a tender thing being returned.

### SUMMARY

* You stopped believing that BAD SUGAR is love or a reward and saw the deserved treat as self-payment with the cause of the flatness you wanted to soothe.
* The sweetness of the cinema, the birthday and the Friday shop is the people, the story and your own pride at being good — BAD SUGAR was only sneaking a ride and taking the credit.
* The lift of a treat fades while the wanting spikes; the first bite wakes the next want, which is why one becomes the box — not because you are weak but because the loop re-triggers itself.
* Leaving even one belief cord plugged in keeps the switchboard lit, so just one treat keeps the Trap alive; freedom is seeing that the good week already was the treat.
* The pattern linking more sweets with later low mood is an association seen in men over time, not proof of cause in you, and is not your reason to escape; your reason is that the treat was never a genuine pleasure in the first place.
