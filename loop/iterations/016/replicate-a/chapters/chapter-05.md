Chapter 5
THE TREAT THAT ISN'T

IN THIS CHAPTER — The kitchen after being good, The shop on the way home, The cinema queue and the glowing counter, The birthday cake with candles, The switchboard with many cords

*The love was never in the packet — the packet was charging you for the love that was already there.*

### WHAT WE MEAN WHEN WE SAY TREAT

You know the sentence before you hear it.

"I deserve a treat."

"I've been good this week."

"Treat when you deserve one."

"It is my reward."

We speak it warmly, even proudly. We say it to ourselves in the shop doorway, in the kitchen after the children are in bed, in the queue at the cinema. The day has been hard. You have been good. And now sweetness will prove it.

I want you to see what that sentence really does.

We have fed ourselves a story that BAD SUGAR is love made edible. That chocolate is affection. That sweets are kindness. That cake is celebration. We did not invent that story. We were handed it. Mothers who loved us put a biscuit in our hand to stop our tears and we learned: sugar equals comfort. Advertisers who loved our money put cartoon tigers and dancing families on bright packets and we learned: sugar equals fun, sugar equals love, sugar equals deserve. Films put the big bowl in the middle of the sofa and we learned: without it the evening is not an evening. Teachers gave sweets for correct answers. Grandparents arrived with a paper bag as proof they had thought of you. It was never your fault. You were brainwashed to believe a dose could be a hug.

And because you believe it, you do not question it. You reach.

Let me ask you as plainly as I can. When you say you deserve a treat, what exactly do you think BAD SUGAR is doing FOR you that the day itself is not already doing?

You will say, it makes me feel rewarded. It makes the hard day feel finished. It tells my body I have been good.

Look closer.

Yesterday was hard. Your shoulders are tight. Your inbox is full. You have held yourself together. The feeling you want right now is relief, softness, a moment where you are held and told well done. That feeling is real and you deserve it. Utterly.

But does BAD SUGAR give it to you?

That chocolate was never a genuine pleasure or treat. That is the whole false belief of this chapter and we will strip it now.

What BAD SUGAR does is sell you back an imitation of reward while taking the real reward away.

### I'VE BEEN GOOD THIS WEEK

Let us walk the shop after being good, because we have all walked it.

It is Thursday evening. Or Friday. You have been good this week. You have eaten the salad at lunch when you wanted the roll. You have said no at work. You have been careful. You are tired from being careful. On the way home you pass the shop and the voice says, quietly, reasonably, lovingly: go on. Treat when you deserve one. You've earned it. Just this once.

Notice how sensible it sounds. It speaks in the language of fairness. It does not sound like craving. It sounds like justice.

You go in. The door chimes. The aisle is bright. Your hand knows where to go. You tell yourself small. You tell yourself just a bar, just a bag, just for tonight. You carry it to the till with a little flicker of excitement that feels like relief already.

What happens when you eat it?

For twenty seconds there is sweetness on the tongue, loud and immediate. Then the mouth coats. Then the eat does not stop where you promised. The bar becomes need for another square. The bag opens and the hand goes again without asking you. The voice that said just one now says finish it, you've started, you may as well.

And afterwards?

You do not feel rewarded. You feel sticky, slightly sick, slightly flat, slightly ashamed that you did exactly what you told yourself you would not do this week. The hard day has not been softened. It has been given a bill.

I have heard you describe it perfectly yourself: "celebrating, rewarding myself, going to the cinema, etc with chocolate" — as if the chocolate were the celebration. And I have heard the morning after that same justice: the heavy mouth, the restlessness, the quiet thought, why did I do that again when I was trying to be good.

Ask yourself: who was rewarded there?

The Sweet Con was rewarded. The Sugar Imp was fed. You paid yourself with the very thing that makes you feel less good tomorrow. That is not love. That is paying a debt in the trap's own currency.

You say, I deserve a treat. I agree. You do. You deserve tenderness, rest, warmth, pride, a moment that says well done without a sting in its tail. BAD SUGAR cannot give you any of those things. It can only imitate them for a few seconds while it creates the flatness you will then call needing another treat.

It is the other way around.

The treat does not relieve the hard day. The treat borrows from tomorrow to paint over today, then makes tomorrow feel harder so you reach again.

There is a flat fact here that you should hear without fear. In one long study that followed men for years, the men who ate more sweets were more likely to report low mood later on. The researchers checked the reverse — did low mood drive the sweets — and they did not find it. That is an observational link in men, not proof of cause, not true for everyone, and it is not a sentence on you. I do not want you to stop because you are afraid of low mood. I want you to see the direction the story points: the daily treat predicts feeling lower, not lifted. The rescue you trust to lift you is associated with the very dip you want rescued from. That is a frightening truth stated flatly, and the relief is this: you do not need that rescue. The feeling you want — to feel held after a hard day — lives in rest, in warmth, in company, not in a packet.

And here is why the same packet stops feeling like a treat even as you want it more.

Imaging work hints at two things happening together. The reward circuit gets crowded — blunted — so the same bar gives less enjoyment than it once did, and you need a family size where a square once did. At the same time the cue circuit gets hypersensitive — wrappers, queues, adverts, the shop doorway fire want before you are hungry. That is why you feel both less pleasure and stronger tug. That evidence is mixed and built largely on brain pictures, not proof of damage, and I do not want you to picture a broken brain. What you can trust is your own memory: the first square of the first bar you ever stole as a child felt huge; the last whole bar you ate felt smaller and left you wanting. Enjoyment fades while wanting spikes. The trap makes its currency feel more precious precisely as it buys less.

So the bargain is revealed. You work all week to deserve love, then pay yourself with something that imitates love for seconds, gives less each time, and leaves you needing to earn the same love again tomorrow.

Who would call that a reward?

### THE CINEMA QUEUE AND THE BIRTHDAY CAKE

We must meet the strongest case head-on, because if the treat is not love there, it is not love anywhere.

Picture the queue. Friday night. The cinema lights are low, the carpet smells faintly of popcorn, children bouncing on toes, you together, coats over arms. You have been looking forward to this all week. You join the glowing counter. The voice says warmly, we deserve this. This is love. This is family. The big bag crinkles. Everyone reaches into the middle.

Or picture the birthday cake. The room goes quiet, candles lit, a face lit from below, everyone singing slightly out of tune, the knife lifted, the first slice handed to you as if it were affection on a plate.

In both scenes BAD SUGAR says, I made this moment sweet. I am the sweetness.

It is not true. It never was.

Isolate the variable and watch credit return to its owners.

Was it the sugar that made the cinema sweet, or was it that you were side by side in the dark with people you love, warm, laughing at the same time, sharing the same story, your hand brushing theirs in the bag in the middle? Remove BAD SUGAR from that row and what remains? Everything. The film remains. The laughter remains. The closeness remains. The bowl was only ever sneaking a ride on the evening. You credited the packet because the packet was there, not because the packet created anything.

Was it the cake that made the birthday love, or was it that people stopped their busy lives, came into one room, looked at one face, sang as one, and said you matter to us? Remove the icing and what remains? The love remains. The attention remains. The made-thing remains — the table set, the name on the card. BAD SUGAR gatecrashed your party and claimed it made the party.

I say this with total warmth toward you and total harshness toward the con that taught you otherwise. Food does not equal love. Food is food. Love is people, touch, laughter, rest, being seen and held. The industry that sells BAD SUGAR has spent lifetimes splicing those two together in your head until you reach for a packet when you want a hug. You were not foolish. You were conned by armies of packaging, music, colour, and grandmothers who were conned before you.

Try this now, not as an exercise to perform but as a fact to see. Remember the last cinema bag. How long did the pleasure last? The first handful bright, the second less, the third mechanical. How long did the film last? Two hours. How long did the feeling of being together last? All the way home. One of those things was fleeting and left a residue you had to brush from your teeth. The other filled the whole evening.

Ask yourself again: what did BAD SUGAR do FOR you there?

It did nothing for you. It did plenty to you. It took your money, coated your mouth, halved your taste, made the next scene a little more about the bag than the story, then left you heavier and a little queasy on the walk to the car while the love you actually came for walked beside you untouched.

It was not love. It was the bill for love you already had.

And you have been paying that bill for years, calling it deserve.

### THE SWITCHBOARD WITH MANY CORDS

This is why one treat keeps the whole trap alive, even when you know the bargain is false.

Picture the primitive wanting part of your brain not as a creature this chapter — we have named the Imp already — but as a switchboard. An old, dumb appliance with many power cords plugged into the wall. Each cord is a belief the Sweet Con installed.

One cord says: I need it to get me through. We pulled that cord in the last chapter and saw it was the other way around.

One cord says: I deserve a treat when I've been good. One cord says: it is my reward after a hard day. One cord says: going to the cinema, celebrating, the birthday — these would be bleak without it. One cord says: just this one can't hurt. One cord says: once a week is fine.

You can unplug four cords and leave one plugged in and the machine still hums. The light stays on. Want still fires. That is the totality trap.

You can see clearly that BAD SUGAR is not fuel, and still think but it is my deserved reward, and the wanting returns fully intact on Friday night in the cinema queue. You can see the reward is false, and still think but just on birthdays, and the wanting returns on every birthday, which is often enough to keep the circuit warm.

The Sweet Con does not need you to believe all of its lines. It needs one.

This is the debut of that image because you have never had language for why moderation feels harder than freedom. Moderation is trying to keep most cords plugged in and one unplugged. You negotiate: I will cut down, I will keep the special ones, I will only have it when I deserve it. Each negotiation is a cord you left in. Each cord says you may still feed the Imp. And any cord that says you may feed the Imp keeps the Imp alive.

Freedom is not cutting down the voltage. It is pulling every cord.

You do not need a new definition of treat to replace the old one. You need to see that a treat, by its nature, should leave you feeling more tender toward yourself, not less. A genuine treat warms you after and lets you sleep. BAD SUGAR does the opposite. It hypes and drops, it blunts and spikes, it turns being good all week into being punished on Friday for the fact you were good.

So let us land the verdict plainly, with no softening left in the sentence.

You do not deserve BAD SUGAR. You deserve relief that actually relieves. You deserve reward that actually rewards.

BAD SUGAR was never love. It was the con selling you love's name.

Keep the love. Leave the packet.

Think of the days you have called deserve and trace what you did. You had a hard day — you deserved comfort and you reached for a dose. You had a good day — you deserved celebration and you reached for a dose. You had a dull day — you deserved a lift and you reached for a dose. Every occasion became its opposite because the same packet was plugged in. Hard, good, dull — all fed the same Imp through different cords. That is not treating yourself. That is tithing every feeling to the trap.

When you pull the last cord — when you see there is not one remaining occasion where BAD SUGAR does anything FOR you that the occasion does not already do for itself — the switchboard goes dark. Not because you are strong. Because there is nothing left to power.

Do you feel the shift? From I deserve this to I deserve better than this. From I have been good so I may be harmed a little to I have been good so I will be kind to myself. From chocolate is love to love was never in the chocolate.

That is the whole move of this chapter, won now.

Your body knows reward. It knows water when thirsty, food when hungry, rest when tired, laughter when together. Those rewards satisfy and end. BAD SUGAR imitates that satisfaction for seconds, then ensures you need it again. The difference is everything. One finishes. The other guarantees return.

You are not being deprived of treats by seeing this. You are being freed from paying for your own affection in the trap's currency. The evenings remain. The birthdays remain. The well-done after a hard day remains — more, not less, because you will finally taste it without the coating on your tongue.

SUMMARY

- BAD SUGAR is not love, reward or proof you have been good; those feelings belong to the people, rest and occasion, not to the packet that rides along.
- Treating yourself with BAD SUGAR is paying yourself with the cause of the flatness you wanted to reward, and the daily dose predicts feeling lower, not higher.
- The same dose gives less enjoyment each time while cues make you want it more, which explains why the treat feels both precious and disappointing.
- One remaining belief that BAD SUGAR is deserved keeps the whole wanting switchboard powered; freedom comes from seeing there is no occasion where it does anything FOR you that the occasion does not already provide.
