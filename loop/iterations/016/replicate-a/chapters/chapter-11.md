Chapter 11
MYTHS ON A PLATE — Q&A

IN THIS CHAPTER — The kitchen table laid with excuses on plates, The biscuit tin kept for special occasions, The empty cupboard that feels like control, The calendar that always reads tomorrow, The packet that says natural on the front

*Every loophole you think you saved for later is not yours — it is the trap talking in your voice.*

### THE BACK-POCKET SCRIPTS

You have felt fear lose its grip. You have seen the prison door open and understood it was never locked, that the Willpower Method was the second trap with no finish line, that the braggers and whingers were both still inside their story.

And now the mind, very quietly, does what it always does. It reaches into a back pocket and finds a folded note. Not fear this time. A reason. A reasonable, adult, moderate reason.

We all do this. We left a few thoughts unexamined on purpose, tucked away for later, because they felt too sensible to kill. They sound like common sense. They sound like you.

The fact is they are not yours. They were installed the same way every other piece of brainwashing was installed — by repetition, by packaging, by the fact that everyone says them. When that thought surfaces next week at the shop or at ten past three at your desk, it will arrive pre-labelled if we label it now. Then it cannot trick you.

So we will lay the plates out now and look at each one plain. No new teaching system. No new rules for life. Just plain seeing. Each myth in your own dialect, quoted back exactly as it sounds inside, and answered in a few sentences that leave no route back.

I put them flat and cheerfully, because there is nothing to fear here and nothing to defend. We are not about to take something good away. We are about to see that the excuse was keeping you in the low that it promised to fix.

If you have diabetes, take prescribed medication, have a history of eating distress or any condition affected by what you eat, talk to your clinician before changing what you eat — I want you clear and safe while your mind gets free.

### "I CAN CUT DOWN — JUST KEEP THE SPECIAL ONES, ONCE A WEEK IS FINE"

This is the favourite. It sounds mature. Controlled. Balanced.

You picture the tin with two biscuits left for Saturday, the family-size bag replaced by a small one, the rule that says only the good chocolate counts. It feels like control.

The fact is cutting down keeps the belief alive. Intermittent access — a little, then a gap, then a little — is the very schedule that in lab animals drives bingeing, wanting, and eating the box when access returns, while animals with free access do not show that pattern. I am not saying you are a rat. I am saying the pattern is about the schedule, not about character. When you make BAD SUGAR precious, you guarantee you will think about it, dream about it, and pay it more attention than ever. The 3pm rollercoaster does not get gentler if you ride it once a week; you just spend the whole week waiting for the ride.

One exception keeps the cord plugged in. Picture the old switchboard with many cords feeding the same machine — unplug most and the light stays on if one cord remains. Keep the special ones and the machine still hums. The pleasure does not shrink to fit the new rule; the want grows to fill the gap between doses. That is why once a week is never once a week for long. The special becomes frequent, the frequent becomes daily, and you are back negotiating with the box.

You do not need a safe level of BAD SUGAR because BAD SUGAR was never doing anything for you at all. There is no healthy level other than zero, not because your body cannot cope with an accidental mouthful — it can — but because your mind cannot keep one deliberate exception and stay free. Moderation is not control. It is the trap's most polite disguise.

### "I DON'T KEEP IT IN THE HOUSE, SO I'M IN CONTROL"

This sounds like proof. The cupboard is empty. You can pass a test.

Look closer at what is being controlled. The house is empty but the day is organised around the empty house. You drive a different way home so you do not pass the shop. You feel virtuous until four o'clock, then you feel restless, then you use the empty cupboard as evidence you are strong, then on Friday you buy it to eat in the car before you get in. The fact is control that depends on absence is not freedom; it is the same wanting, managed by distance.

We have all lived the zigzag. Strict in the kitchen, loose at the petrol station. Strict on Monday, the bowl emptied on Saturday in front of a film. The empty house did not remove the want. It scheduled it. The same lab pattern shows this plain: make access intermittent — allow, forbid, allow — and the binge returns bigger. Your yo-yo restriction mirrors that schedule. You were not weak. You built a preciousness machine and then blamed yourself for wanting the precious thing.

Freedom is not a better lock on the cupboard. It is not wanting to raid it when you walk past. When the belief changes, you can have a shop on every corner and a tin on every worktop and feel nothing but pity for the queue, not a tug to join it. Inhabit the old contexts confidently and they prove you are free. Do not arrange your life to avoid a thought. Change the belief and the thought arrives as relief, not as a command.

### "NATURAL SUGAR IS FINE — I NEED SUGAR FOR MY BRAIN, IT'S HARMLESS ANYWAY"

Two plates that look different but are the same note.

The first says honey, juice, syrup, the raw cane bag, the packet that shouts natural. The fact is your body does not file sugar by story. When we say BAD SUGAR we mean added, refined sugar and the processed junk carbs engineered to spike and crash you — the dose in tasty packaging. Fruit and vegetables and real food stay on the menu. Glucose itself is not an import you must eat; your body manufactures it from protein and other parts of food. You are not depriving your brain. You are not switching it off. You are not prescribing a new diet here. You are simply not needing to import sweetness to keep the lights on.

The second says it's just sugar, kids eat it, everyone does, a little cannot hurt, my brain needs it. The fact is the lift you call energy is real as a sensation and illusory as a benefit. The sweets arrive, insulin does its work, you get the familiar lift-then-fast-drop that you described yourself — quick and sweet, short term lift which will inevitably be followed by a fast drop — and then you blame that drop on needing more. It's the other way around. The dose created the dip it now offers to rescue you from. That dip is usually not clinical low blood sugar at all; in everyday life the slump arrives without any measured low glucose, and true hypoglycemia in people without diabetes is rare. I am not diagnosing you. I am pointing to the ordinary rollercoaster you already ride at three in the afternoon, sticky mouth, heavy eyelids, the vending machine hum, the vending machine promise that one hit will get you through. It will get you through to the next low.

And because I will not trade one myth for another, I will tell you honesty about the debate. Scientists themselves argue about whether sugar is addictive in the clinical sense. A leading review says the bingeing pattern is about intermittent access, not a neurochemical hook; other work shows wanting and cue firing. Both camps grant the same lived fact: under that intermittent pattern, people binge, crave the box from one bite, and feel trapped. You do not need to settle that argument to get free. You only need to see that the reason you think you need sugar for your brain is an installed story, and the harmless story is the frog in slowly heating water feeling fine while being cooked. The harm is not mystical. It is the daily load you already sense.

Leave the natural packet where it is. It is not your brain food. Your brain is fed by the real food you already love.

### "I'LL QUIT TOMORROW — I'LL WEAN OFF SLOWLY — I'LL SWITCH TO THE LESS-BAD VERSION"

Three versions of the same delay. All sound kind. All keep the trap alive tonight.

"Tomorrow" says you will, just not now. The fact is tomorrow never has fewer reasons than today. There will still be stress, still be a birthday, still be tiredness. The trap loves tomorrow because tomorrow keeps today safe for one more dose while the belief stays intact. And the cost is not abstract. Every tomorrow is another day of paying to ride the rollercoaster you pay to get off, another day moving away from relief that is available now.

"Wean off slowly" sounds gentle. The fact is the evidence on short weaning is genuinely split. In adolescents asked to stop sweet drinks for just three days, one experiment found symptoms improved, another found they worsened. That is not a firm foundation to build a gentle staircase on; it is a small, contested window. What is consistent is the wanting that lives between planned small doses. The slow taper stokes anticipation and fantasy between hits. You do not shorten the mild hump — the two to five day flatness that comes and then passes if you do not feed it — you stretch it. You make each small fix more exciting by making it scarce. Weaning is intermittent access by another name. It makes the thing more precious, not less.

"Switch to the less-bad version" says you can keep the ritual and lose the risk. Diet drinks, protein bars, little sweets that claim to be better. The fact is the switch keeps the cord plugged in and the hand reaching in the same way to the same cue — the packet, the aisle, the after-dinner hunt — and the old cue fires want before hunger has any say. The body waits for the hit, the mind rehearses permission, and the negotiation starts again: does this count, is this allowed, can I have a real one on Friday because I was good.

You do not need a kinder exit ramp. You need one plain decision. Not by grit. Not by disgust. By seeing that delay is the trap buying time. The hump, if you feel it at all, is mild and brief and starves if not fed. Lengthening the goodbye does not soften it. It feeds it.

There is no future version of you who is better equipped to do this tomorrow. There is only you, now, hearing each excuse arrive with its label already on it.

### NO ROUTE BACK — AND WHY THAT IS WONDERFUL

We have closed the loops before you reached for them, so when the thought comes next week it will not feel like your reasoning. It will feel like a line we already heard.

I can keep it for a special occasion. You will hear: cord still plugged in.

I just won't keep it in the house. You will hear: control by absence, not freedom.

Natural is different, my brain needs it. You will hear: body makes its own glucose, the slump is the dose's own dip, not a deficiency.

I'll start tomorrow, I'll taper. You will hear: tomorrow and taper are the schedule that builds the binge.

Do not argue with these thoughts. Do not wrestle them. See them. Smile that you saw them before they spoke. Pity the bright aisle of doses, do not envy it. You are not missing a pleasure. You are missing a dip that you no longer need to rescue yourself from.

I am not asking you to change from fear. The facts about load and teeth and pressure are true and then they are set down. I am asking you to see that there is nothing to keep. The rollercoaster you were told you needed to get through the day is the very thing that made getting through the day feel so hard. Step off and the day does not get bleaker. It gets ordinary, and ordinary, without the spike and crash, is marvellous.

We built no new curriculum in this room. We only put the back-pocket notes on the table and turned them over. On the back, in the same handwriting, is one line: the rescue is the perpetrator. Every loophole was offering you the cause as the cure.

Leave the plates where they are. You do not need to clear them. You only need to leave the table.

SUMMARY

- Cutting down and keeping special ones does not give control; intermittent access makes BAD SUGAR more precious and drives bigger binges.
- An empty cupboard is not proof of freedom; arranging life to avoid the shop is still being organised by want.
- Your brain does not need imported sugar; your body manufactures glucose from real food, and the everyday slump after sweets is the dose creating the dip, not a true low that needs sugar.
- Whether sugar is labelled addictive is contested, but the live pattern of bingeing under intermittent access is not contested and is what keeps the loop alive.
- Quitting tomorrow, weaning slowly, or switching to a less-bad version keeps the belief and the cue alive and stretches wanting rather than shortening it.
