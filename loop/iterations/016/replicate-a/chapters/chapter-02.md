Chapter 2
ALREADY HOOKED

IN THIS CHAPTER — Yesterday's kitchen worktop, The empty packet pushed to the back of the cupboard, A web made of sugar strands, A frog in warming water, The small creature gnawing, A body eating real food

*You do not freely choose BAD SUGAR — you are already living inside it, and what feels normal is the trap holding you steady.*

### YESTERDAY, AS IT REALLY HAPPENED

Let us look at yesterday. Not a bad day. A normal day.

You woke up. You told yourself you would be good today. Coffee, maybe a biscuit with it — just one. By late morning a little gnawing arrived, nothing dramatic, just a feeling that something sweet would be nice. You had a cereal bar, or a yoghurt that said light on the front and had sugar hidden in the small print. It helped for twenty minutes.

Lunch was a sandwich on white bread, a bag of crisps, a fizzy drink or a juice. You told yourself that was fine because it was lunch. Mid-afternoon the heavy eyelids came, the sticky mouth, the restlessness. You walked to the shop or the vending machine or the office tin. You said, I will have something quick and sweet to get me through. You did. Then the evening came and the grazing began in earnest. A few sweets while you cooked. A second helping of the starchy bit. Chocolate after dinner. You opened a family bag and told yourself you would have a handful. Then the inner negotiation started — just one more, go on, you have been good this week. The bag emptied. You felt a little ashamed, a little too full, a little annoyed at yourself. You pushed the empty packet to the back of the cupboard or folded the wrapper small so no one would see. You thought, tomorrow I will not overdo it.

Does that sound familiar? Not every detail, but the shape?

We tell ourselves that is normal. That is what everyone does. That we are choosing it and we could stop if we wanted to.

The fact is, we are already hooked and we call hooked normal.

If BAD SUGAR were a genuine pleasure we freely chose, why would we hide the packet? Why would we bargain with ourselves? Why would one bite so often become the box? I have heard you say it in your own words: I just crave sweets and when i eat them, i feel like i cant stop. That sentence is not weakness. It is accurate reporting from inside a trap.

Think about that phrase — once I start I can't stop. We use it lightly, almost as a joke. We say, I have no willpower around biscuits. We laugh. But listen to what it really means. Free choice does not feel like I can't stop. Free choice feels like I can take it or leave it and I genuinely do not mind which. If you needed a book to help you stop eating something, that alone proves you are not freely choosing it. If you wanted to stop a hobby you love, would you need instructions?

You did not decide to become a grazer. You were led there, one dose at a time.

You are not uniquely weak for feeling this. When researchers use validated scales for highly palatable food, pooled results find about one in seven adults and about one in twelve children meet addiction-like criteria — similar to figures we accept for alcohol or tobacco. That is not a diagnosis and it does not label you. It is a measure of self-reported feeling trapped. I tell you for one reason only: so you stop believing this is a character flaw in you. It is not. Many, many people feel exactly this pull, in the same quiet, everyday way, and call it normal because everyone does it.

Normal is not the same as free.

### THE WEB YOU DID NOT FEEL BEING BUILT

I want you to see how the trap is built so finely that you never feel the strands.

Picture a fly in a large room. It moves freely. It lands where it wants. The sun is coming through the window. Somewhere in the corner a spider has begun its work. The first strand is almost invisible. The fly brushes it and thinks nothing. The second strand is there tomorrow. The fly's flight is still easy. The third, the fourth — sticky threads at ankle height you step over without noticing. Each sweet hit is a strand.

The strand is not just the sugar itself. It is the idea that went with it. The biscuit with the morning coffee that makes the coffee count. The pudding that makes the meal complete. The chocolate that pays you for a hard day. The bowl in the middle of the table that makes the evening feel like an evening. Each time you fed the idea, another strand was fixed in place.

We were all taught that sugar is a treat, a reward, a little lift. We believed it because everyone said it, because bright packets and happy families told us so, because our parents gave us sweets for being good. That brainwashing is the real glue on the web. The sugar is just the material.

You felt free between the strands for a long time. That is the cunning of it. The fly between the first ten strands still believes it is free. It can still fly to the window. It is only when it tries to leave that it discovers every strand is holding its wings.

This is why you feel you choose BAD SUGAR. Between grazing sessions you do choose. You choose to have a small bar. You choose to keep a packet in the drawer. It feels voluntary because the web has not yet tightened. Then you try to cut down. You say, I won't have any tomorrow. And about three hours after your last hit a faint unease arrives — not pain, not illness, just an empty, restless feeling that says feed me. That feeling is tiny. But the brainwashing interprets it loudly: you need sugar. You want it. You deserve it. So you feed it and the web tightens by one more thread.

Call this what it is. For the rest of this book we will call it the Sugar Trap. Not a craving inside you, not a sweet tooth you were born with — a trap around you. Once you name it, you stop arguing with yourself and you start seeing the strands.

I am warm to you and harsh to the trap. You did not build this web. You flew into it on phoney information, as we all did. The trap built itself around your normal days, and it did it so gradually you had no reason to notice.

### THE FROG WHO FELT FINE

There is a second reason you did not notice. The harm came slowly.

Put a frog in cold water and heat it degree by degree. The frog does not jump. It adjusts. It feels fine. After all, the water today is only a little warmer than yesterday. This is not willful blindness — it is how a gradual trap works. You never get a single terrible day that says, today you were caught. You get a thousand ordinary days where you ate a little more BAD SUGAR than the year before and felt much the same.

Look at your own timeline. Perhaps as a child sweets were for Saturdays. Then they were for after school. Then for every afternoon. Then the portion grew from one biscuit to three, from a small bottle to a large one, from sugar in tea to sugar everywhere. At each step you felt normal. Of course you did — you were adjusting to slightly warmer water.

The fact is, that gradual warming does put a real load on the body while it feels like nothing. This is not to frighten you into changing — I do not want you to stop from fear. I want you to stop because you see the trap leaves you nothing to miss. But see the truth plainly: the daily lift carries a cost, and the water has been warming for years while we called it treat.

The relief is immediate and wonderful: the moment you step out of the water, your body starts to cool. You do not have to wait months to feel the difference. You only have to stop believing the warm water is comfort.

### THE SMALL CREATURE THAT IS NOT YOU

We need to separate two things that feel like one.

There is a tiny physical gnawing that BAD SUGAR creates in the body. It is real, but it is trivial. In the lab, rats given sugar for twelve hours and then deprived binge, show craving-like behaviour and a measurable dip afterwards. Rats given the same sugar freely all day do not show those signs. It is the on-off schedule — the yo-yo of restriction and access — that drives the binge, not the sweetness itself. That schedule is exactly what our kitchen rules recreate: be good, then allow a little, then be good again. The physical part in those animals is labelled mild but well-defined. In you it is felt as that faint, empty, slightly insecure feeling that says have something quick and sweet.

That tiny creature is not you. I will give it a name so you can see it clearly. It is the Sugar Imp. A little parasite that complains when it is not fed, and that starves very quickly when you stop feeding it. It has no power at all except the power you give it by believing its complaint means you need BAD SUGAR.

And then there is the much bigger thing that feeds it. That is the brainwashing — every idea that says BAD SUGAR is a genuine pleasure, a reward, fuel, love, comfort, the thing that makes the meal and the evening and the celebration. That voice is not yours either. It was installed by the trap and by a culture that profits from it. It talks in your voice, but it is the trap's script. It says I deserve a treat, I've been good this week, just this once, one bite won't hurt, I don't keep it in the house so I'm in control. Each line is a strand. Each line feeds the Imp.

Do you see the reversal? We think the craving proves BAD SUGAR gives us something. The craving proves the opposite. The craving is the Imp complaining because its last feed is wearing off. The relief you feel when you feed it is not pleasure — it is the ending of the discomfort the previous dose caused. You never rise above the normal of a person who never feeds it. You just briefly stop feeling low.

Once you see this split, fear shrinks. You are not fighting a mighty addiction that will make you suffer. You are starving a tiny Imp that is already dying. The work is not in resisting the Imp. The work is in seeing through the script that tells you to feed it.

You do not need to fight yourself. You need to stop feeding a creature that dies on its own when unfed.

### WATCH YOUR BODY EAT

Let me give you something to watch, not something to believe.

In this first third of our work we are installing the world as it really is, so that later demolitions have ground to stand on. Part of that world is your own body, your instinct, and real food — not as a slogan, but as a concrete encounter you can already have.

Tonight or tomorrow, eat a simple meal when you are properly hungry. Not when the clock says so, not when the packet calls, just when your body signals it is ready. Notice what hungry actually feels like before the Imp dresses it up. It is not an emergency. It is a mild, friendly signal, a lightness, a little emptiness asking to be filled. Sit with real food — vegetables, fruit, plain food cooked simply — and eat it slowly. Taste it. Notice how your body tells you when satisfaction arrives: the flavours become less urgent, the hunger quiets, you feel steady and complete.

That is your body speaking. It is not confused. It knows hunger and satisfaction without needing BAD SUGAR to interpret them.

Now compare that to the grazing you know so well: the quick sweet eaten when you were not hungry, the lift then fast drop, the sticky mouth, the need for another hit an hour later. That second pattern is not hunger. It is the Sugar Trap talking. It is the Imp being fed by the trap's ideas.

We have been taught not to trust hunger, to fear it, to fix it quickly with a dose. The fact is, hunger you can trust is gentle and reliable. It comes and goes. It does not shout. It does not need sugar to answer it. Real food satisfies it fully, and when you eat it you feel well — not wired, not crashed, just well.

Keep this encounter with you. We will do all our later work on this ground. Body, instinct and real food are the authorities we will return to, not rules we impose.

And as you watch, notice how little drama there is in not feeding BAD SUGAR between meals when you are not hungry. The Imp whispers, but you are eating what your body asked for, and its voice becomes background noise. That is a preview of freedom: not a life of resisting, just a life of listening to the right signal.

### THE CONTRACT THAT MAKES THIS EASY

You may be thinking, this all sounds logical, but how do I actually do it?

You do it by not turning this book into another test of strength. The method that failed you before asked you to be strong while still believing BAD SUGAR gives you something. Of course you failed. Anyone would. Strong will to persist in something that goes against your instinct is not weakness — but it is being spent on the wrong job.

My method asks you to do one thing while you read: follow the instructions. That is why I give them as clear, spoken commands at the climax of each chapter. They are not suggestions. They are the counter-brainwashing itself. Each one closes one more escape route for the trap.

You have already kept the first three: you kept an open mind, you carried on eating as normal, and you began with elation. Keep them still.

Now add the next:

FOLLOW ALL THE INSTRUCTIONS
All you have to do is follow all the instructions.

That is the whole job. Not to be strong. Not to decide today to be perfect. Not to fight the Imp. Just to follow all the instructions, in order, as they are given. If a line later contradicts something you have heard before — a diet rule, a tip from someone who stopped by suffering through it, a magazine that told you to cut down — ignore that advice. If it keeps the Sugar Trap alive, it keeps you trapped.

Why does this matter so much? Because the trap survives on partial obedience. The little limbic switchboard in your primitive brain has many cords. You can unplug most of them and the machine still hums. One remaining idea — I can have just a treat when I deserve one — is enough to keep the whole web intact. That is why total following is easy and partial following is hard. When you follow all the instructions you unplug every cord at once. The Imp starves. The script loses its voice.

Do not try to cut down yet. Do not try to be good. Keep investigating with me. We have named the Sugar Trap and we have named the Sugar Imp that lives inside it. We have seen how the web was woven strand by strand and why you felt fine while it was woven. We have split the trivial physical gnaw from the big belief that feeds it. We have watched your body eat and remembered that it knows what to do without BAD SUGAR.

You came into this chapter saying, I choose this and I could stop if I wanted. You leave it knowing you were conned into a trap that removed choice while calling your trapped normal. That knowing is not miserable. It is marvellous. Because a trap you can see is a trap you can walk out of — easily, immediately and permanently — once the belief that holds it up is gone.

We will pull that belief apart together in the next chapter, by asking a simple, unsettling question: what does BAD SUGAR actually do for you?

SUMMARY

- What felt like free choice was life inside a trap — you grazed, hid the packet and told yourself you could stop because the web was invisible.
- The Sugar Trap was built gradually, strand by strand, so you felt free between strands until the day you tried to leave.
- The physical craving is tiny and brief; call it the Sugar Imp — a small, starving creature that dies when not fed.
- The powerful pull comes from brainwashing that tells you BAD SUGAR is pleasure, reward or fuel; that script feeds the Imp.
- Your body, instinct and real food already know hunger and satisfaction without BAD SUGAR.
- Freedom comes from following all the instructions, not from strength or willpower.
