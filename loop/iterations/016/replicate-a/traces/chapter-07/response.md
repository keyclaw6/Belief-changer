Chapter 7
HOW THE TRAP WORKS

IN THIS CHAPTER — The tight shoes, A single loop from dose to want, The dip after the sweet, The wrapper that calls before hunger

*The lift you feel is not a lift at all — it is the brief relief from a low that same sweet created.*

### THE TIGHT SHOES

Imagine a man who wears tight shoes all day on purpose.

Not because he likes tight shoes. Because he has been told, since he was a child, that taking them off is one of life's little pleasures. He laces them cruelly tight in the morning, hobbles through his day, and then in the afternoon, in front of the fridge, he kicks them off and sighs. Ahhh. Relief floods his feet. He closes his eyes. He believes the shoes gave him pleasure.

We would not call that man foolish. We would say he has been conned about where the pleasure came from.

BAD SUGAR is those tight shoes.

We put them on with every dose. The hit is not subtle — the bright sweetness flooding the tongue, the rush of sticky flavour, the quick buzz that feels like energy arriving. For minutes it does feel like relief. The mouth coats. The tension eases.

Then we tighten them again for hours.

That is the trick you have lived for years and never named. The relief you call a lift is not pleasure added to a normal day. It is discomfort subtracted for a few minutes — discomfort the last dose manufactured. You never rise above how a person who never touched BAD SUGAR feels all day. You sink below it, and then a mouthful pulls you briefly back toward normal, and then you sink again. The rescue is the perpetrator.

You can test this against your own mouth. Think of the sweet you swear gives you energy. Twenty minutes after it, how does your mouth feel? Sticky. A little sour. A thirst you cannot quite name. Heavy eyelids, a flatness behind the eyes, a hunt that has already started again. That is not satisfaction. That is the shoes tightening.

I know this is hard to accept. Of course it is. For years you have been told sugar is treat, fuel, reward. You have felt the sigh of relief and called it pleasure because who would not? Stay with me while we trace one single loop together, in daylight, without blame, and you will see why you never needed willpower at all.

That loop is the Sugar Trap.

### ONE LOOP FROM DOSE TO WANT

Let us follow one ordinary loop, exactly as you live it, with no theory added.

You have a dose. It might be biscuits with tea, a chocolate bar at three o'clock, the little sweets you keep for after supper. Call it what it is: a dose. Not a treat. A fix for a gnawing you already feel but have learned to misread as hunger, boredom, or need.

For three or four minutes you feel relief. The fact is each hit re-triggers a wanting circuit in the brain — it fires want again even as it soothes it. The wanting feels like liking, but it is not. It is the circuit being switched on by the very thing you believe switches it off.

Then the dip follows. This is not imagination. The fact is after the hit there is a real, measurable dip — the lift drains, a low settles, an opposing unease rises. It is mild. It is not a detox sentence and it does not mean you are broken. If you have a medical condition affected by what you eat, talk to your clinician before changing what you eat. For the general reader it is a small, empty gnawing, a little insecurity, a slight edginess that whispers you need something. That whisper is already dying if you do not feed it.

Before you feel that you need anything, a cue fires. A wrapper sound. The smell from the bakery as you pass. The time on the clock. The vending machine humming. The sight of the packet you told yourself was for the children. The brain has learned that packet equals relief, so want spikes at the sight long before the stomach is empty. You feel pulled before you feel hungry. That is not character. That is a cue habit, trained.

And so want returns. Not as a mighty roar. As a nudge. Have something quick and sweet to get you through. Just one bite. Just this once. That nudge is not you reasoning. That is the story being fed to a small creature inside.

You give it another dose, and the loop resets. Brief relief, dip, cue, want. Brief relief, dip, cue, want. Never once do you float above the line a free person lives on all day without thinking. You pay to ride a rollercoaster that only ever returns you to the queue.

Ask yourself: when in that loop did you feel better than a person who had never taken the first dose? You did not. You felt worse, then slightly less worse for minutes, then worse again.

It is the other way around.

Once you see the loop, the whole language changes. Moderation is not balance; it is intermittent access that makes the next hit precious. You know this pattern intimately: I just crave sweets and when I eat them, I feel like I cant stop and I just ate the box after promising one bite. That one-bite-becomes-the-box is not weakness. It is the loop doing exactly what a schedule of now-you-can, now-you-cant teaches a brain to do — binge when the door opens. Leave the door ajar and the binge lives. Close it, and the pattern starves.

### THE DIP YOU MISTOOK FOR HUNGER

We need to name the dip plainly because it owns your afternoons.

It feels like hunger. It feels like low energy. It feels like I will not concentrate unless I have something sweet. But it follows the dose; it does not precede it in a clean body. Real hunger, as you just watched on that ordinary plate, is hollow and calm and waits. The dip is restless and specific. It wants BAD SUGAR, not food. It is agitated, not empty. It arrives an hour or two after the last sweet, exactly when the body is processing the previous spike and asking for steadiness.

The fact is that spike is insulin working, and that work can create the slump you blame on needing more sugar. You think you need sugar because you slumped, when you slumped because you had sugar. Everyday slumps often happen without any clinical low blood sugar at all — they are the body's protest at the ride, not proof you need to stay on it.

And here is the relief in the same breath: that physical part is mild but well-defined. Small. Brief. Not a mighty monster that will wrestle you for weeks. When people who live on highly palatable food cut it, any physical grumble peaks in two to five days then passes, and many feel little at all. I do not want you to stop because you fear a little dip, and I do not want you to fear the dip itself. It is tiny, passing, and already dying if you do not refuel it. The fear of it has been larger than it ever was.

What felt large was not that gnawing. It was what you believed about it.

You believed enjoyment was fading and you needed a bigger portion — family size — to get the same pleasure. You believed wrappers were calling you because you loved sweets. The fact is enjoyment does fade — the wanting circuit crowds out the liking — while cue-driven wanting spikes at the very sight and smell of the packet. Less pleasure, more pull. That is not mystery. That is the loop tightening: each dose blunts satisfaction a little and sensitizes the cue a little, so the strongest feeling left is I want it, not I enjoy it.

I know. It is uncomfortable to realize a pleasure you defended was mostly relief from the discomfort it caused. It feels like someone telling you the shoes were tight on purpose. It is also the moment the shame falls off. You were not weak. You were loyally misreading signals the trap taught you to misread.

### THE SMALL CREATURE AND THE LARGE CON

So let us separate what is small from what is huge, because you have had them the wrong way round for years.

The small part is the Sugar Imp.

That is the Sugar Imp — the trivial physical gnawing, the little parasite that complains when unfed and dies when starved. It is not powerful. It cannot make you act. It can only whisper, and it whispers less each day you do not feed it. You can rejoice in its complaints; they are death throes, not commands. When you feel that faint, empty, slightly insecure nudge an hour after a dose, smile. It is the Imp starving. It has no say over who you are.

The huge part is the belief system that feeds it. Call it what it is: the Sweet Con. The brainwashing about pleasure, treat, fuel and reward that tells you that whisper means you are deprived and must answer it with BAD SUGAR. The Sweet Con is not in your mouth. It is in your mind, installed by family, by adverts, by every film-night bowl that taught you sweets are love. It says one will not hurt. It says you have been good this week so you deserve it. It says natural sugar is fine so this dose does not count. It says you need it for your brain.

Every time you obey the Con, you feed the Imp. Every time you feed the Imp, you give the Con new proof. Dose, relief, dip, cue, want — fed, proven, strengthened.

That is why cravings felt like character. They are not. They are a small physical whisper endlessly amplified by a big mental lie. You do not need to crush the whisper with force. You need to stop believing the lie that feeds it.

This is also why you cannot keep a special one. Imagine a limbic switchboard with many cords. You have twenty cords plugged in that keep the wanting alive — I need it for afternoons, it is my reward, it makes the meal, it is harmless, just one on weekends. Unplug nineteen and leave one and the machine still runs. Leave the thought this one special sweet is a genuine pleasure or treat and the whole Trap stays lit. Freedom is not about controlling doses. It is about pulling all the cords: no dose is a pleasure, none is a reward, none is needed, none is harmless play.

I am not asking you to white-knuckle your way past wrappers. I am asking you to see wrappers for what they now are: cues that fire want because they once meant relief, not because you need them. Seeing it cuts the wire before willpower is ever called. You are warm to yourself and harsh to the trap that built that wiring.

Do you feel the inversion click? Not I crave therefore I need, but I was given it, it made a low, and that low was sold back to me as need. Not it lifts me above normal, but it drops me and then rents me my own normal for minutes. Not I am weak because I cannot stop, but the Sweet Con kept the Imp alive by making me believe stopping would be deprivation.

Once seen, you cannot un-see it. And you do not need to change from fear of what BAD SUGAR might do to your body years from now to want this freedom now. You want it because the trap is ugly today, in this loop, in this mouth, in this hour — and escape is marvellous today.

If it contradicts escape, it keeps you trapped.

SUMMARY

- The lift from BAD SUGAR is not pleasure added to a normal day but brief relief from a low the previous dose created, never rising above how a free person feels without it.
- One loop runs dose, brief relief, dip, cue, want, and returns — the dip is mild and the cue fires before hunger, so the pull you feel is a trained habit, not a character flaw or a clinical sentence.
- The physical gnawing is small and soon passes if not refed, while the belief that feeds it is large; stopping happens by correcting the belief, and the body then settles on its own.
- Leaving any single justification intact keeps the whole trap lit, because each remaining belief keeps feeding the small creature that keeps asking for another dose.
