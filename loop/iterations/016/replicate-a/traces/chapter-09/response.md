Chapter 9
THE SWEETEST EVENING

IN THIS CHAPTER — The sofa and the low light, The bowl in the middle, The hands reaching, The sticky after-mouth, The same evening without the bowl

*The sweetness you remember was the evening itself — the bowl was only sneaking a ride.*

### THE EVENING YOU CALL PROOF

You have kept one evening safe from every argument so far.

Not the rushed biscuit at your desk. Not the packet you hide. That one you will concede is a trap. But the other one — the good one. Saturday. The sofa. The film you chose together. The big bowl in the middle, glossy wrappers catching the lamplight, hands dipping in without looking. You tell me, this is different. This is love. This is family. This is the treat we deserve.

I know. I kept that evening too.

We tell ourselves — celebrating, rewarding myself, going to the cinema, etc with chocolate — and we mean it tenderly. We are not talking about hunger then. We are talking about making a moment warmer. After a hard week you say, I've been good, I deserve this. After a good week you say, we are celebrating, this is the point. The thought arrives complete and kind, as if denying it would be denying love itself.

So let us not argue about biscuits. Let us take your best case, exactly as you love it, and watch it carefully. If the trap can survive anywhere, it will survive here. If it cannot survive here, it has nowhere left to hide.

### THE BOWL IN THE MIDDLE

Sit in it with me, as it really happens.

The light is low. The film starts. The bowl is in the middle, deliberately — so everyone can reach it. That reach is part of the ritual. Hands cross, fingers brush, someone laughs and says save some for me. For the first few mouthfuls there is a hit — sweet, loud, immediate. The tongue lights up. You feel you have added something to the evening.

Watch what happens next, because you have felt it a hundred times and called it normal.

By the second handful the pleasure is thinner. You are not tasting more, you are chasing the first taste. The sweetness is less bright, the mouth is sticky, the hand goes again not for more pleasure but to get back the pleasure that just faded. That is not your imagination. The wanting circuit fires at the cue — the wrapper colour, the crinkle, the sight of the bowl — before hunger has any say, and each hit re-triggers the want for the next one while the enjoyment itself gets crowded and blunted. The more you feed it, the less it gives, the more you want.

So you keep reaching. Not because each sweet is delightful, but because each sweet promises to restore the delight the last one dulled.

Then the film ends. The lights come up. Stay in that moment. What is actually there in the body? A full, slightly sick feeling. Teeth filmed. Thirst underneath. A faint heaviness, a dip in mood you did not have when the evening began. You tell yourself you are satisfied, but what you feel is not satisfaction. It is the tax being collected. You paid the evening and now the bowl takes its fee.

And tomorrow you will remember the evening as sweet. But what was sweet? The company. The warmth under the blanket. The story that made you laugh or held your breath. The laughter of the person next to you. To that, BAD SUGAR added nothing except a loud hit that faded fast, a sticky mouth, and the need to reach again. It was only ever sneaking a ride on a pleasure that was already there.

You might say, but I do like sweet flavour. Of course — human tongues taste sweet. That is not the question. The question is whether that bowl on that sofa on that night was a genuine pleasure or treat. When you see what it actually did — lifted nothing above the ordinary baseline of that warm evening, dulled its own reward, re-fired the want, left a film and a dip — the answer is plain. The evening was the pleasure. The bowl was the toll.

It is the other way around. We thought the sweets made the evening. The evening made the sweets seem sweet.

### A TRICK YOUR EYES CAN PROVE

You may still feel, yes, but I felt the pleasure. I know what I felt.

I trust that feeling. So let us let feeling prove it can be wrong without shaming you.

Do this with me, right now.

Look at the two sweets on the table in front of you — I will draw them in words. Two circles, side by side, identical in size, both wrapped in the same red foil. One sits on a big empty plate. The other sits in the middle of a crowded bowl, surrounded by other sweets. Which looks bigger?

The one on the empty plate looks bigger. Obviously. You would bet on it.

Now measure them. They are the same.

You felt certainty. Certainty was not truth. Your eyes did their job perfectly — and still were fooled by context. The bowl made the sweet look precious. The emptiness made the single one look lonely. The feeling was real; the judgment was false.

That is exactly what BAD SUGAR does on film night. The context — love, warmth, togetherness — makes the bowl look like the source of the warmth. Your feeling is real — you did feel warm. Your explanation is false. The warmth came from the situation, the body at ease, the moment you were already in. The bowl only sat in the middle of that context and took the credit, the way the crowded bowl makes the middle sweet look smaller than it is.

We have all been fooled by context. That does not make you foolish. It makes you human, with a brain that reads the room quickly and sometimes reads it wrong. Once you see the trick, you cannot unsee it, and the next time context tries it, you smile because you already know.

Try the evening again, in your mind, without the judgment.

Same sofa. Same people. Same film. The lamp the same. You are hungry when you arrive, so you eat something real first — simple, satisfying food that ends in a clear, quiet pause where you have had enough. Your mouth is clean. Your body is steady. Now the film begins. No bowl in the middle. Nothing to reach for, nothing to chase, nothing to film your teeth.

What happens? No hunt. No sticky dip after the credits. No argument about who had more. Your hands stay still and your attention stays on the story. You laugh the same, you lean the same, you feel the same warmth — because the warmth was never in the wrappers. When the film ends you are simply there, warm, awake, with no fee to pay.

If you think you would miss the sweets in that scene, ask yourself what exactly you would miss. The first bright hit that fades by the third sweet? The crowding that makes each next one less enjoyable? The cue that fires before you are hungry and calls it a craving? The dip after? That is not a pleasure being removed. That is a tax being lifted.

I have had that second evening with my own family, after years of the first kind, and I can tell you plain: it is sweeter. Not because I told myself it should be. Because there was nothing dulling the taste, nothing interrupting the story, nothing turning enjoyment into wanting. Real food is sweet when your tongue is clean. Company is sweet when your attention is not being pulled to the bowl.

That was your strongest case — the deserved reward, the celebration, the love expressed in chocolate. We took it exactly as you treasure it and looked closer. Every drop of pleasure in it belonged to the evening itself — to people, to story, to warmth, to a body finally left to feel satisfied. BAD SUGAR added no pleasure. It borrowed the evening's pleasure, faded quickly, made you reach again, and left the dip you later called needing more.

You are not deprived without it on that sofa. You are free on that sofa, with the whole evening finally yours.

SUMMARY

- The film-night bowl feels like the point of a warm evening, but the warmth belongs to the people, the story and the moment together, not to BAD SUGAR.
- Each sweet gives a brief loud hit that quickly dulls while the wanting circuit is re-triggered, so hands reach again to chase what faded.
- After the film the body is left with a sticky mouth and a dip, while the remembered sweetness is reassigned to the evening that was sweet on its own.
- The same evening without the bowl, with real food when hungry, is not bleaker; it is calmer, cleaner and fully enjoyed because nothing is crowding enjoyment or collecting a fee afterwards.
