Chapter 12
BEFORE THE LAST BITE

IN THIS CHAPTER — The Tuesday evening that will prove it, The shop queue on a dull commute, The desk drawer at three o'clock, A kitchen told plain with numbers on the packet, The first few days without the rollercoaster

*You do not need the perfect moment to be ready — you are ready, and the moment is about to prove it.*

### WILL IT WORK FOR ME?

That is the sentence sitting quietly behind everything you have just read.

You have followed the plates as we turned them over. You have seen cut-down make the thing precious. You have seen the empty cupboard organise the whole day. You have seen natural is not different, and tomorrow never comes, and weaning stretches the wanting. And still, behind the seeing, a small voice says: yes, but will it work for me.

I hear you. I would have said the same.

We all stood exactly there, on the threshold, feeling clever for spotting every loophole and suddenly shy about our own freedom. We who have spent years grazing, hiding the packet, telling ourselves just one bite and watching the box empty, we who have paid ourselves with sweets for being good, we are the very people who think this sort of escape happens to someone else. Someone stricter. Someone stronger. Someone who does not like chocolate as much as we do.

The fact is you like it no more than anyone else in the trap liked it. The liking was relief. The wanting was the schedule. And the escape does not belong to a special type of person. It belongs to anyone who sees the trick.

So let me not argue at you. Let me show you one ordinary life, told plain, with numbers and mouth-feel and times of day still on it. Not data. A life. One like yours, until it wasn't.

### A LIFE TOLD PLAIN

I think of a woman who wrote to me after she found the method — I will keep her name to myself, but not a detail of how she lived, because you will recognise the day.

She was thirty-eight, two children, office job that kept her at a desk until five. For twelve years she had told herself she grazed, not binged, that she did not have a problem because she did not keep sweets in the house. In the house she was strict. At the shop by the bus stop she was not. At work she was strict until three. At three she was not.

Her numbers, because she counted them when she stopped counting: a vending bar at 11 a.m. most days, two biscuits with tea at 3 p.m. that became four, then the bag from the drawer, then the thing she did on the way home — she called it the car tax. A family bag of chocolate buttons bought for the children, half gone before she parked, wrappers pushed under the seat. In the evening, after the kitchen was clean, a sleeve of cookies or the end of a loaf with jam while standing up. She reckoned, when she added it later and winced, at somewhere between six and nine hits of BAD SUGAR a day, every day, with Saturdays worse because of the film night bowl in the middle. She had tried what we all tried: no sweets Monday to Friday, then Saturday she ate the week. Cut down to dark chocolate only. No chocolate in the house. Every rule made the next day more exciting.

Look at the sensory string she described, because it is yours too. The quick and sweet hit at the desk — the crack of the bar, sugar on the tongue, a bright lift for ten minutes while the email answers itself, you feel you can get through. Then, exactly as she put it in her own words before she had my words for it, the short term lift which will inevitably be followed by a fast drop. Sticky mouth. Heavy eyelids. The hum of the vending machine again, the hollow just behind the breastbone that feels like hunger but is not hunger, that little empty, slightly insecure nag that says one more will get you back up. She fed it. It quieted for twenty minutes. It returned louder.

The second pattern she knew by heart. One bite becomes the box. I just crave sweets and when I eat them, I feel like I can't stop — that was her sentence, weeks before she read it here. She would break a single biscuit in half to prove she could have one. The half called the other half. The other half called the bag. Once I start I cannot stop was not a confession of weakness. It was an accurate description of feeding a loop that is designed to be fed in full or not at all.

The third pattern was the love story. She rewarded herself. Celebrating, rewarding myself, going to the cinema, etc with chocolate — her Friday. Hard day, good day, both were paid the same way. A treat when you deserve one, I have been good this week. The chocolate was not extra to the evening. It was the evening. Or so it felt, until she saw it.

She did not come to me brave. She came sceptical and tired and a little embarrassed that a book might help where diets had not. She read with the drawer still full. She did not cut down. She followed the one rule that matters while reading — keep going as normal — and let the belief change first.

Here is what changed, in her words when she wrote the week after:

She ate breakfast when she was hungry. Eggs, toast that was real bread, an apple. She noticed — really noticed — that hunger arrived as a mild, friendly signal, not an emergency, and that it went, satisfied, after she had eaten enough. The food tasted sweeter than she remembered. Not sugary. Sweet. Like food.

The 3 p.m. moment came the next Tuesday and she expected a battle. Instead she felt the faint gnaw she had always called need and saw it for what it was: the little creature complaining because it was not being fed. The Sugar Imp already dying, already quieter. She laughed, she told me, because it was so small. She did not fight it. She did not fill it. She watched it fade while she finished her tea. The drop she had blamed on needing sugar did not arrive that day, because she had not ridden the lift.

That Saturday she did the film night. Same sofa. Same children. Same bowl — only now the bowl held popcorn with salt and butter and apple slices. The film was funny. The children leaned. She leant. At no point did she feel she was enduring the evening. At one point she thought: this is the warm evening I was paying the trap to give me. The sweets had been sneaking a ride on the warmth and claiming they were the warmth.

And the numbers did what numbers do when the trap is not fed. The first three days she felt flat at moments, a little empty, a little restless in the afternoon — not strong, not terrible, and gone by day four. If you have any medical condition affected by what you eat, talk to your clinician as you make changes; I want it plain that any physical unease is brief and small and starves if not refed. By the end of the first week she was not counting. By the end of the second she had stopped wanting to count. The weight off her shoulders was not in her handbag; it was in not thinking about the handbag.

She wrote: I thought I would miss it for the rest of my evening. I feel relief it is gone for the rest of my evening. That is the whole move.

That is not a superpower story. She did not become a different person. She did not start running at dawn or craving vegetables every second. She got ordinary life back without the dip that made ordinary feel so hard. She got her taste back. She got her evenings back. She got the shop on the corner back as just a shop.

I tell you this not to impress you but to make the next step feel natural. Because you are not about to do something unusual. You are about to do what people like you — people who said I just can't stop once I start and I need something sweet to get me through — do when they see the trick. You stop waiting for a perfect day.

### THE ORDINARY PROOF COMING TO YOU

Do not wait for a holiday. Do not wait for less stress. Do not wait until the birthday is over or the project is done. The trap loves waiting. Waiting is its best courtship.

Choose any ordinary proof you already possess.

Your boring commute tomorrow. The same bus, the same queue at the same shop where the bright packets are stacked like medicine in cheerful boxes. You will stand there, not because you have arranged to avoid it, you will inhabit it, and you will see the queue with different eyes. Not envy. Pity. They are not indulging. They are paying to ride the same rollercoaster you have stepped off. The thought will come — just one can't hurt — and you will hear it for what it is, a line we heard before it spoke. You will smile that you heard it. You will buy what you came for and walk out feeling lighter than when you walked in. That is freedom proving itself in a place that used to prove the trap.

Your Tuesday evening. Same kitchen, same table, same tiredness after the day. You will feel hunger arrive, mild and clean, and you will eat real food when you are hungry until you are satisfied, noticing the pause where satisfaction arrives. No counting. No emergency. The quick and sweet lift will not be needed to get you through because you will not have dug the hole that needed filling. The fast drop will not follow, because you will not have taken the lift. You will wash the plates and realise you did not think about the cupboard once.

Your three o'clock. The vending machine will still hum. The drawer will still be there. The sticky mouth and heavy eyelids that used to say more sugar will be understood the other way around: the dose created the dip. The dip is not a command. It is the death throes of something tiny that was fed too long. I want you to revel in that faint complaint. It is the sound of the Imp starving. It is not need. It is ending. Do not feed it and it passes. Feed it and you guarantee it returns.

You do not need to feel heroic in those moments. You only need to notice. The relief is in the noticing.

If you feel, even after the plates were turned, a tug that says you are not ready — if you still guard one special occasion as proof you cannot be free — stop and go back to the start and read again with an open mind. Not from the middle. From the beginning, as if for the first time. The readiness you want is not confidence before you see. It is the seeing itself. When you see it plain you will feel what people before you have felt on the page before this one: a little impatience, a little excitement, a champing at the bit. That is your signal. Not nerves. Not dread. A quiet eagerness to take the last ordinary dose and make the vow.

And if you skipped here? If you opened this book at this chapter to see whether it might work for you before reading it? Close this chapter and start at the first page and follow all the instructions in order. This chapter cannot do its job without the ground the early chapters laid. The promise at the start — keep an open mind and carry on as normal while you read — was there to let the belief change before the behaviour is asked to change. You cannot borrow the ending without the seeing that earns it.

You have closed every escape route we left open on purpose before you reached for it. You have felt the lift then the drop and recognised it as the trap's own weather, not your need. You have heard one bite becomes the box and the deserved reward in your own dialect and heard each answered. You have heard that the brief, mild flatness of the first two to five days, if you feel it at all, is small, preliminary, and passes — not a sentence, not a fight, not a reason to taper or delay.

So the only belief left to correct is the small, lingering one that says this will work for others but not for me.

Look at that thought plain.

You are not other. You are the pattern. The grazing, the hiding, the quick and sweet to get through, the box that empties when permission is given, the treat when you deserve one — these were not quirks. They were the trap doing its job, the same job it did in lives counted and lives uncounted. If the trap was the same, the key is the same. And the key is not in your character. It is in the seeing.

I do not want you to change from fear. The facts about load and teeth and the slow cooking of the ordinary have done their work and are now set down. I want you to change from relief. The relief that there is nothing to give up. The relief that ordinary, without the spike and crash, is marvellous and you get it immediately. The relief that the faint gnaw, if it comes, is a tiny thing dying and you can rejoice at a dead enemy, never mourn a lost friend.

Do you feel it? That little lift that is not sugar — the lightness that says, go on then. Let me prove it on a dull day in a shop queue.

That is readiness.

Hold it. We are one chapter from the vow, and you will not wait to be free — you already are, the moment you see.

SUMMARY

- The belief that this will work for others but not for me is the last loop to close; ordinary people with the same grazing and one-bite-becomes-the-box pattern have found the escape is easy when the trick is seen.
- The three lived patterns — quick lift then fast drop, one bite carrying the box, and paying yourself with sweets because you deserve it — are not character but the trap's schedule revealing itself.
- Any physical flatness after stopping is mild and brief, often two to five days if felt at all, and starves quickly if not refed — not a reason to taper or to wait for a perfect time.
- Freedom proves itself in ordinary moments you already own — the dull commute, the shop queue, the Tuesday evening, the three o'clock desk — where you notice wanting does not need to be fought or fed.
- If the eagerness to be free is not yet there, the signal is to re-read from the start with an open mind; when you feel champing at the bit, you are ready for the last ordinary dose.
