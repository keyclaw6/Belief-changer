Chapter 1
HOW TO READ THIS BOOK AND WALK FREE

**IN THIS CHAPTER**
- The staircase, the landing window, the breath that tells the truth
- The consulting chair, the full ashtray, the man who stopped without a struggle
- The reading contract, the open mind, the permission to smoke while you read

*You can stop smoking without misery, and the only thing you need to change first is what you believe cigarettes do for you.*

### You Are Right To Be Wary

I know what you are feeling as you open this book.

You have been here before. Another promise. Another method. Another voice telling you what you already know — that you would love to be free — as if telling you were enough.

So part of you is hopeful, and part of you is braced. Braced for the lecture. Braced for the photographs. Braced for being told how weak you are, how much money you waste, how much harm you do. Braced, most of all, for being told that to stop you will have to go through hell.

“If it sounds too good to be true, it probably is.”

That is what you are thinking, is it not?

Good. I welcome that thought. I would be disappointed if you did not think it. Any smoker who has lit the next cigarette within an hour of swearing it would be the last has earned the right to be sceptical.

I was that smoker.

I am not a doctor wagging a finger from a clean desk. I am not a theorist who read about smoking in a textbook. I smoked heavily, daily, for years. The first thought in the morning, the last thing at night, and a chain of doses in between to get me through stress, meals, breaks, boredom, concentration, celebration — everything. I tried to stop in all the usual ways and failed in all the usual ways, and each failure left me more convinced that life without cigarettes would be flat and grey and impossible to enjoy.

And then I got free. Not after months of torment. Not after white knuckles and clenched teeth. I walked out of the trap and I have never missed it, never wanted it back, never looked at another smoker with envy. I have since spent my life helping other smokers do the same, smokers as hardened and as hopeless as I was.

That is why I can speak to you with complete confidence. I know how you feel because I felt it. I know what you fear because I feared it. And I know that what I am about to tell you will sound outrageous until you see it for yourself.

So let me set the tone at once. This is not a lecture. There is no doom here. There is no need to feel miserable while you read. In fact I ask the opposite of you. I want you to feel excited from the very first page. You are about to go on an exciting adventure, and at the end of it you will be free.

Does that feel impossible? Ask yourself this. If freedom really were impossible, why are you still reading? Why did you pick up this book at all? And if some smokers do get free — completely free, happy to be non-smokers — what makes you so certain you cannot be one of them?

### The Stairs Don't Lie

Let me take you to a staircase.

Not a clinic. Not a laboratory. An ordinary staircase in an ordinary building. Perhaps an office, perhaps a station, perhaps the stairs up to your own flat.

You climb them with a bag in one hand and a phone in the other. By the second flight you are breathing through your mouth. By the third you have to pause at the landing window, pretending to look out while you catch your breath. Your chest feels tight. Your heart is thumping in your ears.

Beside you a non-smoker friend of about your age, no fitter than you, no younger, climbs without a pause. He breathes easy. He does not notice the stairs at all. He is still talking mid-sentence while you nod and try not to wheeze.

You know that moment. We all know it.

Now let me ask you plainly. In that moment on the landing, did you feel like a free person enjoying a genuine pleasure? Or did you feel, just for a second, like a person in a cage who had paid for the cage himself?

Be honest. No one is judging you. I stood on that landing a hundred times. I told myself it was nothing. Getting older. Unfit. Busy. Anything rather than admit what my own body was shouting at me.

Your body is not your enemy. Your body is the most honest friend you have. It does not moralize. It does not lecture. It simply tries to breathe, to taste, to keep you alive and alert. When smoke fills it, it coughs, it tightens, it struggles for clean air. That struggle on the stairs is not a character flaw. It is authority. It is your own lungs telling you the truth that your mind has been trained not to hear.

Here is the question that changes everything. If cigarettes relaxed you and helped you and pleased you, why did they leave you breathless on an ordinary staircase while a non-smoker walked up laughing? What exactly was the pleasure doing for you there?

And here is the second question. If you had never lit that first cigarette, would you be standing on that landing gasping now? Or would you be the one still talking mid-sentence?

There is only one honest answer. You know what it is.

I am not trying to frighten you. I do not want you to stop because you are frightened of the stairs. Fear never freed anyone for long. I show you the stairs because your body already knows you were not born to breathe like that, and remembering it makes the rest of this book easy to hear. Relief is already waiting on the other side of clear seeing.

### I Was You

Let me tell you who is speaking to you, because you have a right to know.

I started as most of us start. Curiosity. Showing off. Belonging. The first cigarettes tasted foul. They made me dizzy and sick. No child picks up a first cigarette because it is marvellous. We persist because we are told, over and over, that there is something adult and relaxing and sociable in it, and because after a little while we begin to feel edgy without one and mistake that edginess for proof we need one.

That is how we all fall in. Not because we are stupid. Not because we are weak. Because we were conned by phoney information before we knew enough to question it.

I became the classic daily smoker. Stress? Light up. Meal finished? Light up. Phone call? Break? Drink? Waiting for a bus? Light up. My day was not a day with cigarettes in it. It was cigarettes with a day fitted around them.

And like you, I tried to get out. Patches. Gum. Cutting down. Stern talks to myself in the mirror. Promises to my family. I lasted hours, days, once a few weeks. Then the little voice would start: “You have been so good. One won’t hurt. You can handle one now.” And I would have one. And within days I was back to full rate, hating myself more than before.

Do you know that voice? “One won’t hurt.” “I’ll stop tomorrow.” “Now is not the right time.”

Whose voice do you think that is? Yours? Or the voice of the trap speaking through you?

After each failure I believed what most smokers believe: that I lacked will. That other people had some toughness I did not. That I would smoke for the rest of my life and die chained to a habit I despised.

Then, almost by accident, I began to look not at why I should stop, but at why I smoked. Not the health warnings. Not the money. The supposed benefits. Relaxation. Concentration. Pleasure. Company. Nerve-steadying. One by one I examined them in my own daily life, while I was still smoking, and one by one they fell apart. There was nothing there. Only a trick of timing that made it look as if the cigarette gave what the previous cigarette had taken away.

When I saw that clearly, something extraordinary happened. I smoked my last cigarette — not in tears, not in terror, but with relief and even joy — and I walked free. The desire left. Not after three weeks. At once. From day one I was happy to be a non-smoker.

I tell you this not to boast. I tell you because you need to know that ease is possible. You have been told so often that stopping is agony that you assume agony is the price. It is not. The agony comes from believing you are losing a friend. When you see you are escaping a con, there is no agony. There is only clean air and a wonderful sense of getting your life back.

Now you may ask: if it is so easy, why does everyone say it is so hard? For the same reason you once believed cigarettes calmed your nerves. Because everyone was taught the same story, and a story repeated by everyone begins to sound like fact. It is not fact. It is brainwashing, and brainwashing dissolves the moment you look straight at it.

### Nothing To Fear From Reading On

Let me remove the dread that makes smokers put books like this down.

You do not have to stop while you read. You do not have to cut down. You do not have to smoke your “last” cigarette with trembling hands on page one and then suffer through the rest. While you read, go on smoking exactly as normal. Smoke when you want. Enjoy your little parties as you always do. I ask nothing heroic of you yet.

Why? Because this book changes what you want before it asks you to change what you do. How could you investigate fairly what cigarettes really do for you if you were already in a panic about doing without them? You cannot. So there will be no panic. Read as a smoker, curious and comfortable, and let the argument do the work.

And what do you risk by reading? A few hours of your time. That is all. Just think about it for a moment and you will see that you have nothing to lose and everything to gain.

On one side, the worst that can happen is you finish the book, you understand smoking better, and you carry on as before. On the other side, the best that can happen — and what happens for the vast majority who finish with an open mind — is you walk free and you never crave cigarettes again. No loss. Only freedom ahead. Health, money, energy, self-respect, mornings without coughing, nights without planning where to hide and smoke — all of that is bonus. The real prize is joy. To look at other smokers and feel not envy but pity and relief. To think of a cigarette and feel glad, deeply glad, that you no longer need it.

Is that not worth a few hours with an open mind?

You may still doubt that stopping can be easy. I understand. “Easily, immediately and permanently” sounds like advertising. It sounded like advertising to me.

But ask yourself a third question. If stopping were truly a matter of toughness, why do the toughest people you know — people who run businesses, raise children, work night shifts, survive grief — still fail to stop by force? And why do some ordinary smokers, no stronger than you, stop overnight and never look back? Could it be that ease has nothing to do with character and everything to do with understanding?

The fact is, when the false belief goes, the struggle goes with it. I mean you can stop easily, immediately and permanently. Not after a year of counting days. Not one day at a time for the rest of your life. At once, as an identity. A non-smoker from the moment the last cigarette goes out, happy to be free from day one.

Hold that thought lightly for now. You do not have to believe it fully yet. You only have to allow that it might be true for you. That permission is enough to begin.

### What Makes This Different

Perhaps you have read other advice. Perhaps friends have told you to use will, to distract yourself, to avoid pubs and coffee and smokers, to chew and suck and patch your way through cravings.

I blame no one who gave you that advice. They meant well. But notice what all of it assumes: that cigarettes give you something real, and that to live without them you must fight yourself forever.

This book assumes the opposite, because the opposite is true. Cigarettes never gave you anything real. They only took. Every so-called benefit you feel will be examined in daylight, in your ordinary life — stress, breaks, meals, nights out — and you will see for yourself where the pleasure really came from. It came from the moment, the pause, the company, the food, the drink, the breath — never from the tube of paper and leaf that was riding along.

We will not rush. We will take each illusion in turn. We will be thorough. A lock with ten tumblers does not open because you picked nine. We will pick them all, and the door will swing wide by itself.

All I need from you is that you read in order, without skipping ahead, and that you let each chapter finish before you borrow another opinion about quitting. Other voices will crowd in — the friend who suffered, the advert for another crutch, the smoker in the pub who swears he enjoys it. Let them wait. Give this argument a fair hearing first. If it is wrong, you will know. If it is right, you will be free.

So here is my contract with you. I will never shame you. I will never talk down to you. I will never ask you to rely on toughness. I will show you, step by step, that there is nothing to lose by stopping and everything to gain by escaping, and I will make it so clear that stopping feels like relief.

In return, I ask one thing, and one thing only at this stage. It is the key that opens every other door.

1. KEEP AN OPEN MIND
Give this book a fair hearing and it will free you.

**SUMMARY**
- Stopping can be easy and joyful from the start, not miserable and endless.
- Your body already tells the truth on ordinary stairs, and that truth is on your side.
- Past failures were not proof you are weak, they were proof the approach was wrong.
- Reading costs you nothing and risks nothing, while freedom offers everything.
- An open and curious mind lets the rest of the book do its work.
