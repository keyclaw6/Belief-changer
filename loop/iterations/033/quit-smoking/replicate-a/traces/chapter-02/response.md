Chapter 2
ARE YOU REALLY CHOOSING THIS

*You never chose to need a cigarette — you were drawn into a trap, and seeing the trap is how you walk free.*

### THE GARAGE DOOR HALF OPEN

Keep that fair hearing open a little longer. Read as a smoker, comfortable and curious, and look with me at one ordinary evening you know by heart.

Winter. Cold on the step. Your partner is inside with the kids. The television is on. You say you are just taking the rubbish out. You slip into the garage and leave the door half open for air. You light fast, puff hard, keep an ear out for footsteps. You check over your shoulder. You stub it quickly, bury the end deep in the bin under a pizza box, spray the air and your coat, chew two mints, wash your hands in cold water, and walk back in smiling as if nothing happened.

We have all lived that small pantomime. The coat shaken at the back door. The window cracked in the car in January while your fingers go numb. The quick spray in the bathroom and the whispered boast that you Febreeze the hell out of myself before anyone notices. The lie about numbers that comes out smooth because you have said it so often. Only five today. Hardly any. The careful mathematics of packs and hours that runs all day underneath everything else. Will this last me till morning. Where can I stand without being seen. Who will smell it on my breath.

I have stood in that garage. I have stood in the rain outside pubs while laughter went on inside without me. I have stood in office doorways and car parks and stairwells, a grown adult hiding a legal habit like a schoolboy hiding a secret. I know the hurry. I know the listening for footsteps. I know the little rush of shame and the quick rearrangement of the face before you walk back in.

Now let me ask you as one smoker to another, with warmth and without a flicker of judgment. Does a free person do that?

If cigarettes relax you and please you and help you cope, why hide the pleasure. You do not hide a good meal. You do not spray a cup of tea. You do not lie to your children about a walk in clean air. You do not stand outside your own home in the cold to enjoy a true friend. Choice does not need an alibi. Joy does not need spray and mints.

I choose to smoke. I enjoy it. Nobody forces me.

Is that what you are saying to yourself right now. Good. Say it out loud. Hold it up to the garage door and see if it fits.

If you choose it freely, why do you apologize for it with every puff. If you enjoy it, why do you hate the hiding more than you love the dose. And if you are in charge, why did you need to divide yourself in two in order to keep it — the smoker outside in the cold and the actor inside on the sofa.

There is only one honest answer. A free choice would not make you lie to the people you love most in order to keep it. A free choice would not make you stand outside your own home like a visitor. Something else is moving you.

The fact is you are caught in the nicotine trap. Not weak. Not foolish. Caught. The trap removed your choice long ago and left you the feeling of choice to keep you quiet.

Think how clean that sentence feels once you let it in. You are not a bad chooser. You are a decent person in a clever catch that calls itself habit. Seeing the bars is not misery. Seeing the bars is the beginning of walking out into clean air and getting on with enjoying your life.

### IF YOU ARE IN CHARGE, WHY DO YOU NEED THIS BOOK

Let me put the plain logic to you gently, because it settles the matter without a single harsh word.

If you wanted to stop a hobby you truly enjoyed, would you need a book on how to stop it. Would you hide it. Would you promise the mirror never again and light within the hour. Would you count the pack at midnight and feel panic rise. Would you swear off in the morning and bargain by lunch.

The mere fact you are holding this book proves you are not freely choosing. I say that with complete warmth. I held the same book in my mind for years. We smokers know the landing window, the garage door, the car window. We know the divided self very well.

Does a chooser smoke in the rain and call it freedom. Does a chooser tell himself one will not hurt about a true pleasure. Does a chooser say I cannot quit now every month for years and still call that choosing. You have lived those questions. You already know what they prove.

Think back to how we all began. No child dreams of garage doors and mints. The first cigarettes tasted foul. They made us dizzy and sick. We coughed. Our eyes watered. There was no pleasure in it. I remember mine — hot, bitter, nauseating — and I persisted. Why. Not because I loved it. Because I was told, over and over, that there was something adult and calming and sociable in it. Because older kids smoked. Because films smoked. Because the break outside looked like belonging.

That is not choice. That is a confidence trick played on phoney information before we knew enough to question it. We chose the first puff on false pretences, curious and wanting to belong, and then the trap closed and took choice away.

We fell in through curiosity and belonging. We stayed in because something learned to call and old stories learned to answer. We hid to keep it. We lied to protect it. None of that is the portrait of a free chooser enjoying a genuine pleasure. All of it is the portrait of a person caught by a clever con.

You were not foolish. You were conned. That is the whole truth about your past, and seeing it takes shame away rather than adding it. Shame belongs to the trap, never to you.

Ask yourself three honest questions and answer them as you would to a friend you trust. When you lit that very first dose, did you mean to sign for midnight maths and garage doors and lying about numbers. When you smoke now, do you feel like a host enjoying a guest or like a tenant paying rent to someone you never invited in. And if this were truly free enjoyment, why would the thought of never smoking again bring panic rather than a shrug.

You know the answers. We both do. A shrug is what freedom feels like. Panic is what a catch feels like.

### WHOSE HAND IS THAT

Watch yourself today. Not tomorrow. Today, while you are smoking exactly as normal.

You finish a call. The phone goes down. Your hand is already reaching. Pack, stick, lighter — one smooth chain before you have decided anything. You watch your own fingers do it as if they belong to someone else.

Or the alarm rings. Your feet have not touched the floor and your mind has already counted pack, lighter, window. Or the meal ends. Plate back. Chair back. Hand out. No debate. No vote. Movement first, reason after. Or the kettle boils. Or the car door shuts. Or the computer freezes. Or there is a pause in conversation. The moment changes and the hand moves.

If this were choice, would it look like that. Choice pauses. Choice weighs. Choice can wait until evening. Choice can say not now and feel nothing. What you live with is different. It is a tug that arrives uninvited and a hand that obeys before you speak.

That tug is small. It is not agony. It is not illness. Between doses you feel it as an empty, slightly restless, slightly edgy little tug — a faint emptiness, a slight edge, as if something needs to be done and you cannot name what. You know that feeling well. We all do. We misread it for years as hunger, as nerves, as boredom, as I want one.

I call it the Nipper. It lives on nicotine and complains when it is not fed. I mention it only in passing so you have a name for the tug when you feel it today.

So why does so small a tug run so large a life. Because behind it stands something bigger.

I call it the Smokescreen. It is the fog of brainwashing we all breathed in since childhood — that smoking steadies, rewards, belongs with drink and friends and breaks — that tells you the tug means you need a dose to cope, to enjoy, to belong. The Nipper creates the tug. The Smokescreen tells you what the tug means. Together they keep you lighting doses you never freely chose. I name them only in passing. You do not need to fight them. You do not need to study them. You only need to see that choice did not put them there.

You never decided to need a dose to get through stress and meals and breaks and nights out. A child does not dream of counting sticks at midnight. You were led in by curiosity and belonging, kept in by a tiny tug and a thick fog of stories.

Feel the relief in that. There is nothing wrong with you to fix. There is only a trap to see and an escape to enjoy. You are not your tug. You are the person watching the tug. The tug is small and foreign and dying. The fog is old and borrowed and thin. You are the clear mind underneath, already able to breathe and taste and cope without paying for every finished act with a light.

Notice today how automatic it is. Notice the closet smoker in you checking wind direction before you light. Notice the habit smokes that arrive with coffee, with keys, with keys in the door, with the end of anything. Notice bumming cigs and offering them as friendship, as if the stick were the bond rather than the company. Each noticing loosens one bar without any strain. You do not need to change anything yet. You only need to watch with clear eyes and let the pattern show itself.

### HEAVEN FOR A MINUTE, FLAT FOR AN HOUR

I love smoking. I hate being a smoker.

Do you recognize yourself in that sentence. The moment I light up I am in heaven, but feel shitty afterwards. So whilst I love smoking I hate being a smoker. Those are not my words. They are the words of smokers like you and me, spoken honestly about the same ten minutes.

Hold that honesty gently, because it proves you are not lying and you are not blind. You feel a flash of heaven at the light. Of course you do. Then minutes later comes the aftertaste, the stale mouth, the faint shame, the knowledge you will need to do it again in an hour. Heaven, then flat. Love the light, hate the life.

Now ask the trap questions. If the dose gave you a real pleasure, would the pleasure live only in the lighting and then turn to shame. Would a genuine friend lift you for three minutes and chain you for the other twenty three hours. Would you need to tell yourself one will not hurt about a true friend.

You know the answer. We both do.

That heaven never lasts the day. It cannot last, because it was never a day long gift. It was a moment at the light, followed by the stale cost and the need again. We smokers know that circle by heart.

I do not mock that three minutes. I lived for it. I chased it through rain and lies and midnight maths. I told myself stories about taste and ritual and my moment. Look at those stories in daylight and they fall apart without cruelty.

Did you love the taste when you began. No. You coughed. Did you love the smell on your coat. No. You sprayed it. Did you love stepping out alone while laughter went on inside. No. You hurried. Did you love the little parties you told yourself each dose would be. No. You paid for each party with an hour of need.

Consider the closet routine again with clear eyes. You hide, you light, you feel heaven, you feel flat, you hide the evidence. Which part of that chain looks like a free pleasure. The hiding. The hurry. The aftertaste. The need to repeat. If one link were joy and the rest were cost, would a free person keep the chain. Or would a trapped person call the one quiet link joy because the trap taught him to.

You are not divided because you are weak. You are divided because you feel two true things at once. The light feels good. The life of needing it feels bad. Both feelings are honest. What is false is the lesson the Smokescreen draws from the first feeling — that the stick is a friend. A friend does not make you hide in your own garage.

Let that stand as settled fact, not as a scolding. The light brings a brief heaven. The heaven fades. The need returns. That circle is not choice. That circle is feeding.

Think of your day as a string of lights. First light on empty lungs, harsh and dizzy, yet chased. Mid morning light between tasks, smoked fast. After meal light, longer, with coffee. Afternoon lights to break the stretch. Evening lights to mark the end of this and the start of that. Night light before bed, stale and tired. Which one would you keep if you could keep only one and lose the need for the rest. Every smoker picks a favourite and every favourite, examined, is the same heaven minute followed by the same flat hour. The favourite does not prove benefit. The favourite proves the trap taught you to treasure the quietest link in a heavy chain.

### TAKEN CARE OF

If anything stressful happens in my life, a cigarette takes care of it.

Do those words live in your mouth. Hand already reaching while the phone is still warm. Puff, and a sense of being taken care of washes over you. Shoulders drop. Breath slows. The world feels manageable for a minute.

I believe you feel that. I felt it a thousand times. Phone down, hand out, puff, and the wash of relief. Bills and bosses and children and traffic and grief — through them all the reaching hand and the brief sense that someone has put a hand on your shoulder. I do not mock that feeling. It is real as feeling. No one who has smoked through years of pressure can deny the wash. Denying it would make this book a lie and you would close it.

See it now only through the eyes of choice, which is the work of this chapter. Notice how under pressure the hand moves even faster, with even less decision. Notice how the thought arrives dressed as your own voice. Time for one. I need a break. Notice how a free pleasure would not need to arrive uninvited through a tug and a reaching hand.

A free pleasure waits to be invited. A free pleasure does not yank your sleeve in the middle of bad news. A free pleasure does not shorten your temper until you feed it and then call the quieting kindness. What you live with under stress is not choosing. It is answering. The louder the stress, the quicker the answer, the smaller the pause where choice would live.

Watch the sequence closely today. The trigger, the tug, the reach, the light, the wash. Trigger, tug, reach, light, wash. It runs like a reflex. Reflexes are not decisions. No one congratulates himself on choosing to blink when dust flies. Why congratulate yourself on choosing to reach when the tug fires.

And notice who reaches. It is not the calm, clear you who loves your family and does your work well and laughs with friends. It is the edgy you, the restless you, the you between doses who cannot sit still until the next feed. That edgy you is not your nature. That edgy you is the Nipper complaining and the Smokescreen translating the complaint as need a dose. The clear you watches and wonders why choice feels so much like obedience.

I felt taken care of in the pressured minute and still saw my problems sitting untouched on the desk after the puff. You have seen the same. The phone call ended badly before the dose and remains ended badly after it. The bill waits. The boss waits. The ache waits. The wash came and went and left the world as it was, except you now need another wash in an hour. Is that taking care. Or is that being taken.

Hold that question lightly. You do not need to solve stress today. You only need to see that stress makes the reaching faster and the choosing smaller. A pleasure that must be snatched before thought, hidden from love, and repeated within the hour is not a choice. It is a small master with a loud story.

### CONNED, NOT WEAK

Let me gather what this chapter has shown and put it plainly, as one escapee to one reader still inside.

We fell in through curiosity and belonging, on phoney glamour, tasting foul and persisting because everyone said there was something in it. We stayed in because a tiny tug learned to call and old stories learned to answer. We hid to keep it. We lied to protect it. We watched our own hands move before our minds decided. We felt heaven at the light and flat after. We felt taken care of in the pressured minute. None of that is the portrait of a free chooser enjoying a genuine pleasure. All of it is the portrait of a decent person caught by a clever con that removed choice and called it habit.

Picture it without shame and the whole struggle changes shape. You are not a weak chooser who keeps choosing badly. You are a clear mind with a small tug and a borrowed fog. The tug is the Nipper, already dying, already beaten, asking for one more feed. The fog is the Smokescreen, old and thin, telling you the tug means you need a friend. The place they keep you is the nicotine trap, with bars you paid for and polished. Seeing the bars is not misery. Seeing the bars is the beginning of walking out into clean air.

Do you feel how different this is from everything you were told. Before, you were told you lacked toughness and must fight yourself forever. Here you are told you were conned completely — first by glamour before your first puff, then by stories that taught you to read the tug as proof of benefit — and that understanding, not toughness, sets you free. Which sounds more like truth. Which sounds more like escape. Which leaves you feeling warm toward yourself and harsh toward the trap that used you.

I was conned in exactly the same way. We all were. The strongest people I know — people who run businesses, raise children, work nights, carry grief — smoked in garages and lied about numbers. Were they weak. Or were they outgunned by an engineered catch that turns a tiny tug into a life of doses. You know the answer. It takes no weakness to be caught. It takes clarity to see the catch.

There is no doom here, only freedom ahead. The more clearly you see the tug and the fog, the less you will feel like a chooser and the more you will feel like a person ready to escape. That readiness is joyful, not heavy. Rejoice that you can investigate without panic. Celebrate that no one asks you to suffer through these pages. Breathe easy and keep watching.

So do not change your smoking yet. Let the argument do the work while you live your ordinary days with new eyes. Notice the half open door and ask why joy needs hiding. Notice the hand and ask why choice moves before thought. Notice the heaven and the flat after and ask why a gift would behave like that. Notice the taken care of wash and watch it with curious eyes. Each noticing loosens one bar without any strain.

I make you this promise as a fellow escapee. There is no pain waiting for you here. There is no loss waiting for you here. There is only the marvellous moment when you see the trick and the want falls away by itself. You have nothing real to lose by seeing. You have clean air, mornings without coughing, nights without planning where to stand — all as bonus. The real prize is joy. To think of a cigarette and feel glad, deeply glad, that you no longer need it.

2. DON'T STOP OR CUT DOWN YET
Smoke as normal until your last cigarette.

**SUMMARY**
- Hiding to smoke shows this stopped being free choice long ago.
- Your hand moves before you decide because a small tug prompts and old stories interpret it.
- The heaven at lighting lives only at the light, followed by stale cost within the hour.
- Feeling taken care of after a puff is real as feeling, yet the reaching under stress looks like answering, not choosing.
- You were conned, not weak, and seeing the trap is the first step to walking out of it.
