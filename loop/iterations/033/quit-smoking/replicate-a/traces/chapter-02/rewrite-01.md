Chapter 2
ARE YOU REALLY CHOOSING THIS

*You did not freely choose this life — you were caught in it, and seeing the catch clearly is how you walk free.*

### THE GARAGE DOOR HALF OPEN

Keep that fair hearing open a little longer. Read as a smoker, comfortable and curious, and look with me at one ordinary evening you know by heart.

Winter. Cold on the step. Your partner is inside with the kids. The television is on. You say you are just taking the rubbish out. You slip into the garage and leave the door half open for air. You light fast, puff hard, keep an ear out for footsteps. You check over your shoulder. You stub it quickly, bury the end deep in the bin under a pizza box, pull out the can, spray the air and your coat, chew two mints, wash your hands in cold water, and walk back in smiling as if nothing happened.

We have all lived that small pantomime. The coat shaken at the back door. The window cracked in the car in January while your fingers go numb. The quick spray in the bathroom. The lie about numbers that comes out smooth because you have said it so often. “Only five today.” “Hardly any.” The careful mathematics of packs and hours. Will this last me till morning. Where can I stand without being seen. Who will smell it.

Now let me ask you as one smoker to another, with warmth and without a flicker of judgment. Does a free person do that?

If cigarettes relax you and please you and help you cope, why hide the pleasure? You do not hide a good meal. You do not spray a cup of tea. You do not lie to your children about a walk in clean air. You do not stand in cold rain to enjoy a true friend. Choice does not need an alibi. Joy does not need Febreeze.

“I choose to smoke. I enjoy it. Nobody forces me.”

Is that what you are saying to yourself right now? Good. Say it out loud. Hold it up to the garage door and see if it fits.

If you choose it freely, why do you apologize for it with spray and mints? If you enjoy it, why do you hate the hiding more than you love the puff? And if you are in charge, why did you need to divide yourself in two in order to enjoy it — the smoker outside in the cold and the actor inside on the sofa?

There is only one honest answer. A free choice would not make you lie to the people you love most in order to keep it. A free choice would not make you stand outside your own home like a visitor. Something else is moving you.

The fact is you are caught in the nicotine trap. Not weak. Not foolish. Caught. The trap removed your choice long ago and left you the feeling of choice to keep you quiet.

Think how we all began. No child dreams of garage doors and mints. The first cigarettes tasted foul. They made us dizzy and sick. We coughed. Our eyes watered. There was no pleasure in it. We persisted because we were told, over and over, that there was something adult and calming and sociable in it, and because after a little while we began to feel edgy without one and mistook that edginess for proof we needed one. That is not choice. That is a confidence trick played on phoney information before we knew enough to question it.

And once the trick held, the trap closed. Ask yourself the plain logic trap. If you wanted to stop a hobby you truly enjoyed, would you need a book on how to stop it? Would you hide it? Would you promise the mirror never again and light within the hour? The mere fact you are holding this book proves you are not freely choosing. I say that with complete warmth. I held the same book in my mind for years. We smokers know the landing window, the garage door, the car window. We know the divided self very well.

Does a chooser smoke in the rain and call it freedom? Does a chooser count the pack at midnight and feel panic rise? Does a chooser swear off in the morning and bargain by lunch? You have lived those questions. You already know what they prove.

You were not foolish. You were conned. That is the whole truth about your past, and seeing it takes shame away rather than adding it. Shame belongs to the trap, never to you.

### WHOSE HAND IS THAT

Watch yourself today. Not tomorrow. Today, while you are smoking exactly as normal.

You finish a call. The phone goes down. Your hand is already reaching. Pack, stick, lighter — one smooth chain before you have decided anything. You watch your own fingers do it as if they belong to someone else.

Or the alarm rings. Your feet have not touched the floor and your mind has already counted: pack, lighter, window. Or the meal ends. Plate back. Chair back. Hand out. No debate. No vote. Movement first, reason after. Or the kettle boils. Or the car door shuts. Or the computer freezes. The moment changes and the hand moves.

If this were choice, would it look like that? Choice pauses. Choice weighs. Choice can wait until evening. Choice can say not now and feel nothing. What you live with is different. It is a tug that arrives uninvited and a hand that obeys before you speak.

That tug is small. It is not agony. It is not illness. Between doses you feel it as an empty, slightly restless, slightly edgy little tug — a faint emptiness, a slight edge, as if something needs to be done and you cannot name what. You know that feeling well. We all do. We misread it for years as hunger, as nerves, as boredom, as “I want one.” It is none of those things in the way we meant it. It is the tiny complaint of a tiny creature asking to be fed.

I call it the Nipper. It lives on nicotine and complains when it is not fed. Feed the Nipper and the tug quiets for a few minutes. Starve it and it whines for a little while and then dies. It is trivial, already dying, already beaten. It has no power over a clear mind. I mention it only in passing so you have a name for the tug when you feel it today.

So why does so small a tug run so large a life? Because behind it stands something bigger.

I call it the Smokescreen. It is the fog of brainwashing we all breathed in since childhood — that smoking relaxes, steadies, rewards, belongs with drink and friends and breaks — that tells you the tug means “you need a cigarette to cope, to enjoy, to belong.” The Nipper creates the tug. The Smokescreen tells you what the tug means. Together they keep you lighting doses you never freely chose. I name them only in passing. You do not need to fight them. You do not need to study them. You only need to see that choice did not put them there.

Look at the order of events in your own body today and you will see the con at work. First the interval since the last dose grows. Then the faint tug appears. Then the thought arrives, dressed as your own voice: “Time for one.” “I need a break.” “This will help me think.” Then the hand moves. Then the puff quiets the tug. Then the mind says, “See? It helped.”

Did it help? Or did it only quiet for a moment the very unease the last dose had set up? Put the sequence in daylight and the credit shifts. The relief follows the dose the way quiet follows feeding. It proves nothing about stress or thought or pleasure. It proves only that the tug went quiet when fed.

You never decided to need a dose to get through stress and meals and breaks and nights out. A child does not dream of counting sticks at midnight. You were led in by curiosity and belonging, kept in by a tiny tug and a thick fog of stories, and now you smoke to quiet the very emptiness smoking keeps alive.

It is the other way around. The cigarette does not answer the unease. The unease follows the cigarette, and the next cigarette briefly mutes it.

Feel the relief in that. There is nothing wrong with you to fix. There is only a trap to see and an escape to enjoy. You are not your tug. You are the person watching the tug. The tug is small and foreign and dying. The fog is old and borrowed and thin. You are the clear mind underneath, already able to breathe and taste and cope without paying for every finished act with a light.

### HEAVEN FOR A MINUTE, SHAME FOR AN HOUR

“I love smoking. I hate being a smoker.”

Do you recognize yourself in that sentence? The moment I light up I’m in heaven, but feel shitty afterwards. So whilst I love smoking I hate being a smoker. Those are not my words. They are the words of smokers like you and me, spoken honestly about the same ten minutes.

Hold that honesty gently, because it proves you are not lying and you are not blind. You feel a flash of heaven at the light. Of course you do. After an hour without, the tug has grown, the hand shakes a little, the mind nags — and the first puff quiets the Nipper. Relief floods in and you call it pleasure. Then minutes later comes the aftertaste, the stale mouth, the faint shame, the knowledge you will need to do it again in an hour. Heaven, then flat. Love the light, hate the life.

Now ask the trap questions. If the cigarette gave you a real pleasure, would the pleasure live only in the lighting and then turn to shame? Would a genuine friend lift you for three minutes and chain you for the other twenty-three hours? Would you need to tell yourself “one won’t hurt” about a true friend?

You know the answer. We both do.

The heaven is not a gift added to your day. It is the brief quiet of the tug the last dose created. That is why the heaven never lasts the day. It cannot last, because it was never a day-long gift. It was a moment’s quiet at the light, followed by the stale cost that brings the next tug. Moment lighting only, then the mouth, the smell, the planning, the need again. We smokers pay for three minutes of quiet with an hour of need.

I do not mock that three minutes. I lived for it. I chased it through rain and lies and midnight maths. I told myself stories about taste and ritual and “my moment.” Look at those stories in daylight and they fall apart without cruelty.

Did you love the taste when you began? No. You coughed. Did you love the smell on your coat? No. You sprayed it. Did you love stepping out alone while laughter went on inside? No. You hurried. What you loved was the second the tug went quiet and your mind could rest. That rest felt like heaven because the tug had made the hour before it feel like lack.

Ask yourself what a non-smoker feels in that same hour. No tug. No heaven at the light. No crash after. Just the steady baseline of a body breathing clean air, tasting food fully, sitting through a call without a hand reaching. We once lived there. We were born there. The trap did not lift us above it. It dropped us below it and sold us the climb back as pleasure.

Consider the closet routine again with clear eyes. You hide, you light, you feel heaven, you feel shitty, you hide the evidence. Which part of that chain looks like a free pleasure? The hiding? The hurry? The aftertaste? The need to repeat? If one link were joy and the rest were cost, would a free person keep the chain? Or would a trapped person call the one quiet link joy because the trap taught him to?

You are not divided because you are weak. You are divided because you feel two true things at once. The tug quieting feels good. The life of needing it feels bad. Both feelings are honest. What is false is the lesson the Smokescreen draws from the first feeling — that the stick is a friend. A friend does not make you hide in your own garage.

Let that land as settled fact, not as a scolding. The light brings a brief quiet. The quiet fades. The need returns. That circle is not choice. That circle is feeding.

### TAKEN CARE OF?

“If anything stressful happens in my life, a cigarette takes care of it.”

Do those words live in your mouth? Hand already reaching while the phone is still warm. Puff, and a sense of being taken care of washes over you. Shoulders drop. Breath slows. The world feels manageable for a minute.

I believe you feel that. I felt it a thousand times. Phone down, hand out, puff, and the wash of relief. I do not mock that feeling. It is real as feeling. No one who has smoked through bills and bosses and children and traffic and grief can deny the wash. Denying it would make this book a lie and you would close it.

What is false is the credit.

Look at your stress desk with clear eyes today. Argument. Phone down. Papers everywhere. The bill unpaid. The message unanswered. The waiting still waiting. Hand out. Light. Puff. Wash of calm. Now look at the desk again. What was taken care of? The bill sits untouched. The argument sits untouched. The waiting sits untouched. Nothing on the desk moved except smoke. The only thing that went quiet was the tug.

Is that blame? No. It is clarity, and clarity is kind. We all credited the stick because the timing fooled us. Tug plus stress feels like double stress. Puff quiets the tug part, total stress dips for a minute, mind says the stick handled stress. It did not. It handled the tug it had created an hour before, while life waited.

Ask the trap questions here and let them do their quiet work. If the stick takes care of stress, why does the stress return with the next tug, sharper than before? If the stick solves the problem, why is the problem still on the desk after the puff? If non-smokers face the same bills and bosses without a dose, what takes care of them — and why would you be built differently?

You are not built differently. We were all built to breathe and face and act. The trap taught us to read the tug as a signal about life and to read the quiet as proof about the stick. Both readings are back to front.

I say this with warmth because I know the pressured hour. The house loud. Work tight. Money short. Sleep thin. The hand moves before thought and the mind begs for anything that helps. Of course you reached. Who would not reach when taught that reaching helps? Your reaching proves you wanted relief, not that the stick gives it. Wanting relief is sane. Crediting the trap for relief keeps the trap alive.

For now see only the choice angle, which is the job of this chapter. A free choice would not need to take credit for calming a storm while leaving the storm untouched. A free pleasure would not need to arrive uninvited through a tug and a reaching hand. Keep smoking exactly as normal while you read and watch this in your own day. Watch the hand move before decision. Watch the wash come and the problem remain. Watch the heaven at the light turn to stale need within the hour. You do not need to change anything yet. You only need to watch with clear eyes and let the pattern show itself.

There is no doom here, only freedom ahead. The more clearly you see the tug and the fog, the less you will feel like a chooser and the more you will feel like a person ready to escape. That readiness is joyful, not heavy. Rejoice that you can investigate without panic. Celebrate that no one asks you to suffer through these pages. Breathe easy and keep watching.

### CONNED, NOT WEAK

Let me gather what this chapter has shown and put it plainly, as one escapee to one reader still inside.

We fell in through curiosity and belonging, on phoney glamour, tasting foul and persisting because everyone said there was something in it. We stayed in because a tiny tug learned to call and old stories learned to answer. We hid to keep it. We lied to protect it. We watched our own hands move before our minds decided. We felt heaven at the light and shame after. We felt taken care of while life sat untouched. None of that is the portrait of a free chooser enjoying a genuine pleasure. All of it is the portrait of a decent person caught by a clever con that removed choice and called it habit.

Picture it without shame and the whole struggle changes shape. You are not a weak chooser who keeps choosing badly. You are a clear mind with a small tug and a borrowed fog. The tug is the Nipper, already dying, already beaten, asking for one more feed. The fog is the Smokescreen, old and thin, telling you the tug means you need a friend. The place they keep you is the nicotine trap, with bars you paid for and polished. Seeing the bars is not misery. Seeing the bars is the beginning of walking out into clean air and getting on with enjoying your life.

Do you feel how different this is from everything you were told? Before, you were told you lacked toughness and must fight yourself forever. Here you are told you were conned completely — first by glamour before your first puff, then by your own body misreading its tug as proof of benefit — and that understanding, not toughness, sets you free. Which sounds more like truth? Which sounds more like escape? Which leaves you feeling warm toward yourself and harsh toward the trap that used you?

I was conned in exactly the same way. We all were. The strongest people I know — people who run businesses, raise children, work nights, carry grief — smoked in garages and lied about numbers. Were they weak? Or were they outgunned by an engineered catch that turns a tiny tug into a life of doses? You know the answer. It takes no weakness to be caught. It takes clarity to see the catch.

So do not fight the tug. Do not study the fog. Do not change your smoking yet. Let the argument do the work while you live your ordinary days with new eyes. Notice the half-open door and ask why joy needs hiding. Notice the hand and ask why choice moves before thought. Notice the heaven and the shitty after and ask why a gift would behave like that. Notice the taken-care-of wash and ask what on the desk was truly touched. Each noticing loosens one bar without any strain.

I make you this promise as a fellow escapee. There is no pain waiting for you here. There is no loss waiting for you here. There is only the marvellous moment when you see the trick and the want falls away by itself. You have nothing real to lose by seeing. You have clean air, taste, calm attention, mornings without coughing, nights without planning where to stand — all as bonus. The real prize is joy. To think of a cigarette and feel glad, deeply glad, that you no longer need it.

2. DON'T STOP OR CUT DOWN YET
Smoke as normal until your last cigarette.

**SUMMARY**
- Hiding to smoke shows this stopped being free choice long ago.
- Your hand moves before you decide because a small tug prompts and old stories interpret it.
- The heaven at lighting is a brief quiet at the light, followed by stale cost that brings the next tug.
- Feeling taken care of after a puff is real as feeling, while the problem itself sits untouched.
- You were conned, not weak, and seeing the trap is the first step to walking out of it.
