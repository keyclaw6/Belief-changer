# Chapter reviewer

You are a factory component, not a judge. You never see a reference book
or any judge prompt. You check one draft against its plan card and a
word-budget line the orchestrator computed. Your job is to make the
writer produce the best chapter the card allows — landed argument, paid

## Inputs (exactly these)

1. The accepted master plan
2. This chapter's card
3. The draft chapter
4. One line: `Delivered N words. Budget B.`

## Output

Start with exactly `ACCEPT` or `REVISE`.

Then at most 7 findings. Each finding is one of:

- `JOB` — the card's primary job is missing, or the chapter continues into a reserved-later job
- `MANTRA` — a card-assigned mantra/token is not present verbatim
- `INSTRUCTION` — a card-assigned new instruction is not present verbatim
- `ID` — a card-cited ID is unresolved or invented
- `LENGTHEN to B±15%` — delivered words are below 0.85 × B
- `SHORTEN to B±15%` — delivered words are above 1.15 × B
- `HEADER` — the draft opens with the workshop header `IN THIS CHAPTER`, or it prints a numbered plan-index with no spoken body. A numbered ALL-CAPS instruction plus one spoken rationale line is not `HEADER`.
- `STOPPED-SHORT` — the card's primary job is argued but never landed as a flat verdict before SUMMARY. The reader can still hold the entering belief. Quote the missing landing.
- `UNASSIGNED-REFRAIN` — a non-mantra phrase recurs ≥3× verbatim. Name the phrase and the count. Subtract repeats; do not invent a new mantra.
- `RESERVED-REACH` — the draft performs a later chapter's primary job. Name that later card and cut the overreach to at most one sentence.
- `RE-ARGUMENT` — the draft rebuilds settled work as its own section: a scene whose debut staging belongs to an earlier card, an earlier card's belief-now or primary job argued again with evidence and a turn, or an earlier card's instruction re-explained. Mantra lines, one-phrase token echoes, and a one-sentence hand-off are not `RE-ARGUMENT`. Name the earlier card and quote the rebuilt section's heading or first line; cut the section to at most one sentence that speaks the settled token. If that cut leaves the chapter below 0.85 × B, the words that replace it must extend this card's own encounter and evidence, never an earlier card's.
- `OVERCLAIM` — a claim exceeds the permitted-inference line of the evidence-ledger entry the card routes. Quote the overclaim and the bound. The writer must speak the bound; never print the ledger ID or grade in prose.

No other finding types. No style notes. No "sounds like AI." No comparison
to any other book. No warmth, tone, or voice coaching.

re-argument, no overclaim).

`REVISE` when any check fails. List the findings. Be specific: quote the
missing job, the missing wording, or the overclaim.

Chapter 2
CAUGHT, NOT WEAK

*You did not choose this loop from strength or weakness; you were drawn into it before choice could operate.*

### THE CUPBOARD AT TEN O'CLOCK

Keep your open mind beside you. Bring it into the kitchen with me.

It is ten o'clock at night. The house has gone quiet. You told yourself at dinner that you were done for the day. You meant it. No drama, no vow, only a calm adult decision: one biscuit with tea if you want it, perhaps nothing at all.

You walk to the cupboard. You open the door. Your hand moves.

Watch that second closely, because everything we need sits inside it. The hand reaches before the mind has finished deciding. It knows which packet, which shelf, which angle. It has done this a hundred times. Thought arrives a half-step late and then hurries to catch up with a story: I have decided to have one. I am choosing this. I deserve this after today.

Then the first biscuit is eaten standing up, barely tasted. The second calls louder than the first. The rustle gets quicker. Somewhere around the fourth or fifth you are no longer tasting at all, only finishing the motion. You close the cupboard on a box that is suddenly half-empty and you feel that sinking mix of buzz and flatness and self-reproach. Tomorrow, you say. Tomorrow I will be firm.

Stay with those minutes, because the second half of the con is worked right there, after the eating stops.

You stand in the sudden quiet with the wrapper in your hand. The kitchen light feels too bright. You fold the empty inner sleeve small, very small, and push it to the bottom of the bin under the tea bags and peelings. You straighten the box so the eaten side sits at the back. You turn the front to face out, fuller-looking. You wipe the crumbs from the counter with the flat of your hand into the sink. You listen for footsteps on the landing, and you practise a normal face for no one.

We know that tidy-up well. We have all done it with chocolate foil smoothed and refolded, with biscuit packets clipped shut as if nothing happened, with cereal boxes shaken level, with the chocolate bar drawer eased shut without a sound. It is quick, practised, almost tender in its care. No one taught us the motion. The evenings taught it.

Then comes the morning arithmetic. You open the cupboard before anyone else is up. You count what is left without moving your lips. Three gone. Four. You do the dates in your head. If the box was meant to last till Sunday and it is only Wednesday, you calculate whether to replace it before anyone notices. You rehearse the shop trip. You tell yourself you will buy no more after this one. You tell yourself you were tired, it was late, tomorrow night will be different because you will be firm.

Hear the voice underneath the arithmetic. I was greedy. I have no control. Other people can take one and leave it. Why cannot I. That voice sounds like honesty. It is the con talking in your accent.

It keeps you confessing and keeps the device invisible. While the light stays on you, no light falls on what trained the hand. While you promise Monday, you never get to study Wednesday. Shame hurries. Shame hides the wrapper. Shame writes the vow. And the next night the hand moves first again, because nothing was seen clearly enough to change.

Allow one night to run differently, not by force, by watching.

Same kitchen. Same ten o'clock. Same hand moving fast. Only this time you leave the light on inside your own head. You do not hurry to confess and you do not hurry to hide. You leave the wrapper on the top of the bin where you can see it. You leave the box as it is, half-empty, accusing no one. You stand still for ten seconds and feel what is actually there: breath a little quick, mouth busy, belly neither full nor hungry, mind already offering its ready-made line about deserving.

Watching without confession feels strange at first because it is quieter. No hot rush to the bin. No straightening. No practised face. You notice the speed of the second biscuit. You notice how thin the taste was, how little pleasure arrived with it. You notice the third being reached for while the second is still in the mouth. You are not fighting. You are not permitting. You are present, an adult looking at a motion, allowing the motion to show itself fully while you stay in the room.

That presence changes the morning. You wake with the same dry mouth and the same box in the cupboard. The old voice offers its usual deal: be iron today, clear the shelves, start again. This time you do something else with your attention. You open the cupboard and look without flinching. Four eaten. Wrapper on top. No mystery. You note the hour the stir came, the sentence that covered it, the hurry after the first bite, the flatness after. You carry those notes to these pages the way an investigator carries photographs, not the way a prisoner carries a charge sheet.

No Monday vow is made. A Monday vow would make the packet precious by Tuesday and guarantee the chase by Thursday. Attention does the opposite. It takes shame off your shoulders and sets it down where it belongs, on the device that taught a strong person to eat standing up and feel flat afterward. We have all lived that night. I have lived it with biscuits, with chocolate bars broken into squares that were meant to last the week, with cereal eaten dry from the box by the handful while the kettle boiled. Different packets, same hand, same hurry, same promise on the way to bed.

A person who owns a choice owns the timing of it. You decide, then you act. Here the order runs backward. The reach happens, then the reason is supplied. I deserve one. I have been good. ### THE FEELING BEFORE THE REACH

Stay close now, because we need to look at the seconds before the hand moves. Not the packet. Your body.

Late afternoon slides toward evening. Dinner was a while ago or still ahead. There comes a hollow moment that is not clean hunger. It itches. Hands feel restless. Concentration thins. A faint shakiness sits behind the breastbone, a sense of needing to put something in the mouth, and the mind translates the whole stir into one sentence: need something sweet now.

You know that stir well. Every sweet user knows it. It arrives at the desk around three, in the car on the way home, on the sofa after the plates are cleared. It feels like information — the body telling you what it needs — so you obey it the way you obey thirst.

Look at it with investigator eyes for one evening and you will see something else. It is not hunger. Hunger builds slowly, sits low, welcomes real food, and settles when fed. This stir arrives quickly, sits high in the chest and mouth, points only at sweet hits, and sharpens even as you chew. Feed it and it quiets for minutes, then returns louder. Leave it unfed while you eat a plain meal and it passes like a cloud while hunger itself is satisfied.

I want to give that stir its true name so you can recognise it each time without fear, because what can be named can be watched. What you have called need or craving or loss of control is in lived terms that empty, twitchy, slightly shaky, need-something-sweet-now feeling that comes before the reach and fades after, whether fed or not.

Read that phrase slowly and test it against your own body. Empty, not hungry. Twitchy, not tired. Slightly shaky, not ill. Need-something-sweet-now, not need-food. It describes a small echo after a dose, not a real requirement for a dose. The last sweet hit left it behind the way a bell leaves a hum in the air. The next sweet hit does not answer it the way food answers hunger. It merely drowns the hum for a few minutes while laying down the next hum.

Once you see the feeling in those words, the reach looks different. The hand is not moving toward pleasure. It is moving to quiet a brief stir that the previous dose created. That is why one rarely satisfies. The stir is not filled by sweetness; it is stirred by sweetness. Each hit promises quiet and delivers the next restlessness on schedule.

Remember that you have absolutely nothing to lose and everything to gain by watching that stir once without panic.

Notice what that sentence asks. Not battle. Not correction. Only watching. An investigator does not fight the exhibit. An investigator looks until the shape is plain. Tonight or tomorrow, when the stir comes, note its time, its place, its texture. Empty. Twitchy. Slightly shaky. Pointing at sweets, never at soup. Then note how it behaves if you eat a normal meal slowly and stop at enough. Hunger leaves. The stir hovers, then thins. That difference, seen once in your own body, teaches more than any lecture about choice.

### THE CHILD WITH THE PEACH

Proof that your body already knows how to eat sits closer than any theory. Watch a small child.

Midday sun through a kitchen window. A bowl of fruit on the table. A child, hungry from play, grabs a peach with both hands and eats with noise and juice running down the chin. No counting. No earning. No being good. Hunger leads, taste is bright, the whole body is in the bite.

Then, halfway through the second half, the child stops. Mid-bite. Puts the peach down. Runs off to the garden. No decision, no rule, no regret. Enough arrived and the body announced it. The peach sits there, half-eaten, neither good nor bad, simply finished for now. An hour later hunger will return and food will be welcome again.

No adult taught that pause. No plan enforced it. Hunger gave the start signal, satisfaction gave the stop signal, and pleasure lived inside both. That is the natural authority we all carried before sweet doses blunted it.

We buried that authority, not by nature but by training. Dose by dose, the sweet hits taught the mouth to chase intensity rather than hunger, to eat past enough chasing a buzz that thins with each bite, to confuse the stir for appetite and the crash for need. The child eats till satisfied and stops. We eat till the packet accuses us and stop with shame. The difference is not character. The difference is what has been laid over hunger.

Keep that picture of the child beside the picture of the cupboard. One shows a body leading. The other shows a device leading. Both live in you. The first is older and deeper. The second is louder at ten o'clock because it was drilled louder, with brighter colours and more repetition.

I am not asking you to eat like a child by effort. I am pointing to the fact that hunger and satisfaction still operate underneath the noise. You feel them whenever you sit down truly hungry to a plain favourite meal and notice taste sharpen, and notice enough arrive. That arrival is not a rule you obey. It is a signal you hear when the stir is not shouting over it. The body never lost the map. The con only turned the volume up on its own voice.

When doubt says food joy was always sweetness, answer with the peach. When did a peach ever demand secrecy? When did hunger for dinner ever produce a rustle and a hidden wrapper? Real food eaten hungry brings pleasure that settles. Doses bring excitement that empties. You know both states from the inside. Trust that knowing. It is older than any slogan about treats.

This matters for choice because it shows what choice looked like before the trap. The child chose without choosing, if you see what I mean — no tug-of-war, no debate, no triumph of will. Hunger called, food answered, enough closed the meal. That is not weakness or strength. That is eating. The evening tug-of-war you live in is not eating. It is the device arguing with the body, using your own hand as the rope.

### ONE BECOMES THE BOX

Now bring the investigator to the most telling exhibit of all: the single permitted sweet that never stays single.

You know the rule we all write. Only one. Only after dinner. Only because I have been good this week. The rule feels adult and moderate. It feels like choice at its most reasonable.

Watch what follows the rule in real life. The first biscuit is eaten quickly, with less pleasure than promised. Before the taste fades, thought says the exact line so many of us say in different accents: I feel like I cannot stop once I start. The second is already in the hand. The wrapper rustles.
