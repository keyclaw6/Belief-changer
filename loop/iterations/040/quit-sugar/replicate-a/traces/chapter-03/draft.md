Chapter 3
WHAT DOES IT DO FOR YOU

IN THIS CHAPTER
The notebook on the kitchen table, the drawer at three o'clock, the dentist's mirror, the till receipt, the question by the ditch, the two columns that will not balance

*Forget weighing harm against pleasure and ask a harder question — what pleasure did it ever actually give you?*

### THE WRONG SCALES

You have seen that the late hand is not choice. It moves first and finds its reason afterward. That seeing lifts blame off you and sets it where it belongs, on the device that trained the hand.

Most readers answer that seeing with a bargain. Very well, they say, it catches me. It hurries me at night. It leaves me flat and a little ashamed. But surely it gives me something too. Surely the harm and the good must be weighed against each other, and the good, at least on a hard day, weighs more.

I understand that bargain because I made it for years. We all make it. It sounds balanced and adult. It sounds fair to both sides.

It is the wrong weighing, and while we use those scales the trap holds.

Harm against benefit sounds sensible only while the benefit is real. If a man sells you a coat, you may argue about the price, the cloth, the stitching. You argue because a coat keeps you warm. The good is settled. Only the cost is in question.

BAD SUGAR is not a coat. The good is not settled. The good is the question.

I am not asking you to list diseases against desserts and decide which pile is higher. That game can run for years without moving you an inch, because every harm can be answered with yes, but. Yes, it rots teeth, but I brush. Yes, it piles weight, but I had a hard day. Yes, it costs, but I deserve one. The yes-but keeps the hand moving while the mind debates.

I ask you to put harm to one side entirely. Leave teeth, weight, money, mood out of the courtroom for an hour. Look only at the other pan of the scales, the one we never empty in public. What does it actually do FOR you?

Hear how strange that sounds at first. We are so used to defending the dose that being asked to produce one real gift feels almost rude. Yet it is the only fair test. A thing that asks for your money every day, your time every day, your hunger signals every day, ought to be able to name its gift without stammering.

You say it helps. I say show me where.

You say it comforts. I say show me the comfort that stays past the swallow.

You say it treats. I say keep that word beside you, because we will look it in the face before this chapter closes.

For now hold the new scales steady. Not does harm outweigh good. But what good is there at all?

### A DAY ON PAPER

Take an ordinary day, not a bad day, not a binge day, the sort of Tuesday you would call normal. Put it on paper hour by hour, with the sweet hits left in plain view. No hiding, no straightening the packet for the photograph. An investigator's notebook, not a confession.

Morning. Tea with two sugars and a biscuit while the house wakes. The taste is quick, bright at the edge of the tongue, gone by the third sip. Energy? No lift you could point to. Only a small bustle in the mouth and then the day beginning much as it would have begun.

Mid-morning. The drawer opens at work. A chocolate bar broken without looking up from the screen. Eaten in four bites while reading mail. Did you taste the third bite? Honestly? Most of us cannot recall it. The hand fed while the eyes worked. The bar did not improve the mail. The mail did not improve the bar. Two acts ran side by side and neither honoured the other.

Lunch eaten fast, pudding added because it was there. A brief fullness, then that familiar afternoon sag. Attention thins around three. The same drawer opens again. The sentence arrives ready-made, the one we all know in our own accent: sugary somethings to get me through. You obey, not with joy, with haste. Ten minutes of buzz, thin and noisy at the edges, then the fog comes back a little thicker. A second reach follows before five.

Evening. Cooking, plates, sofa. The treats come out with tea. One biscuit taken standing up. Then the rustle quickens. You do not decide to have five. You have five while deciding to have one. Taste thins with each bite. The last two are eaten for motion rather than flavour. Regret arrives before swallowing ends.

Night. Dry mouth. Vague unease about sleep. A small vow made to the ceiling: tomorrow I will be good this week. I have managed to be good before, you tell yourself, and the telling itself proves how little goodness has to do with it.

Now rule two columns on the page. Left column: what it did TO you. Right column: what it did FOR you.

Left column fills without effort. Money out. Time out. Hunger blurred. Taste dulled by the fourth bite. Teeth bathed again and again. Attention broken at three and again at five. Evening eaten standing up. Sleep a little thinner. Shame humming low under it all.

Right column? Put down only what stayed. Not what flashed for three minutes and charged you for three hours. What stayed.

The buzz did not stay. The comfort did not stay. The lift did not stay. What stayed is the left column.

Answer me from your own Tuesdays, not from slogans.

When did a dose leave you calmer an hour later than a plain hour would have left you? Name the date.

When did a bar eaten over mail leave the work better and the mouth happier than mail with tea alone? Point to the page.

If the right column holds a real gift, why must it be topped up at ten, at three, at five, at ten again? Gifts that must be paid for four times a day are not gifts. They are hires, and poor hires at that.

You will feel the mind wriggle here. It will offer the roller coaster as proof of life — ups and downs mean I feel, mean I live. I want to get off the roller coaster, you have whispered on bad nights, and by morning you call the ride a treat again. Notice the turn. The same motion mourned at night is praised at dawn. That is not two truths. That is one device seen from below and then from inside.

Keep the notebook open. We are not done with it.

### TEETH AND THE RECEIPT

Some costs shout. Most whisper. The whispering ones hold you longer because you learn to live inside them.

Look first at the shouting one, because it leaves marks you can see in a mirror.

Every frequent sweet dose bathes the teeth in sugars that plaque turns to acid. Acid softens enamel. Softened enamel, hit again and again without pause, decays. That is plain mechanism, not moral talk. Brushing and fluoride matter — I am not saying they count for nothing — but they cannot stand guard all day while dose follows dose and acid keeps returning. Frequency is the stake. A mouth rinsed in sweetness morning, noon and night lives in acid far longer than a mouth that meets sweetness rarely.

I say that as fact about mouths, not as a sentence on your mouth. I am not telling you what your dentist will find next month. I am pointing to what frequent doses do in a mouth by nature.

Now look at the whispering columns, the ones we never add up because adding up would frighten us into another Monday vow, and Monday vows keep the packet precious.

Money. Not the single bar. The week. The month. The year of weeks. Till receipts kept for seven days embarrass most of us. Small coins, daily, without pleasure to show for them. We count pennies on bills and pour pounds through wrappers. If a neighbour charged you that sum each week for ten minutes of buzz followed by fog, you would call him a cheat. When the packet charges it, you call it a treat.

Time. The minutes spent chewing without tasting, queuing for the drawer, unwrapping, refolding foil, hiding sleeves, counting what is left, planning the shop trip to replace before anyone notices, rehearsing I will be firm. Add them across a year and whole days have gone standing up in kitchens.

Mood. Not deep sadness, the daily weather. Short lift then fast drop, brightness then flatness, resolve then hurry. You know the line from your own mouth: I would have something quick and sweet but now know that will only give a short term lift which will inevitably be followed by a fast drop. That sentence is not theory. It is Tuesday described truly.

Teeth, wallet, hours, weather. All on the left. All paid daily.

Now turn again to the right column with investigator coldness. What stayed to balance teeth bathed in acid, money poured through foil, hours eaten standing, mood riding up and down on a string?

“I like the taste.” Keep that line, we will test it fully when the ground is ready. For now ask only whether taste explains the fourth biscuit eaten without tasting, the bar eaten over mail without looking, the box emptied after the promise of one. Taste cannot explain eating past tasting. Something else drove those bites.

“I needed the lift.” Keep that line too. We will follow it hour by hour later. For now ask only whether a lift that must be repaid with a drop within the hour is a gift or a loan with cruel interest.

Do you see how the audit changes the question? We began with does harm outweigh good. We end with where is the good at all. The left column grows while we watch. The right column fades while we watch. That fading is not your failure. It is the first clear sight of the con.

### FEELING FINE PROVES NOTHING

“But I feel fine,” says the voice that guards the last corner. “My teeth are sound. My weight is steady. My work gets done. A few sweets cannot be doing much TO me.”

I hear affection in that voice. It wants the audit to be someone else's story. It wants normal to mean safe.

Normal does not mean low. That is the fact we must face without flinching and without fear-mongering.

The public guidance for free sugars sits below a tenth of daily calories, with a lower mark below a twentieth for extra benefit. Ordinary daily intake in sweet-eating countries sits far above both, day after day, year after year. The packet in the drawer, the bottle by the till, the cereal that eats like pudding — they have made far-above feel like just-a-little. Feeling fine inside far-above proves nothing about the level. It proves how slowly the water warmed.

Think of a pot set to warm so slowly that the frog never notices the heat rise. No single degree alarms it. Each minute feels like the last. By the time the water troubles it, the heat has been high for a long time. I use that picture for pace only. I am not saying any one spoonful scalds you. I am saying gradual harm hides inside feeling fine, and feeling fine is therefore no measure.

Ask three plain questions and answer from life, not from hope.

If feeling fine meant intake was low, why do till receipts, wrappers and yearly sums tell another story? Which witness do you trust, the feeling at noon or the bin at night?

If a few meant little, why must the few be repeated at ten, at three, at five, at ten again, every day, without a day of quiet? Little things that must be repeated without pause are not little in sum.

If the level were truly low, why the stir before each dose, the empty tug that points only at sweetness and sharpens even as you chew? Low use that must be fed on schedule does not behave like low use.

There is only one honest answer that fits all three. Intake sits high because the device keeps it high, and feeling fine while intake sits high is exactly what slow warming feels like from inside the pot.

I lay that flatly because you deserve plain speech, and I lay it without threat. I am not handing you a diagnosis. I am not telling you what your body will do tomorrow. I am showing why normal is no defence. The audit stands whether you feel fine or flat, whether teeth are sound today or tender. High frequency bathes, costs, breaks attention and trains the hand, fine-feeling or not.

Keep that beside the notebook. Left column heavy. Right column empty. Feeling fine written across the top in pencil, telling you nothing about the sums below.

And now face the sharper edge of time. Do you want to keep paying for an empty column for the rest of your life? Count it not in bars but in years — money out, hours out, teeth bathed, attention broken, every single day, for something you cannot name as a gift. That is the threat inside the phrase, and you should feel its weight for a second.

Then turn the phrase over, because it has another side. Picture plain mornings, hunger met and left behind, taste bright because it is not blunted, evenings without rustle and arithmetic, work without the three o'clock tug, lived day after day for the rest of your life. No buzz bought and repaid. Only appetite and enough, doing their old quiet work. That is the reward inside the same words, and you should feel its lightness too.

Same years. Two uses. One pays for emptiness. One lives without the bill.

### THE QUESTION BY THE DITCH

We have named what it does TO you. We have failed to find what it does FOR you. One puzzle remains: if the right column is empty, why does the dose feel like help for three minutes?

Allow a small question to sit beside the notebook, not a lesson, only a question. Leave it unanswered for now and let it work while you read on.

If a man pushed you into a ditch each morning and then held out a hand to pull you out with a smile, would you call him your rescuer?

You would not. You would see the hand is part of the push. The smile does not change the order. Push first, hand second, gratitude collected for a trouble he made himself.

Keep that picture near the three-minute help. The buzz quiets a tug. The tug pointed only at sweetness. The sweetness laid the next tug while it quieted this one. Push first, hand second, thanks collected at the till.

I do not stage that scene fully here. The ditch and the hand will meet us again when the ground is ready and we look at the tiny echo and the large story separately. Today take only the question. Let it hover over each dose you watch this week. Which came first, the need or the dose that taught the need? Which hand lifted, and which hand pushed?

Once that order is seen, the empty right column stops looking mysterious. Of course the dose feels like something for three minutes. Any hand out of a ditch feels kind for three minutes. Kind feeling is not kind doing. The feeling passes. The ditch remains, waiting for the next fall on schedule.

That is why one becomes the box, why the permitted sliver carries the binge, why I feel like i cant stop once I start is the truest sentence sweet users say. The craving, once triggered, carries the eating. The hand out of one ditch drops you at the edge of the next. Call it mechanism, not character. Triggers fire in order. People do not fail in order. Devices operate in order.

You were never thanking a friend. You were thanking a motion that made its own need and sold the answer back to you. Warm to you for the thanks — who would not thank a hand? Harsh to the motion for the push — who would not condemn a push dressed as kindness?

Hold the question lightly. Do not fight the next tug with it. Only watch. Note the hour the stir arrives, the sentence that covers it, the speed of the second bite, the flatness after. An investigator does not wrestle exhibits. An investigator lets exhibits repeat until the pattern shames the device rather than the person.

### THE EMPTY GIFT

We can now call the supposed gift by its true name, and naming it will change how every later tug sounds.

From childhood we were taught to read certain packets as love, rest, reward, warmth in a hard hour. Bright colours, kind voices, jokes about needing sugary somethings to get through, praise for being good this week earning a treat when you feel you deserve one. On that teaching we took the first doses willingly and learned to translate every hollow stir as proof we needed sweetness.

That translation is the whole empty gift. A small body echo arrives — hollow, restless, mouth-busy, pointing at sweet hits. Belief reads the echo as value. Value sends the hand. The hand lays the next echo. Round and round, echo feeding story, story feeding hand, hand feeding echo, while the audit fills on the left and stays blank on the right.

Say it in plain words and feel how much falls away. You were taught to read it as a genuine treat or fuel, and the reading itself made the tug feel like proof.

Read that phrase slowly. A genuine treat or fuel. Those five words carry every excuse we print in our defence. Treat when you feel you deserve one. Fuel to get through the afternoon. Love in a wrapper. Light in a hard day. Once the phrase is spoken as one object, it can be tested as one object. Does it treat when the fourth bite is tasteless? Does it fuel when the hour after is fog? Does it love when it leaves you hiding sleeves at night? A phrase that fails every test it sets is not a description. It is a spell.

I speak that flatly because soft speech would leave you one foothold, and one foothold rebuilds the trap. There is no surviving corner where BAD SUGAR treats or fuels in any sense a plain meal does not do better without the bill. Hunger welcomes food and settles. The echo welcomes only sweetness and sharpens. Food eaten hungry brings pleasure that rests. Doses bring excitement that empties. You know both states from the inside. Trust that knowing. It is older than any slogan.

So here is the verdict the notebook forces, landed without anger and without gloom.

It does plenty TO you. It does nothing FOR you.

Read those two sentences again and test each word against your own week. Plenty TO you — teeth bathed, money out, hours out, attention broken, mood riding up and down, evenings eaten standing. Nothing FOR you — no calm that stays, no energy that stays, no comfort that stays, no taste that survives the third bite. Plenty on the left. Nothing on the right. That is not my opinion of your Tuesdays. That is your Tuesdays added up truly.

If that verdict stings, let it sting for a second and then feel what it removes. A thing that does nothing FOR you cannot be missed. It can only be left. The sting is brief. The removal is large.

You came to this chapter conceding harm but guarding benefit. You leave it with a harder task and a lighter load: name one real benefit that stays past the swallow and find none. Not one that flashes. One that stays. The mind will offer flashes. The notebook asks for stays. Offer the mind that test each time it pleads just a treat, once a week is fine, celebrating with chocolate. Flash or stay? Bought buzz or lasting gift? Every flash belongs on the left as cost. Only a stay may enter the right. None enters.

What follows from that seeing is simple and must be spoken as a command rather than a suggestion, because commands honour clear sight. From now on measure every sweet thought on one scale only. Forget the old weighing of harm against supposed good. Demand the good itself, and let emptiness answer.

I-03 — JUDGE ONLY BY WHAT IT DOES FOR YOU
Forget harm versus benefit and ask for the benefit.

SUMMARY
- The fair test is not whether harm outweighs good but whether any lasting good can be named at all.
- An ordinary day written hour by hour fills the cost column and leaves the benefit column blank.
- Frequent sweet doses bathe teeth in acid and drain money, time and mood, with brushing counting but not cancelling frequency.
- Feeling fine does not mean intake is low, because daily intake sits far above guidance and slow harm hides inside normal.
- The three-minute help is a hand out of a ditch the last dose dug, not a gift that stays.
- The supposed benefit is a belief reading a small body echo as value, summed in one empty phrase that fails every test.
