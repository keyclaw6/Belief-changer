Chapter 11
THE LAST ORDINARY TREAT

IN THIS CHAPTER
A calendar with tomorrow circled; an ordinary biscuit on an ordinary saucer; a quiet kitchen table at tea-time; a solemn vow spoken aloud; the first clear mornings after; two thin doors that look like exits

*Tomorrow never makes it safer — this one ordinary sweet, seen truly, ends it now.*

### TOMORROW NEVER ARRIVES

You know this voice better than any other because we all hosted it for years.

“I'll start after the holidays. After the birthday. When work calms down. When I am ready someday.”

It sounds kind. It sounds sensible. It asks nothing of you today and promises everything later. It lets you nod along with every page so far and still keep a small private door open at the back of the head.

Look at that door with me as an investigator, not as a mourner.

Take last month's tomorrow. What date wore it? The birthday came. Did the start come with it — or did the birthday bring its table, its corner piece, its box to finish up first? The holiday came. Did the start come — or did travel bring stations, stations bring kiosks, kiosks bring just-for-the-journey? Monday came. Did the start come — or did Monday bring a hard morning that needed getting through first?

Ask flat, with the calendar still open.

If tomorrow were safer, why has every past tomorrow arrived with its own fresh reason to wait again?

If waiting helped, why does the want grow louder the longer you wait, not quieter?

If this last sweet is so precious, why does the thought of never needing another land light in the chest, not heavy?

One honest line runs through all three. Tomorrow never thins the want. It fattens it. Each delayed day adds another rehearsal of anticipation, another memory painted bright, another proof to the whisper that life needs paper to mark time. Delay is not preparation. Delay is feeding.

We all lived that feeding. We, who swore we would start when life calmed, found life never calmed while the daily flood-and-drop kept manufacturing its own storms. The three o'clock fog made work feel harder, so work became a reason to wait. The evening restlessness made home feel tight, so home became a reason to wait. The very discomfort the last dose dug became the excuse to postpone leaving the spade.

The fact is plain. Waiting is not caution. Waiting is the con talking in your accent.

“I just need the right moment.”

Print it whole to answer it whole. There has never been a right moment inside the loop because the loop spoils every moment it touches. There will always be a birthday somewhere ahead. There will always be a dinner, a tired Friday, a bright holiday, a dull Tuesday. A line that moves for occasions is not a line. It is a ribbon in wind.

Waiting keeps. Seeing ends.

### AN ORDINARY BISCUIT ON AN ORDINARY SAUCER

I am not asking you to stage a ceremony. No last supper. No laboratory tasting. No big bag bought for the occasion.

Take the kind you actually eat on an ordinary day. The biscuit from the tin you know. The square from the bar in the drawer. The spoon from the pudding you serve without thinking. Take it normally, at your table, with your usual cup beside it. Kettle warm. Feet on lino. Room as it is.

This time do one thing differently. Pay attention.

Not with dread. Not with guilt. With eyes open the way an investigator watches. Notice the whole of it while it happens.

Notice the hand. Quick. A little too quick for hunger. Notice the first bite loud in the head, large with promise, and how fast the promise thins. Notice the second bite already less than the first, the third already a chore of chewing, the mouth filming, the tongue asking for tea to wash itself. Notice the sweetness turning cloying at the edges, thick, sticky, too much and not enough at once. Notice the pause after the swallow when the head does not say thank you but says again.

Ask with the saucer still in front of you.

Did this bite feed hunger — or did it interrupt it?

Did the mouth grow cleaner — or did it grow coated and asking?

Did the afternoon grow brighter after such bites in the past — or did it grow fogged and reaching?

We all recorded the same audit when we truly watched. The glow lived in the seconds before, in the picture, in the rustle. The eating itself was blunt hurry followed by flatness. The pleasure you remember was never in the paper. It was in the room, the pause, the company, the hour — and the paper only ever sneaked a ride on those good things.

Taste that plain. The dose does not add. It borrows and blurs.

Stare the ordinary sweet down to ordinary size and something large happens in a quiet way. The crown slips. The jewel shows as glass. No packet can re-crown it once your own mouth has told on it.

Paper watched. Crown slipped.

### ARE YOU READY TO CROSS?

I will not hurry you past your own sight. I have seen too many white-knuckle weeks to mistake hurry for help.

Readiness here is not courage. It is clarity. Do you see that there is no safe corner left inside BAD SUGAR? The Friday tin taught waiting. The single carried the box. The bare shelf left the mind stocked. Tomorrow moved with the calendar. The swap kept the ritual. If those rooms are settled in you — not as phrases, but as your own weeks remembered — then you are ready.

You should be champing to cross. A little nervous, yes, and underneath the nerves a tug forward. A wish to have the thinking done.

If you are not there, do not summon grit. Put this chapter down and read back through the weeks that still glow for you. No shame in that. I did the same. We all circled once or twice before the lock clicked, pacing by the open cage from habit. Return when the glow is gone and the forward tug is stronger than the backward picture.

If you are there, hear this plain.

Remember, you have absolutely nothing to lose and everything to gain.

Nothing real leaves your plate. Hunger stays. Satisfaction stays. Meals stay. Company stays. Mornings stay. What leaves is the fog between you and them, the three o'clock slump, the evening rustle-and-regret, the Monday-to-Thursday pictures of Friday. No real food is taken. No real joy is taken. The only thing taken is the hole that the last dose dug.

Ask the last backward whisper once more, because it speaks in your accent:

“What if I miss it?”

Miss what? The coated mouth? The second reach? The box empty before the swallow ends? The clean days spent picturing the allowed day? You do not miss a ditch once you see it is a ditch. You miss nothing you never owned.

All you have to do is follow all the instructions.

You have followed them this far with eyes open. One remains, and it is not a burden. It is a door you walk through on purpose, with the saucer still on the table.

### THE VOW AT THE TABLE

Hold the whole picture steady while the room stays ordinary.

The saucer with its crumbs. The cup warm in the hand. The tin on the shelf holding no vote unless you hand it one. The thought of a kept sweet may cross, thin as cloud, and pass while talk goes on, while supper cooks, while the evening runs its good length. A passing cloud is weather, not orders.

You have seen the Friday glow was waiting painted as taste. You have seen the single carry all. You have seen the house-rule move the address but not the leash. You have seen tomorrow fatten the want. You have seen the swap keep the hand on the same clock. With that sight, there is no waiting left to feed and no countdown left to keep.

That long pull both ways, want one way and dread the other, was the tug-of-war of craving and fear.

Both ropes belonged to one hand. That hand was the belief that any corner fills. Cut-down sawed at one rope while feeding the other. Total sight drops both at once. Not dimmed. Dark. The chest where the old pull lived feels strange for an hour and then feels like morning air through an opened window.

Speak now, quietly and solemnly, not to me but to yourself, as a settled decision, not a wish:

This ordinary sweet is my last. I close the whole line behind me as of now.

Say it aloud if you can. Hear your own voice close it. Let the crumbs stay where they are. Push the saucer aside. Stand. Rinse the cup. The act is small. The meaning is total.

With that sight held and that sentence spoken, there is only one clean decision left to make, and you are ready to make it now because the argument is already won.

### THE FIRST DAYS AFTER

You leave it easily, immediately and permanently.

Not over weeks. Not after a test period. As of the saucer pushed aside. The decision does not need time to become true. Time only reveals what sight has already done.

What will the body say in the next days? Hear it plain so no whisper can dress it up later.

For a day or two, perhaps two to five at most, there may be a faint echo. A thin grumble at the old hours. Three o'clock glancing toward the drawer. Evening glancing toward the cupboard. A little emptiness, a little twitch, a little thought of something sweet now. Small. Passing. Thinning each time it is not fed.

Do not measure it. Do not name it large. Smile at it the way you smile at a clock still ticking after it has been unplugged. The tick does not mean the clock still runs the house. It means the hands are running down.

Ask with the echo present.

Does a faint grumble prove a need — or does it prove an old habit losing its voice?

Does its passing in days prove a life sentence — or does it prove a brief hump already behind you by the weekend?

Does feeding it quiet it — or does feeding it rewind it to full volume?

One honest answer fits all three. The echo quiets when unfed and restarts when fed. Its mildness is the proof. This was never a mighty hunger demanding battle. It was a tiny loop kept loud by a large belief, and the belief just went dark.

Drink water. Eat when hungry. Stop when satisfied. Sleep as usual. Change nothing else in life. Let hunger lead and let meals close when they close. The body knows its work and returns to it fast once the daily flood-and-drop stops interrupting.

And for the thought itself — because thoughts will cross, thin as cloud — keep one set reply ready from this hour on. Do not argue with the cloud. Do not push it away. Answer it with the whole chest:

BRILLIANT! I'M FREE!

Think it whenever BAD SUGAR crosses your mind. At the till. At the party table. On the sofa at ten. Let the thought become the bell for joy. The same crossing that once meant pictures now means lightness that you no longer feed the loop. Use it word for word. Keep it forever.

Cloud crossed. Joy answered.

### THE TWO THIN DOORS

Two doors will try to look like exits later. Both are painted on the wall. Know them now so they arrive pre-labelled.

The first comes on a bad day.

Money tight. Child ill. Boss sharp. Rain at the window. The old whisper returns in kind tones: “Just for today. You have earned a break. One will steady you.”

Print it whole to answer it whole. Did the dose ever steady a bad day — or did it film the mouth, fog the hour after, and add regret to trouble? Will one quiet the storm — or will it restart the clock that guarantees the next storm at three o'clock tomorrow? A bad day needs supper, sleep, company, air. It never needed paper.

Ask inside the bad hour.

Did past bad-day doses leave the day kinder — or leave the evening tighter and the morning heavier?

Does kindness today lie in re-digging yesterday's hole — or in letting the day stay hard without making it fogged as well?

One answer ends it. The bad-day offer is the old rescuer dressed up again, the hand that pushed offering to pull out. Thank the sight that names it and let the day run its course with mouth clear.

The second comes on a good day.

Sun out. Work done. Laughter in the room. Someone holds out a box with love in the gesture. The whisper changes coat: “Just one can't hurt. You are strong now. Prove it by tasting.”

Print it whole to answer it whole. If one cannot hurt, why did one always carry all? If strength proves by tasting, why did every tasted proof end with Thursday creeping into Saturday and Monday already picturing Friday? Strength here proves by seeing, not by sampling. You do not test a closed lock by rattling it for fun.

Ask with the box still held out.

Does love live in the paper — or in the hand offering, the faces round, the hour itself?

Does one keep the evening bright — or does it dim the brightness by re-crowning the glass?

Does refusing the paper refuse the people — or does it keep you fully with the people while the cloud passes?

One straight line answers all three. Take the company. Leave the paper. Pity the box a second if you must — all that hurry and fog still ahead for those still caught — and turn back to the talk with lightness in the chest:

BRILLIANT! I'M FREE!

A slip, should your hand ever move before sight catches up, is a rumble strip, not a crash. It does not unmake the vow. It warns that a picture slipped back in. Spit the lesson out with the mouthful, name the script that spoke, renew the same sentence at the same table, and walk on. One unchosen crumb rides out like a jolt. A deliberate dose steers back in. Guard the sight and the path stays straight. Panic never kept anyone clear. Sight keeps clear.

You stand now past the saucer with the whole board dark behind you. Kettle warm. Feet on lino. Tea in hand. Mornings ahead unpictured by Friday. Shops ahead unlit by hunt. Evenings ahead long in a good way. Hold that ordinary room and let it widen.

SUMMARY
- Tomorrow never arrives with a safer start, waiting only feeds the want it claims to calm.
- An ordinary sweet watched closely shows cloying hurry and flatness, not a gift.
- Readiness is clear sight that no corner fills, not courage or perfect calm.
- A quiet spoken vow at an ordinary table closes the line as of now.
- Any faint echo in the next days is brief and small, thinning when unfed.
- A bad-day offer and a good-day taste are painted doors, both reopen the same loop.
- A passing sweet thought gets one joyful reply and life goes on with meals, hunger and company intact.
