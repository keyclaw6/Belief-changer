Chapter 13
YOUR DAYS WERE NEVER MADE OF SMOKE

IN THIS CHAPTER
Cold kitchen floor and kettle steam, shop glass and lunch queue, work doorway and mid-morning air, evening table with friends and drinks, tired pictures met with a bright reply.

*You have not lost your ordinary days. You get to live them exactly as they are.*

### Morning Feet, Kettle Steam

You wake to the first morning that is not ruled by the clock.

For years the feet on the cold floor in the morning moved before thought did. Alarm, hand to the pack, throat still dry, first dose dragged on empty lungs while the kettle stayed unfilled. We told ourselves it started the day. It started nothing but the timetable.

This morning watch what actually starts it.

Feet touch cold lino. You stand. You fill the kettle and hear it rise. You run cold water on your face. Your mouth tastes of sleep, not ash. Your chest takes its first pull of the day without heat on top of it, and it does not ask for shallower next time. Nothing is missing from your hands because your hands have their ordinary work — tap, mug, window latch.

"I will feel flat without it on the step."

I hear you. We all hear that thin voice on day one. So ask it straight, with your mug warm in your hands:

Did the kettle ever need smoke to boil?
Did cold water ever need a lit end to wake you?
Did your legs ever need permission from a pack to stand up?

You know. The kettle boiled. The water woke. The legs stood. The dose never lifted the blanket, never made the light, never poured the tea. It only stood beside the morning and took the bow.

Smoke it through in memory if the picture tugs. Three seconds of shoulders dropping while the hollow paused, then dry mouth, then checking the clock for the next gap. That was never morning. That was interruption of morning. This — steam on the glass, clean teeth, food that tastes of itself — this is morning with nothing standing in front of it.

It is dull in the best way. No thunder. No tearing. The quiet is the receipt.

### The Shop Queue At Lunch

By lunch the street does its ordinary test.

You walk in for food and the glass is there, rows of packs behind the till, the old price list you knew by heart. The hand remembers the small scrape of cellophane, the flat weight in the pocket, the counting of coins for the next box. We paid that meter all day to keep the fade quiet for minutes.

Stand in that queue as you are now — a non-smoker who sees the glass plain.

Let the line move. Let the man ahead buy his box and step out to the wind end to manage ash. Watch his shoulders. Does he look soothed, or tapped on the ribs and answering? Watch his eyes slide to the door before he has finished paying. That slide is not pleasure. That is the timetable knocking in daylight.

"What if I feel left out at the till?"

Left out of what? Ask your own pocket:

Did paying ever make lunch taste better?
Did a soft corner and loose cellophane ever add an hour to your life?
If the box held something honeyed, why did every box end in grey dust and a smell you washed off?

You know the answers because your coat and your curtains know them. You paid to quiet a hunger the paying lit again. You do not pay now, and the hunger does not build without end. It knocks and passes when unfed, because there is nothing behind it but a fading echo.

Take your food. Feel the light pocket. Walk out with both hands holding what you came for. The till keeps its glass. You keep your afternoon.

### The Break Outside The Door

Mid-morning the old break calls.

Work piles, shoulders tighten, eyes slide to the clock and the doorway. For years we read that slide as need for a pause and need for a dose in one. We stepped out, lit, talked half-talk while watching the burning tip, came back with dry mouth and the same pile waiting.

Take the pause. Leave the paper.

Push the door and step into mid-morning air. Feel it on the face without heat laid over it. Roll the shoulders. Drink water. Let the hands hold mug or rail or nothing. Talk if there is talk. Look at the street if there is nothing to say. The pause does the work — the air, the stand, the minute away from the screen — not the burning leaf.

"But the break feels short with nothing in my fingers."

Hold that thought up to what you have already seen. Were your fingers ever empty on a break? They held hammer, pen, mug, steering wheel for years before the timetable taught them one more grip. And did that grip lengthen the break, or cut talk in half to watch ash?

It cut talk in half. It is the other way around.

I stood on those steps for years believing the step needed the lit end. The step never did. The legs wanted standing. The eyes wanted distance. The lungs wanted air. Your body does on its own what no timetable taught it. You only have to stop interrupting it.

Go back in when you are ready, not when the tip burns down. The work sits where you left it, untouched by smoke and untouched by lack of it. The minute did what minutes do when no one sets them on fire.

### That Late-Night Patio With Friends And Drinks

Evening comes kind, and kind is where the old script speaks best.

That late-night patio with friends and drinks — laughter, glasses sweat, someone tips a pack toward you. We all know that hand. We all know that grin. "Go on. One won't hurt." For years that moment dressed itself as belonging itself.

Sit in it as you are now.

Hear the talk carry without your help. Someone tells the long story twice. Someone laughs too loud. The night air moves. The glasses clink. Nothing in that warmth asks for paper and ash laid over it. The company does all. The drink does its own small loosening. The dark does its own softening. Smoke only ever stood beside them and claimed them.

"You don't want to be the only one not lighting."

I know that pinch. I stood in it with empty hands and thought all eyes were on me. Ask the pinch straight:

Did talk ever stop because you had nothing burning?
Did laughter ever check your pockets before it came?
If one never hurt, why did every one you ever took call for the next within the hour?

You know. Talk did not stop. Laughter did not check. One always called, because one wakes the hollow and gives it voice again in your own accent, so you mistake it for yourself.

Look at the hands around the table with clear eyes. The lit ends moving up and down, the stepping to the wind end, the half-missed line while managing ash — does that really look like enjoyment? You are not missing. They are tapped.

When any picture of smoke crosses your mind — tray, doorway, offered pack — answer it at once with the same bright reply, spoken inward as lived fact:

YIPPEE! I'M FREE!

Say it as joy, not as effort. Mean it as pity for the old timetable, not envy of those still in it. The reasons are already finished. The stain told them. The ash told them. The talk tonight tells them again.

### When An Old Picture Walks Past

It will walk past again. Not as pain. As picture.

Tired hour, phone down, papers still there. Rain on the shelter outside the shop. Coffee steam with nothing beside it. Your own hand half-lifting before thought. The trap speaks in your voice because it lived in your mouth for years. That does not make it you.

You will not need to debate it. You know the uniform too well to follow it.

There are pictures that promise to close the day and pictures that promise to prove command. The first leaves the papers and the strain exactly where they were, plus ash on the tongue. The second opens the box it claims to check. Both are old mail with your name misspelled.

Do not picture the draw. Do not weigh. Keep to the talk, the work, the walk you were in. Drink water. Move. Keep hands in ordinary work. The knock grows fainter when no one answers, and one morning you notice the morning asked nothing at all.

I stood where you stand, on cold steps hearing that whisper, and I tell you plain: each promised a taste and delivered a timetable. Seeing the livery is enough. You do not follow a con once you know the hand.

If in past years you once lit after a clear spell, read it only as a warning light. It does not prove you are broken and it does not doom what comes next. It proves one cord was left plugged in. Pull that last cord now by keeping the belief shut. You keep no dose because you keep no doubt that a dose gives — not because you were told to keep none, but because your own eyes have shown you.

Hold the standing minute. Pack shut. Tray cool. Hands holding what they hold. Chest taking air without heat. Eyes on the room as it is. From that stubbed end, you are a non-smoker. Do not wait to become one. Do not serve days to earn it.

YIPPEE! I'M FREE!

SUMMARY
- Mornings run on kettle, water and light, with nothing missing from the hands.
- The shop glass holds boxes that add nothing to food, taste or afternoon.
- The work pause works by air, standing and distance, with the pile waiting the same either way.
- The night holds talk, laughter and company that need no lit end to keep going.
- An old picture is old script in a familiar voice, answered at once and left to pass.
