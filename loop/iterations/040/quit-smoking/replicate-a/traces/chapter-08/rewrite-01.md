Chapter 8
THE KETTLE, THE PLATE AND THE OPEN DOOR

*Morning, food and a pause please you on their own — the dose only got in the way.*

### FEET ON THE COLD FLOOR

We know that first minute by heart.

Alarm. Eyes half open. Feet have not touched the floor before the hand finds the pack. Lighter. First dose on empty lungs. Cough, then the shoulders drop, then we say we have started the day.

I started a thousand days that way. Shirt cold on the shoulders. Kitchen dark. Kettle empty because I had not yet filled it. I stood at the cracked door and dragged hard and called the bite waking up.

Live that same minute now without the dose in your hand, and watch what was really there.

Feet touch cold lino. That cold is awake. You stand. You fill the kettle. Water hits steel. Click. Hiss. You breathe while you wait, right down to the bottom of the lungs, the way a sleeper breathes before the day has named anything. Steam rises. Cup warms the palms. First mouthful of hot tea or coffee scalds pleasantly and the nose opens.

“I won’t wake properly without it.”

You say it, I hear it, I said it for years. So answer plainly, with the kettle in front of you:

Did the kettle need smoke to boil?
Did your lungs need smoke to fill?
Did the cold on your feet fail to wake you until the bite came?

You know. The cold woke you. The standing woke you. The light coming up at the window woke you. The dose added a sting on empty lungs and a dizzy quiet for minutes, and we read the sting as opening and the quiet as clearing.

Try it as a watched morning. Keep the dose in the pack. Feet, floor, tap, kettle, cup, window. Notice the order. The eyes sting, then water clears them. The mouth is stale, then water and hot drink clean it. The chest is tight from night, then three slow breaths loosen it without any teaching. No step misses a dose because no step asks for one.

We called that first dose appetite, habit, need. The fact is duller. The hand moved first because night was the longest gap, so the tug woke first. What stirs is an empty, slightly restless, slightly edgy little tug, and nothing more. After hours without feeding it speaks loudest in the morning, so we mistook loud for important. Loud is only long unfed, not true.

Watch the order again, slower, because the morning lies fastest.

You wake. The body has been still for seven hours. Mouth dry. Bladder full. Light thin at the curtains. The brain comes online in pieces — sound first, then ache, then list of the day. In that half-lit minute the tug is already awake, hollow under the ribs, attention fidgety. The hand has learned to answer that hollow before the feet answer the floor. Pack. Lighter. Step to the door. Shiver. Drag. The shiver and the drag fuse in memory, and memory files it as cause: drag woke me.

But separate what happened. What woke the feet? Cold. What woke the bladder? Fullness. What woke the eyes? Light and water. What woke the nose? Steam. The dose woke nothing. It stung lungs still narrowed from night, and the sting passed for signal.

Ask the shirt-cold minute:

If the dose woke you, why did the cold wake the skin before the lighter lit?
If the dose cleared you, why did the first deep breath of plain air clear more than the first drag?
If the dose started the day, why did the kettle, the cup, the window, the piss, the stretch all start without it?

You know because you have lived mornings without it when you had to — illness, travel, a house with a new baby where you stood in the garden in rain and told yourself you could not wake indoors. You woke indoors. You washed, dressed, found keys, answered the door. Mouth went on working. Legs went on carrying. The day started. The only thing missing was the cough and the ash taste with tea.

I remember a winter morning when the lighter failed. Flint dead. Shops shut. I stood with an unlit dose in my mouth like a fool, cold biting my ankles, kettle screaming behind me. I took the dose out, drank the tea standing, breathed, shivered, woke. Then I laughed at the doorway with the dead lighter in my hand. Nothing had failed but the flint. Waking had gone on as usual. The body had done its old work while I had been crediting the spark.

That is the whole morning. Feet. Water. Heat. Light. Breath. The smoker’s morning adds cough on the doorstep, ash with tea, shallow breathing because deep hurts for a minute, then calls the whole bundle pleasure. Take the bundle apart and the pleasure stays where it belongs — with heat, light, water, breath.

Your morning asks for nothing to be added. It is complete with feet, water, heat, light.

### THE PLATE THAT STAYS WARM

You know that old reward pellet we used to light on finished work.

Eat one ordinary meal now and stay to the end of it without moving.

Cook or buy what you like. Sit. Eat while it is hot. Notice what your mouth already knows how to do. Teeth, tongue, saliva. Heat, salt, fat, sweet. Chew slows near the end. The last mouthfuls taste strongest because hunger has quieted enough to let flavour speak. Put the fork down. The plate is warm under your hand. The taste lingers on the tongue for minutes, clear and full, changing as you breathe through the nose.

“Food won’t finish without it.”

That is the voice. So ask it, with the plate still warm:

Did the food taste of tobacco while you chewed, or did tobacco arrive after and cover what you tasted?
Did the lingering taste need burning to stay, or did burning drive it out?
When you lit at the table, did the meal feel closed or did the mouth feel spoiled for the next hour?

You know because your tongue knows. Smoke does not seal flavour. It coats it. Smokers finish and light and within two drags the mouth is dry ash and the nose is dull, and they call that rounding off. A man who never smoked finishes and sits and tastes food for ten minutes after eating, and calls nothing anything. He simply tastes.

I tested this on a meal I loved. Soup, bread, cheese, apple. One night with the dose at the end, one night without. With the dose, the apple after the cheese was smoke and hurry and standing. Without, the apple was cold juice and crunch and the cheese still on the tongue underneath. Same apple. Same mouth. One night stolen, one night owned. The work of the cook, the work of my own day that paid for it — all of it was there both nights. Only one night let me stay for it.

We feared meals would turn flat. It never fixed the itch. It caused it. The flatness we blamed on missing was the coating we called finishing.

Leave the plate where it is. Drink water. Breathe. Talk. The taste thins by itself into satisfaction, the way a note thins after it is struck.

### THE HOUR THAT FOLLOWS THE PLATE

Stay where you are. Do not get up to chase the taste or to bury it. Let the hour after the meal show what a finished meal does when left alone.

The plate sits. The cup sits beside it, half full. The room holds the smell of cooking for a little while, then the smell thins. Your mouth goes on working though chewing has stopped. Saliva comes, swallows, comes again. The tongue finds traces — salt at the sides, sweet at the front, fat smoothed across the middle — and each swallow carries a fainter echo. No effort in it. No decision. The body closes the meal the way a pond settles after a stone.

Notice attention in that hour. It does not vanish. It returns.

First ten minutes, attention stays low and wide. You hear the clock, the street, the other room. Talk comes easy because there is nothing to finish in the mouth. You answer in full sentences. You laugh without coughing halfway. If you are alone, you look out of the window without hunting for something to do with your hands. Hands rest on the warm cup, or wash the pan, or turn a page. They are occupied because life occupies them, not because a gap occupies them.

Second ten minutes, attention narrows by itself toward the next thing. Dishes. A letter. A bench to wipe. A child’s question. A page to write. You stand, carry the plate to the sink, run water. Hot water on hands. Clink. Steam again, different from kettle steam, with soap in it. The mouth still holds a faint trace while the hands work, and the trace does not fight the work. It rides along underneath, like music from another room while you sweep.

Ask that working minute:

Did the hands need a lit errand to wash, wipe, carry?
Did the mind need a lit pause to leave the table and take up the next task?
Did the trace on the tongue pull you back to the table, or did it walk with you to the sink and thin there?

You know. Washing went on. Wiping went on. The trace thinned while you worked and left no order behind it.

Third ten minutes, the body shifts into the afternoon’s posture. Shoulders drop from eating posture to working posture. Breath finds its daytime depth. You sit to write, to sew, to mend, to add numbers, to read. The first lines come slow, as they always do after food, blood busy elsewhere. Then they come. You re-read a sentence. You cross a word. You look up, look down. The mind wanders to the morning, to money, to weather, then returns without scolding. This wandering and returning is attention doing its natural work after a meal. It is not a hole. It is digestion and thought sharing the same blood for a while.

Smokers misread that slow twenty minutes as lack. We used to stand at its edge, feel the dip in sharpness, and say the meal had not finished us. We lit to sharpen. For minutes the tug quieted and the mind felt bright, and we credited brightness to the dose. Then the brightness thinned and the page still needed writing, and we lit the hour again.

Live the hour without that lighting and watch what sharpness does on its own.

It dips, then climbs. It wanders, then gathers. At minute forty after eating you look up and realize you have read three pages without remembering the tongue. At minute fifty you answer a message in full, with humour, without leaving your chair. At minute sixty you stand, stretch, notice hunger is still far off, notice the mouth is plain water-clean, notice the next work is already half-shaped in the head. No bright snap announced it. No quiet announced it. The hour simply carried you from fed to ready, the way a path carries you from gate to field without a gate at the other end.

I lived that hour on a day of writing I had dreaded. Lunch of stew and bread. No dose at the table. I kept expecting the afternoon to stall, to itch, to demand. The stall came as a soft ten minutes of staring. I let it sit. I washed cups. I looked at rain on glass. I wrote a bad paragraph. I wrote a better one. By three the pages held the morning’s thread again. The dread had been a memory of old gaps, not a reading of this hour. This hour had its own rhythm — slow start, steady middle, clear end — and the rhythm needed no props.

Notice wanting in that hour, because wanting is where we feared loss most.

After a meal smokers want. Mouth wants. Hands want. We called it wanting a dose, but watch it closer while the taste lingers clean. What does the mouth want? Movement. Swallow. Water. Talk. A toothpick. A second apple. What do the hands want? Hold. Warmth. Wash. Turn. What does the mind want? Close. Next. Rest before next. These wantings are small, multiple, shifting. A dose pretended to answer them all with one act — put something in mouth, put something in hands, mark an end. It answered none. It only paused the tug for minutes while wantings waited.

Leave the wantings unpaused and they answer themselves in turn. Mouth drinks water and settles. Hands wash and settle. Mind closes one task and opens the next and settles. Each settling is small and complete. Together they make the large settling we used to ask smoke to give — satisfaction that lasts past the table.

Talk in that hour, if there is talk. After food with others, voices soften. Stories stretch. Nobody performs. You listen with the taste still faint, nod, ask, laugh. The listening is full because attention is not splitting toward the door. You do not watch the clock for the moment to slip out. You do not rehearse the standing, the pushing back, the pocket. You stay. Staying lets the other person finish. Finishing lets you remember what was said at four o’clock when the day asks. Memory holds because you were there for the whole hour, not for the meal plus the step outside.

Work in that hour, if there is work. The work after lunch is never the morning’s sprint. It is plod, mend, file, carry. Plod suits a fed body. You carry boxes, you file, you sweep, you drive short runs. The body moves steadily, breath even, legs willing. The mouth, left clean, does not call for pause. The hands, left empty, do not call for errand. Pauses come when the task pauses — unload done, room done — and they come as standing, looking, drinking, not as lighting. Each small completion gives its own close. The day strings closes together without ash between them.

Walk in that hour, if there is a walk. Short street after food, coat on, air on face. Food sits warm inside while legs move. The tongue still faint, the nose open. You smell rain, bread from a shop, diesel, leaves. Smells arrive distinct because the nose has not been dulled at the table. Steps fall even. Breath deepens to meet them without cough. At the corner you turn for home with the body lighter than when you set out, digestion helped by movement, mind helped by distance. No stop to light. No stop to finish. The walk finishes itself at the gate.

This is what completeness does when left to run. It does not end at the fork-down. It spreads. Taste lingers, thins, leaves cleanliness. Attention dips, gathers, holds. Wanting shifts, settles, quiets. Work resumes, continues, closes. An hour that we feared would gape stays full without stuffing. Full with water, talk, wash, walk, page, sink, street. Ordinary stuff, but enough. Enough is the proof we asked for at the table, delivered not in a flash but across sixty minutes of lived time.

You do not need to measure it. You will feel it as absence of hunting. No checking pocket while listening. No watching door while talking. No counting minutes to the step out. The hour holds you because you hold the hour — present for its small closes, not pulled to a corner for a brief quiet that would have broken it into before and after.

Let that absence teach. Hunting was never hunger for food or rest. Hunting was the interval asking for its coin at the old hour. Unfed, the interval speaks for a while — hollow, fidgety, hands wanting errand — then thins when eyes stay on dishes, pages, faces. The thinning is not will. It is time passing while you live. Minutes do the work when belief no longer hires the dose to do it.

Sit one more minute at the sink with clean hands in warm water. The plate you left is now washed. The taste you left is now gone, gone the way it should go — thinned to nothing, not burned to ash. The stomach is warm and quiet. The head is clear enough for next. Nothing hangs. Nothing calls. The meal has not only fed. It has led, step by step, into workable afternoon. That leading is the consequence the table promised. Stayed with, it keeps its promise past the table.

### AIR ON THE FACE AT TEN AND THREE

The break is the last place we trust.

Mid-morning the screen blurs. Mid-afternoon the shoulders ache. The huddle moves to the door. Lighter clicks. We went because we needed air, and then we filled the air with smoke and called it a break.

You remember the stairs breath, where we arrived puffed and a man who never smoked breathed easy.

Take the break apart and live it clean.

Push back the chair. Stand. Walk to the door. Cold or warm, the outside touches the face at once. Eyes lift from close work to distance — roof, sky, street. Shoulders roll. Jaw unclenches. You breathe without thinking about breathing, in through the nose, out through the mouth, the chest rising properly for the first time in two hours. Blood moves in the legs. Hands hold a mug and warm themselves. Talk, if you want talk. Quiet, if you want quiet. Ten minutes of not working.

“I need to step out for one or it isn’t a break.”

Watch that thought with the door open:

Did the air enter your lungs, or did the smoke?
Did your legs need walking, or lighting?
Did the pause need burning to count as pause?

You know. Legs needed walking after sitting. Eyes needed distance after close print. Lungs needed depth after shallow desk breath. The dose gave none of those. It gave a sting and a brief quiet of the tug while legs still ached and eyes still stung, and we mistook quiet for rest.

Stay out the full ten. Notice how the tug behaves when you do not answer it with a story. It stirs around the old hour. Hollow. Fidgety. Hands wanting an errand. It does not grow teeth. It does not stop you talking or laughing or watching clouds. It tints the minutes grey for a little while, then thins when you keep your eyes on the street and your hands on the mug. The grey was never the morning or the work or your nature. It was the interval asking for a coin.

I stood those breaks for years believing empty hands meant missing something. Empty hands hold the mug. Empty hands button the coat. Empty hands go into pockets for warmth and come out for nothing. Nothing missing. The chat holds the warmth. The pause holds the rest. The dose only held the interruptions — leave to light, leave to finish, leave to light again.

A break is a pause from work. Smoke never paused anything. It paused the pause.

Live the doorstep minute by minute and the pause proves itself.

First minute, the shock of air. Face tightens, then loosens. Nose runs a little. You sniff, wipe, laugh. The body wakes from chair-sleep. Second minute, the legs report. Calves ache, then ease as you shift weight. You pace two steps, lean on rail, pace back. Third minute, the eyes report. They water, then clear, then take distance hungrily — chimney, cloud, bus roof. You watch without purpose and purpose returns by itself.

Fourth minute, talk finds its level. Not work talk. Weather, food, a joke from morning. You answer, you add, you listen. The mug warms the fingers between sips. Fifth minute, quiet if you want it. Others go in. You stay. Street sounds fill — brakes, gulls, a drill far off. Breath deepens to meet them. Sixth, seventh, eighth, the shoulders learn they are not needed to hold the screen. They drop. Neck rolls. Jaw yawns. A real yawn, wide, wet-eyed, that shakes the head clear better than any drag ever did.

Ninth minute, the tug, if it came, has thinned to background. You notice you have not thought of the pack for three minutes while thinking of plenty else. Tenth, you push off the rail, roll the cup in the hands, head back in with legs looser, eyes clearer, lungs fuller than when you stepped out. Work waits where you left it, but you meet it with a body that has moved, breathed, looked far.

That is a break. Air, distance, movement, warmth, talk or quiet. No sting. No ash. No watching the stub while the story ends without you.

### YOUR BODY ALREADY KNOWS

This is the part we have been trained not to believe, so look at it with your own senses as witness.

Your nose knows smoke from food without being told. Your tongue knows clear from coated without lessons. Your lungs know deep from shallow without practice. You did not teach a baby to breathe. You did not teach yourself to taste soup. The body arrived with its instruments tuned.

We jammed the signal and then credited the jam with music. Morning cough we called clearing. Meal coating we called closing. Shallow breath at the door we called rest. Each time the body protested cleanly — cough, dull nose, tight chest — and the big story above translated protest as proof of pleasure. Below only a small tug complained for its feed. Above a lifetime of pictures said the complaint meant you cannot wake, taste, or pause without help.

You can. You have, in every gap you were forced to sit through. Long meeting. Long film. Long flight. Mouth went on tasting after. Lungs went on breathing after. Sleep came without a dose in the mouth. Waking came without a dose in the hand until the hand taught it otherwise. The instruments never left. They were only covered.

So do not learn waking, tasting, pausing as skills. Do not rehearse them. Live the next ordinary round as it comes — kettle, plate, doorway — with nothing added and nothing avoided. Let the tongue linger. Let the chest fill. Let the hands stay empty. When the old hour speaks, hear it for what it is and keep drinking your tea, keep talking, keep walking. The tug fades when unfed on its short clock. The body stays.

You do not need a prop to wake, to finish, to rest. You need eyes that no longer credit the thief with the house he robbed.

Morning proved the feet wake before the lighter. The plate proved the tongue tastes longest when left alone, and the hour after proved the leading lasts. The doorway proved the pause rests through air and movement. Three rooms, one finding. The body does its work in each without teaching, without topping, without marking. Trust what you have watched with your own mouth, nose, chest, legs across one lived day.

I-08 — TRUST YOUR BODY TO BREATHE AND TASTE
Your body knows freedom without being taught.

SUMMARY
- Morning woke me through cold, light, water and heat, not through a sting on empty lungs.
- Food lingered clear and led into workable afternoon without coating.
- A pause rested me through air, distance and movement with empty hands.
- The stir at the old hour was small and passing, not a picture of what I am.
- Nose, tongue and lungs did their work without lessons once left uncovered.
