Chapter 8
THE KETTLE, THE PLATE AND THE OPEN DOOR

IN THIS CHAPTER — cold floor and kettle steam, the plate with taste still on the tongue, doorway air and empty hands

*Morning, food and a pause please you on their own — the dose only got in the way.*

### FEET ON THE COLD FLOOR

We know that first minute by heart.

Alarm. Eyes half open. Feet have not touched the floor before the hand finds the pack. Lighter. First dose on empty lungs. Cough, then the shoulders drop, then we say we have started the day.

I started a thousand days that way. Shirt cold on the shoulders. Kitchen dark. Kettle empty because I had not yet filled it. I stood at the cracked door and dragged hard and called the bite waking up.

Live that same minute now without the dose in your hand, and watch what was really there.

Feet touch cold lino. That cold is awake. You stand. You fill the kettle. Water hits steel. Click. Hiss. You breathe while you wait, right down to the bottom of the lungs, the way a sleeper breathes before the day has named anything. Steam rises. Cup warms the palms. First mouthful of hot tea or coffee scalds pleasantly and the nose opens.

“I won’t wake properly without it.”

You say it, I hear it, I said it for years. So answer plainly, with the kettle in front of you:

Did the kettle need smoke to boil?
Did your lungs need smoke to fill?
Did the cold on your feet fail to wake you until the bite came?

You know. The cold woke you. The standing woke you. The light coming up at the window woke you. The dose added a sting on empty lungs and a dizzy quiet for minutes, and we read the sting as opening and the quiet as clearing.

Try it as a watched morning. Keep the dose in the pack. Feet, floor, tap, kettle, cup, window. Notice the order. The eyes sting, then water clears them. The mouth is stale, then water and hot drink clean it. The chest is tight from night, then three slow breaths loosen it without any teaching. No step misses a dose. No step waits for one.

We called that first dose appetite, habit, need. The fact is duller. The hand moved first because night was the longest gap, so the tug woke first. What stirs is an empty, slightly restless, slightly edgy little tug, and nothing more. After hours without feeding it speaks loudest in the morning, so we mistook loud for important. Loud is only long unfed, not true.

Watch the smoker’s morning beside yours. He coughs on the doorstep in his shirt while the kettle boils unwatched inside. He tastes ash with his first tea. He breathes shallow because deep breathing hurts for a minute. Then he says he enjoys a morning dose. What did he enjoy? The steam? He left it. The quiet kitchen? He left it for the step. The first clean breath? He burned it.

Your morning asks for nothing to be added. It is complete with feet, water, heat, light.

### THE PLATE THAT STAYS WARM

We paid for finished work the same way.

Finished a class. Finished a paragraph. Drove the long road. Drove to the shops. Each done thing got that old reward pellet lit on top, and we said the pellet closed it nicely.

I know that pellet. I laid courses and pages and miles end to end with lights between them like full stops. The full stop felt good going in. Aftertaste came after.

Eat one ordinary meal now and stay to the end of it without moving.

Cook or buy what you like. Sit. Eat while it is hot. Notice what your mouth already knows how to do. Teeth, tongue, saliva. Heat, salt, fat, sweet. Chew slows near the end. The last mouthfuls taste strongest because hunger has quieted enough to let flavour speak. Put the fork down. The plate is warm under your hand. The taste lingers on the tongue for minutes, clean and full, changing as you breathe through the nose.

“Food won’t finish without it.”

That is the voice. So ask it, with the plate still warm:

Did the food taste of tobacco while you chewed, or did tobacco arrive after and cover what you tasted?
Did the lingering taste need burning to stay, or did burning drive it out?
When you lit at the table, did the meal feel closed or did the mouth feel spoiled for the next hour?

You know because your tongue knows. Smoke does not seal flavour. It coats it. Smokers finish and light and within two drags the mouth is dry ash and the nose is dull, and they call that rounding off. A non-smoker finishes and sits and tastes food for ten minutes after eating, and calls nothing anything. He simply tastes.

I tested this on a meal I loved. Soup, bread, cheese, apple. One night with the dose at the end, one night without. With the dose, the apple after the cheese was smoke and hurry and standing. Without, the apple was cold juice and crunch and the cheese still on the tongue underneath. Same apple. Same mouth. One night stolen, one night owned. The work of the cook, the work of my own day that paid for it — all of it was there both nights. Only one night let me stay for it.

We feared meals would turn flat. It never fixed the itch. It caused it. The flatness we blamed on missing was the coating we called finishing.

Leave the plate where it is. Drink water. Breathe. Talk. The taste thins by itself into satisfaction, the way a note thins after it is struck. No full stop of ash is needed. The meal closed itself when you put the fork down.

### AIR ON THE FACE AT TEN AND THREE

The break is the last place we trust.

Mid-morning the screen blurs. Mid-afternoon the shoulders ache. The huddle moves to the door. Lighter clicks. We went because we needed air, and then we filled the air with smoke and called it a break.

Take the break apart and live it clean.

Push back the chair. Stand. Walk to the door. Cold or warm, the outside touches the face at once. Eyes lift from close work to distance — roof, sky, street. Shoulders roll. Jaw unclenches. You breathe without thinking about breathing, in through the nose, out through the mouth, the chest rising properly for the first time in two hours. Blood moves in the legs. Hands hold a mug and warm themselves. Talk, if you want talk. Quiet, if you want quiet. Ten minutes of not working.

“I need to step out for one or it isn’t a break.”

Watch that thought with the door open:

Did the air enter your lungs, or did the smoke?
Did your legs need walking, or lighting?
When you climbed those stairs with a dose halfway up and arrived puffed, did the dose give you breath or borrow it?

You know the stairs breath as well as I do. We coughed up them, lit at the top to reward the climb, then coughed again. A non-smoker climbs them breathing easy and stands at the top with clear eyes. His break starts at the top. Ours never started; we only rented quiet hands for minutes while the tug paused.

Stay out the full ten. Notice how the tug behaves when you do not answer it with a story. It stirs around the old hour. Hollow. Fidgety. Hands wanting an errand. It does not grow teeth. It does not stop you talking or laughing or watching clouds. It tints the minutes grey for a little while, then thins when you keep your eyes on the street and your hands on the mug. The grey was never the morning or the work or your nature. It was the interval asking for a coin.

I stood those breaks for years believing empty hands meant missing something. Empty hands hold the mug. Empty hands button the coat. Empty hands go into pockets for warmth and come out for nothing. Nothing missing. The chat holds the warmth. The pause holds the rest. The dose only held the interruptions — leave to light, leave to finish, leave to light again.

A break is a pause from work. Smoke never paused anything. It paused the pause.

### YOUR BODY ALREADY KNOWS

This is the part we have been trained not to believe, so look at it with your own senses as witness.

Your nose knows smoke from food without being told. Your tongue knows clean from coated without lessons. Your lungs know deep from shallow without practice. You did not teach a baby to breathe. You did not teach yourself to taste soup. The body arrived with its instruments tuned.

We jammed the signal and then credited the jam with music. Morning cough we called clearing. Meal coating we called closing. Shallow breath at the door we called rest. Each time the body protested cleanly — cough, dull nose, tight chest — and the big story above translated protest as proof of pleasure. Below only a small tug complained for its feed. Above a lifetime of pictures said the complaint meant you cannot wake, taste, or pause without help.

You can. You have, in every gap you were forced to sit through. Long meeting. Long film. Long flight. Mouth went on tasting after. Lungs went on breathing after. Sleep came without a dose in the mouth. Waking came without a dose in the hand until the hand taught it otherwise. The instruments never left. They were only covered.

So do not learn freedom as a skill. Do not rehearse it. Live the next ordinary round as it comes — kettle, plate, doorway — with nothing added and nothing avoided. Let the tongue linger. Let the chest fill. Let the hands stay free. When the old hour speaks, hear it for what it is and keep drinking your tea, keep talking, keep walking. The tug fades when unfed on its short clock. The body stays.

You do not need a prop to wake, to finish, to rest. You need eyes that no longer credit the thief with the house he robbed.

I-08 — TRUST YOUR BODY TO BREATHE AND TASTE
Your body knows freedom without being taught.

SUMMARY
- Morning woke me through cold, light, water and heat, not through a sting on empty lungs.
- Food finished itself and lingered clean, and smoke only coated what the mouth had already tasted.
- A pause rested me through air, distance and movement, and empty hands missed nothing.
- The stir at the old hour was small and passing, not a picture of what I am.
- Nose, tongue and lungs knew their work without lessons once they were left uncovered.
