## The Stairs Don't Lie

I remember the stairs that started it for me. Not a doctor's warning. Not a photograph on a pack. Just an ordinary staircase in an ordinary office building, three flights up to a meeting I was already late for.

I took them two at a time because I wanted to look sharp. By the second landing my chest was pulling tight. By the third I had to stop and pretend to check my phone while I got my breath back. A colleague my age, a man who had never smoked, came up behind me talking and laughing, able to breathe easy all the way to the top, and he did not even notice the climb.

We all know that moment. We light a cigarette to get through stress, to take a break, to finish a meal, to enjoy a night out, and then a small ordinary thing — stairs, a run for a bus, a laugh that turns into a cough in the morning — shows us the body keeping score. No lecture was needed. My body was telling me plainly that something had taken hold of me.

I am not telling you this to frighten you. I am telling you because I stood exactly where you stand now. For years I was the adult daily smoker who could not imagine a day without cigarettes and who lit the next one within the hour of saying I should stop. I told myself I enjoyed it. I told myself I needed it to cope. And privately I was tired of being out of breath, tired of the smell on my coat, tired of planning my life around the next light.

If you feel braced for another lecture, I understand. You have earned the right to be wary. You have tried patches and promises. You have tried cutting down and white-knuckling through evenings. You have woken up resolved and gone to bed disappointed. Of course you want to stop — that is why you picked up this book. I don't blame you for smoking, and I will not waste your time telling you that you are weak.

## I Was the Last Person to Believe This

I smoked heavily for over twenty years. I am not proud of the number, and I will not parade it, except to say this: I was not a casual smoker. I was the one who smoked first thing before my feet had properly found the floor, the one who smoked when I was ill, the one who smoked when I swore I would not.

I tried to stop in every way you have tried. I threw packs away at midnight and bought another by ten. I made rules about only smoking after meals, only smoking outside, only smoking five a day. Every rule ended the same way. I concluded what you may have concluded: that I lacked something other people had.

That conclusion was wrong. When I finally stopped, I stopped overnight, without a fight, without bad temper, without sitting on my hands. What happened that night felt less like a battle won and more like a quiet escape back to myself. I had expected misery. What I felt instead was clarity, as if a noise I had lived with for years had suddenly switched off.

I know this is hard to accept. "This sounds too good to be true." That was my reaction too when I first heard that stopping could be easy. I thought easy was a word other people used. I thought freedom of that kind was not for the likes of me.

Since then I have watched the same change happen for thousands of smokers, including hardened cases who had smoked longer than I had and who arrived certain they would fail again. They did not succeed because they were stronger. They succeeded because they finally saw clearly. The fact is, difficulty never lived in the cigarette. It lived in what we believed the cigarette was doing for us.

You can stop smoking easily, immediately and permanently. I state that with total confidence, not as a slogan but as what I have seen again and again. Easily, because there is no suffering to endure once the belief changes. Immediately, because you do not need to wait months to feel the benefit. Permanently, because once you see, you cannot unsee.

Do you want to stop? Of course you do — that is why you are reading this book. And do you believe right now that stopping will be hard and gloomy? Of course you do — that is what every failed attempt has taught you to expect. Both feelings can live in you at once. This book will resolve them.

## Why This Time Is Different

Let me speak plainly about why nothing worked before.

We fell into smoking as teenagers or young adults because of phoney information. We saw adults smoking to look grown up, to belong, to handle pressure. We tried one, coughed, felt dizzy, and persisted until we learned to inhale. Did we sit down and decide we needed cigarettes to enjoy a meal or to survive stress? We never thought it over when we found it. We copied what we saw, and then the habit closed around us.

Once we were inside, we gave the cigarette credit for things it never did. The coffee break felt good, so we thanked the cigarette. Work finished, so we paid ourselves with a light. A quarrel left us shaking, we smoked, the shaking eased a little, and we said the cigarette calmed us. Picture yourself closing this book happy to be free from the first morning without a cigarette, and ask whether that picture frightens you or warms you. If it warms you, you already know the truth: you do not love smoking, you love the idea of being clear of it.

I will not ask you to take my word for any of this. I will ask you to investigate with me. We will look at stress, at pleasure, at taste, at habit, at the social cigarette with drinks among friends. We will isolate each one and ask what the cigarette actually added. You will supply the evidence from your own life. You will reach your own conclusion.

Is it true that you cannot cope without cigarettes? Look at all the hours you cope without them — sleeping, flying, working where you cannot smoke — and you do cope. Is it true that cigarettes give you pleasure? Then why do you hate being a smoker even while you say you love lighting up? Why do you hide it, excuse it, wish you had never started?

You do not need courage to read. You need curiosity to read. Past failures did not prove you are weak. They proved the method was wrong.

This is not the willpower method wearing a new coat. Willpower tells you to fight desire while still believing you are losing something precious. That fight creates misery, and misery sends you back. This time is different. We remove the desire first, so there is nothing to fight.

## What I Ask Before We Begin

There is one thing I need from you at the start, and it is small.

"This will not work for me. I have tried everything." I hear that voice in almost every smoker, and I heard it in myself. It is a fair doubt. You have tried everything except seeing the thing clearly. Will you allow that there might be something you have not yet seen? Will you read as an investigator rather than as a defendant?

If you read with an open mind and I am wrong, what have you lost? An hour or two with a book, and you will smoke as before. If you read with an open mind and I am right, what will you have won? Everything you have wanted for years — health, money, time, self-respect — without paying for it with misery. On those terms, you have nothing to lose and everything to gain by giving this a fair hearing. You have nothing to lose and everything to gain, and that is why I ask for an open mind and nothing more.

I do not ask you to believe me yet. I do not ask you to change your smoking yet. I do not ask you to be brave or strong or cheerful. I ask only that you keep reading, follow the argument step by step, and let each point finish before you judge it. Doubt is welcome here. What closes the mind is deciding in advance.

All past attempts belong to the wrong method, not to you. You were never shown the full picture. This time you will be shown it.

This time is different.
