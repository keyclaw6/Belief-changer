Chapter 12
LIGHT IT, SEE IT, LEAVE IT

IN THIS CHAPTER
The gate question, the ordinary table, the stain and ash, the cooling tray, the two doors, the bright reply.

*Smoke this last one with open eyes and the door shuts behind it.*

### HAVE YOU SEEN ENOUGH?

You have watched the favourite fail on its own ground. You have watched fewer turn each kept dose huge and let the number creep back through broken rules and clock-watching. You have watched one after a long clear spell wake the old hollow and rebuild the daily timetable. You have watched tomorrow slide from date to date while the cost was paid daily.

What is left to prove?

You have looked at the nicotine trap from both sides now. We built it together, dose by dose, year by year, telling ourselves it steadied hands and filled pauses and closed meals. We saw, patio by patio and desk by desk, that talk carried the night and work carried the day and paper only stood beside them and took the bow. That seeing is yours. No one lent it to you. You did the looking.

"I want to be sure I am ready."

I hear you. We all ask it at the gate. Let me answer plain as one who stood there with a full pack and a hollow chest.

Did fewer ever loosen the timetable, or did it only tighten it?
Did one ever stay one, or did it always call for the next?
If delay ever gave you a calmer day to start, why did every delayed start begin with cough and ash?

You know the answers because your pocket and your diary and your mornings know them. Fewer gilded. One rebuilt. Tomorrow slid. There is no smaller cage that opens and no later date that softens. There is only inside and out, and you have already seen which side holds nothing.

Are you champing at the bit to finish this? You should be. A man who has seen an empty box does not bargain for a better day to put it down. He puts it down.

If you are not — if some small voice still says keep a corner, keep a date, keep a drawer dose just in case — do not push yourself with jaw and fists. Put this chapter down. Read again the diary that crept and the lamp that rebuilt and the calendar that slid. Look again at your own evenings where company did all and smoke added none. Come back when the seeing is whole. Leave it unfed and the Nipper starves. All you have to do is follow all the instructions.

But if your stomach turns at the thought of another year of yellow fingers and hourly tapping, if you look at the pack now and feel no tenderness, only tired knowledge — then stay with me at the table. The hour has come.

### THE LAST ORDINARY DOSE

Bring nothing special to this. No fresh pack bought for the occasion. No last supper brand. No extra smoked to make it grand. Take the ordinary dose you have left, at an ordinary table, in ordinary light, and look at it for once the way it is.

I remember my own table. Chipped mug. Tray with grey dust. Evening coming in at the window. Hands doing the old work without asking. For years I had lit without looking. This time I looked.

Light it and watch.

Watch the paper brown at the tip and the ash grow long and pale. Watch your fingers take on the smell within two draws. Taste the first draw — hot, dry, faintly sweet — and then the second, already bitter at the back of the tongue. Notice how quickly the taste turns from fire to ash-tray. Hold the filter afterward and look at the brown stain ringing the end. Press the butt and see the yellow bleed. Smell the end you stubbed. That sour, stale, shut-room smell is not an accident of this one. It is every one.

We told ourselves for years that lighting was heaven. I said it. You said it. The match flares and for three seconds something lifts — the shoulders drop, the noise dips. Then what? The problem on the desk sits untouched. The people on the patio talk on without us while we step to the wind end to manage ash. The mouth fills with dry heat. The chest takes a shallow sip and asks for shallower next time. By halfway the hand wants it done with, and still the hand lifts it again.

Ask it while it burns, with no mercy and no poetry:

What did this draw add to talk, to taste, to company?
Whose hunger did it quiet except the hunger it lit an hour ago?
If this is heaven, why does heaven need paying for again within the hour, and why does it leave ash on the tongue that water does not lift?

It gave you nothing. It took everything.

That is not a slogan. It is the plain reading of this table. The stain, the ash, the stale end — your eyes have it. The shoulders dropped for seconds because the hourly call paused for seconds, and the pause will return louder in an hour unless the calls stop for good. You do not need my word for it. Your mouth tells you. Your fingers tell you. The tray tells you.

Smoke it to the end, slowly, with full attention, not as a treat to remember but as evidence to finish. Do not hurry it and do not nurse it. Let it be dull. Let it be ugly. When it shortens to heat on the fingers, stub it hard and look at what is left: grey dust, a stained tube, a smell you wash off clothes and hair and curtains. That is the thing we once called friend.

### WHAT THE TUG WILL DO NEXT

"What will I feel in the morning without it?"

We all ask it, and the trap loves the question because it can dress a small tug in giant clothes.

Here is the fact, stated flat the way your body will state it. When no dose comes, a small hollow asks to be fed — an empty, slightly restless, slightly edgy little tug. For most, it taps most loudly in the first few days and then eases within a few weeks, beginning within hours for those who dosed hourly. Not a sentence on you, for bodies keep their own timing.

Read that straight. It is a tug, not a storm. It does not prove you need smoke to be yourself. It proves a tiny creature is unfed and complaining. We fed it every forty minutes for years, so of course it pipes up when the timetable stops. It piped before every dose you ever smoked. You called that pipe strain, boredom, hunger, need for a pause. It was only the timetable knocking.

And note the shape of it. The tug comes as thought more than pain — hands looking for work, eyes sliding to the clock, a picture of yourself on a step with something between fingers. That picture is not command from your blood. It is memory wearing your voice. When you see the uniform you do not obey it. You name it for what it is: the old call asking for the old timetable.

Will it last forever? Ask your own history of gaps. Long flights. Long meetings. Nights of sleep. Days of flu when you took nothing and lived. Did the call climb without end, or did it rise and fall and pass while you were busy elsewhere? You know. It rose and it passed. It always passes when unfed, because there is nothing behind it but a fading echo.

Do not measure your days by it. Do not count hours to prove you are strong. Counting keeps the timetable alive under another name. Let the tug knock and walk past the door. Drink water. Move. Keep hands in ordinary work. The knock grows fainter when no one answers, and one morning you notice the morning asked nothing at all.

It is small. It is brief. It is already dying.

### WHY THIS ONE STAYS LAST

"You have shown me one rebuilds. But could I not keep the memory of this last one warm without touching another?"

No. And that no is kindness, not harshness.

I know I can never have just one. I learned that the hard way, thinking after 5 months I could control myself and just have one. Nope.

I did not write that line. A man with five clear months wrote it with his own lungs. Clear mornings, pocket light, hourly call gone quiet — then a night, a grin, a tipped pack, and the hand went out to prove command. By nightfall the bargaining was back. By the week the diary was back. Not because five months meant nothing, but because one told the mind the bottle still held something worth reaching for.

That is the whole law of the last dose in one mouthful. One does not test whether the trap is gone. One rebuilds it. The first draw wakes the hollow and gives it a voice again, and the voice asks in your accent, so you mistake it for yourself.

Do you need another test of that law on your own skin? Did your own past one stay one? Did the Christmas dose stay at Christmas? Did the hard-week dose close the week, or open the timetable again? You know. The test creates the very hunger it claims to measure.

So this stubbed end is not one among many. It is the line. The drawer holds no spare. The bag holds no emergency. The date holds no holiday exception. A kept dose is a kept thought, dusted and waiting, tapping the ribs at tired hours. Let the drawer hold nothing that taps.

If in past years you took such a puff after a clear spell, read it only as a warning light. It does not prove you are broken and it does not doom what comes next. It proves one cord was left plugged in. Pull that last cord now and never reach to check whether the figure is gone. You do not prove a shut box by opening it.

### THE VOW

Look at the tray. Grey dust. Stained tube. Sour air.

You have seen what the dose adds to talk and taste and company — nothing that night and leisure and people have not already supplied. You have seen what fewer does and what one does and what tomorrow does. You have seen what the tug is and how briefly it knocks when unfed. Remember, you have nothing to lose and everything to gain.

There is no better dose waiting behind this one. There is no milder paper, no truer blend, no special night that turns ash to honey. There are only more trays like this one, more mornings with ash on the tongue, more missed lines while you step to the wind end — or none. You walk out easily, immediately and permanently.

Do you see that this last dull burn is the whole story told straight? Do you see that keeping none is not harshness but accuracy — the plain reading of your own book? Then there is only one act that fits what you now know. Take it with solemn joy, not with gloom. Mark it. Own it. Shut it.

Close the trap with joy, not sadness.

### THE TRAY COOLS, YOU STAND

It is done. Let the pack shut. Let the tray cool.

Stay a minute at the table. Do not rush off to prove anything. The solemn part matters as much as the joyful part. You have closed something that took years of your life and many thousands of your coins and much of your breath. Mark it the way you would mark any true parting — quietly, fully, with eyes open. No doom here. No wobble. A steady hand on a finished thing.

Pick up the pack. Feel the flat weight of it, the cellophane already loose, the cardboard soft at the corners from living in pocket and bag. It ruled hours. It chose doorways. It cut talk in half. Now it is paper and leaf and glue. Close the lid. Push it away. Hear the small scrape on the wood. That scrape is the sound of a door shutting that you will never need to open again.

Look at your fingers. The smell is still there from the last two draws. Wash them. Use hot water and soap and watch the smell thin and go. Look at the yellow at the nail edge that will fade in days. That fading is your body doing on its own what no timetable taught it. You do not have to command it. You only have to stop interrupting it.

Breathe where you sit. Take a slower pull through the nose and feel the cool line of evening air come in without heat on top of it. Notice the chest take it without the shallow sip and the cough to clear. Nothing grand. Nothing forced. Just air moving where smoke used to push through. Your shoulders sit lower now than they did between doses, because there is no next gap to watch for. The minute hand on the wall moves and you do not follow it. That is what resolve feels like tonight — not a clenched jaw, but an unwatched clock.

This is solemn joy, and you must feel the difference from gloom. Gloom would say: I have lost my prop, my pause, my friend on hard nights. Solemn joy says: I have finished examining the thing and found it empty, and I am laying it down with full knowledge. Gloom looks back at the tray and mourns. Solemn joy looks at the tray and tells the truth — grey dust, stained tube, sour air — and feels the shoulders lighten because mourning would be a lie. You do not mourn a timetable. You leave it.

Are you champing at the bit? You should be. Not with nerves. With the fidget of a man kept too long in a waiting room who has just been called and finds the corridor leads straight outside. The legs want to move. The hands want ordinary work. The mouth wants clean water and food that tastes of itself. Let that eagerness rise. Do not damp it with talk of taking care or being careful. Eagerness is not danger. Eagerness is the proof the seeing is whole.

I stood where you stand. I pushed the pack and washed the fingers and sat with the cooling tray and felt almost foolish at how ordinary it was. No thunder. No tearing.

Did nothing happen, then, because no thunder came? That doubt is the last whisper of the timetable. We expect a chained door to clang to prove we have left the cell. When the lid only clicks shut under our own hand, we stand in the passage doubting the leaving.

Read the quiet the other way around. If the dose had held honey, parting would tear. Hands would shake. The chest would hollow for a lost friend. No tearing came tonight because no friend left this table. Grey dust left. A stained tube left. Sour air left. The quiet is not absence of change. The quiet is the receipt. It tells you, in the body before big words arrive, that there was nothing to keep and therefore no wound in laying it down.

Must you wait days to know the name holds? Ask what waiting would add. Would three mornings of unwatched clocks make the tray less grey? Would a week of washed fingers make the stained end less sour? The tray will not grow uglier with time, and it will not grow dearer. Your eyes have already finished the weighing that days cannot improve. To wait for a feeling to confer what seeing has already conferred is to put the clock back on the wall after you took it down.

So take the name tonight, in this ordinary light, without asking feeling for permission. Gloom would stand here and say: I feel flat, so nothing changed, I must serve days to earn it. Solemn joy stands here and says: I feel ordinary because the thing was empty, and ordinary is exactly how laying down an empty thing feels. One mourns. The other tells the truth and lets shoulders lower.

That lowering you felt when the minute hand moved and you did not follow it — that is not mood. That is timetable gone. No next gap to watch for. No doorway to choose. No pocket weight to check. The evening goes on with phone and talk and sleep ahead, held without the hourly tap on the ribs. You do not need thunder to prove the tap stopped. You need only this unwatched minute, lived through, to know.

I stood where you stand and felt that same foolish ordinary minute, and I tell you it was the proof, not the lack of it. No thunder means no loss. No tearing means no friend. That wish to get up and get on with the evening — to answer the phone without stepping out, to finish the talk without watching the door, to sleep without setting the bedside dose for morning — that wish is not small. That wish is the whole change arriving in the body before the mind has found big words for it.

So I congratulate you now, at once, without waiting for days to prove it. Well done. You saw what few are shown so plainly — the stain and the ash and the stale end as one story — and you acted on what you saw. All you have to do is follow all the instructions.

Hold that standing minute. Pack shut. Tray cool. Hands clean. Chest taking air. Eyes on the room as it is. From this stubbed end, you are a non-smoker. Do not wait to become one. Do not serve days to earn it. The change is now, in belief and in name, because there is nothing left to keep.

When you rise from the table tonight, rise as one who has already left. Carry nothing out to the bin with ceremony and carry nothing back in reserve. Leave the tray for the morning to empty without tenderness. Put the washed glass down. Turn the light off on the table as you would turn it off on any finished work. The night outside the window is still yours — talk, food, rest, work tomorrow — held now without the hourly tap on the ribs. That holding without tapping is the first hour of the rest of your life lived without the timetable.

### WHAT TO THINK WHEN THE THOUGHT COMES

A thought will come. Not today with the book warm in your hands, but later, at a tired hour, in your own accent.

"You have had a hard day. Take one to close it."

"You are out with old friends. Just this once to belong."

"You have proved the point. One now cannot hurt."

Know those lines now as old script, not fresh thought. The trap speaks in your voice because it lived in your mouth for years. When the whisper comes you will not need to debate it. You will know the uniform too well to follow it.

There are two doors and both are painted kind. The first is the bad-day door: strain on the shoulders, phone down, hand already reaching while papers sit untouched. It promises to take care of it. It will take care of nothing but its own tug for minutes, and leave the papers and the strain exactly where they were, plus ash. The second is the proving door: one to show command, one to test whether the figure is gone. Testing opens the box it claims to check.

Shut both with one thought, kept for life. Do not argue. Do not weigh. Do not picture the draw. When any picture of smoke crosses your mind — patio, desk, morning step — answer it at once with the same bright reply, spoken inward as lived fact:

YIPPEE! I'M FREE!

Say it as joy, not as effort. Mean it as pity for the old timetable, not envy of those still in it. I have stood on cold steps hearing that whisper and I tell you plain: each promised a taste and delivered a timetable. Seeing the livery is enough. You do not follow a con once you know the hand.

That bright reply is yours to keep. It does not need reasons attached each time because the reasons are already finished at this table. The stain told them. The ash told them. The stale end told them. When the whisper comes you will not reopen the book and re-weigh. You will answer with what you settled at this table and keep to the talk, the work, the walk you were in.

Well done. I knew you would see it. Hold the seeing and totality holds itself. You keep no dose because you keep no doubt that a dose gives — not because you were told to keep none, but because your own eyes at this table showed you.

SUMMARY
- The last ordinary dose showed stain, ash and a stale end, and added nothing to talk, taste or company.
- The hollow after is a small tug that taps loudest early and then eases when left unfed.
- One after a clear spell wakes the old timetable and never stays one, so none are kept for any occasion or date.
- A past slip warned where belief still had a gap and gave no licence for another on purpose.
- From the stubbed end the reader stands as a non-smoker, with a bright reply ready for any old whisper.
