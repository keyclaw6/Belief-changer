### The Hand That Moves First

Keep the clear watching you brought to the plate and the step, because I want you to watch it again where it feels most true.

Late afternoon. The kitchen or the small office. You have just had the row — with your partner, your boss, your child, the voice on the phone. The phone is face down now. Your shoulders are up near your ears. Your jaw aches. On the desk the papers sit spread and waiting, the bill, the letter you must answer, the thing you said badly and cannot unsay.

Your hand is already moving.

It finds the pack before thought finishes. It finds the lighter. Feet shift toward the door. You strike and draw, and for a minute the shoulders drop a fraction. The noise in your head softens. You call it taking care of it.

I know that minute from the inside. I am not mocking it. If you feel ill or worried, speak to your own clinician for your own care. I say that once so you can look without dread. The softening you felt was real as a feeling. I only want you to place it where it belongs.

Say your own sentence out loud, in your own dialect, as you stood there:

"If anything stressful happens in my life, a cigarette takes care of it."

I said that sentence myself for years. I believed it more than any other sentence about smoking. Breaks and meals and nights out were pleasures I liked. This felt like need. This felt like proof the dose did something no pause or laugh could do.

Watch the order again, because the order gives it away.

Tug first. Hand second. Story third.

Long before the row, since your last dose, a thin edginess had been building between doses. You did not name it. You called it the day. Then the row landed on top of it. Two pressures at once — the hot sting of the argument, and the faint gnaw of the interval. You lit. The gnaw quieted for minutes as the new dose arrived. The quiet arrived at the same moment as the row, and the mind did the old sum at once. The dose calmed me.

Did it?

### What Did It Take Care Of

Leave the doorway and go back to the desk, because the desk tells the truth.

Put the papers in a row in memory. The letter still unanswered. The money still owed. The shift still to face. The harsh words still said. You stepped out, you drew, you stared at nothing for five minutes, you came back in. Which sheet moved while you were gone?

Ask yourself plainly, with the ash still warm:

When the end burned down, was the argument mended?
When you came back inside, was the problem smaller?
When you sat down again, did you know more clearly what to say?

You know the honest answers because your own desk gave them. No. No. No.

The problem sat exactly where you left it. It did not shrink. It did not soften. It did not answer itself. It waited, untouched, while you stood elsewhere and breathed smoke.

I smoked through deadlines and debts and sick nights and rows that shook the house. I can tell you what a dose does to a problem. Nothing. It does not pay the bill. It does not unsay the sentence. It does not make the boss kind or the child well or the phone less heavy. It leaves every paper where it found it.

"But you do not know my nerves," you say. "You do not know my job. Anyone would need something with pressure like mine."

I know those nerves because they were mine. We all lived there. We were not weak to feel them. We were conned on phoney information before we could judge, and then every hard minute was paired with a light until pairing looked like proof.

Think of a non-smoker in the same row. Phone down. Shoulders tight. Papers waiting. He does not light. Does his problem stay forever unsolved because he has no tube? Does he sit frozen until someone hands him a dose? He paces, he drinks water, he stares out, he answers badly or well — but his hands do not need paper and fire to begin. His stress does not prove a tube takes care of stress. It proves stress passes and work begins with or without smoke.

So what softened for you in the doorway?

Not the row. The interval.

### The Ten-Second Quiet

The fact is simple, and it holds across every desk and every doorway.

When smoke enters the lungs, the nicotine reaches the brain within seconds. The lift peaks fast. Then it fades fast, with the brief sense of reward fading with it. That rapid rise and rapid fade is what keeps the smoker dosing again to keep the feeling topped up and to hold off the empty feeling between.

I put that plainly, not to frighten you, and not to ask you to change from fright. I put it so you can see the timing that fooled us.

You light after the row. Within seconds the new dose arrives. The thin edginess that had built since the last dose quiets. For minutes attention steadies and temper feels less sharp. You call that calm.

It is not calm above normal. It is the muting of a lack the previous dose left behind, placed next to real trouble until trouble seemed to need it.

Picture it as it happened at your desk.

Morning dose fades. Faint gnaw builds so low you call it hunger or boredom or need for air. Midday dose quiets it. Midday fades. Gnaw builds again. Row lands. Now you have two storms — the row shouting, the gnaw whispering underneath. Evening dose quiets the whisper for minutes. The shouting is still there, but for minutes the total noise is lower because one layer went quiet. You thank the tube for handling the row. The tube never touched the row. It only turned down for minutes its own whisper underneath.

It never lifted you above where you stood before you started. It let you up for air and called the air a gift.

That is why stress smokers smoke more when life gets harder, and feel harder done by the more they smoke. Not because life needs more smoke. Because shorter intervals mean more whispers to quiet, and more quiets mistaken for help. The harder the week, the more doses, the more fades, the more edginess to mistake for life. The calm creates the storm it pretends to calm.

Once you see the two layers, you cannot credit them the same way.

You were you steadying yourself, not the tube steadying you. You paused. You breathed. You stepped away. You let seconds pass. Those steadied you, as they steady any person. The tube rode along and took the bow, while charging stale mouth and divided minutes and another fade an hour later as the price.

It did not take care of it. It took from you while it left it.

### The Papers Still There

Stay with the papers a moment longer, because this is where the keystone comes out.

We told ourselves the dose helped us think and cope. Watch what coping actually needed at that desk.

Coping needed reading the letter. Coping needed answering one line. Coping needed saying sorry or saying no. Coping needed hands free and head clear and mouth clean to speak. The dose gave none of those. It gave seconds of draw, then smoke in the eyes, then standing apart from the desk where the work was.

If the dose helped thought, why did thought return thinnest just before lighting and clearest for minutes after, in exact time with the interval? If the dose helped temper, why did temper shorten as the gap lengthened, row or no row? If it was about the row, why did a quiet day with long gaps still end with the same hand moving first?

Because the help was timing, not care.

The Smokescreen lends you my voice in your head, so the translation sounds like you. My nerves. My crutch. My off-switch. Said thousands of times, while the hand moved first and the whisper quieted for minutes, it began to sound like truth. It was a script laid over timing.

And the nicotine trap needs both keepers or it cannot hold you. The tiny pull in the body, the Nipper calling with faint emptiness. The big story in thought, translating every whisper as need for a fix. Tug first. Hand second. Story third. At the stress desk you watched all three in daylight.

You do not need to force a bright mood to see this. You only need to let the mood match what you know. A person who finds the papers still there does not thank the doorway. He puts the credit where it belongs — to his own pause, his own legs, his own breath of cold air — and sees the tube for what it did. Plenty to your mouth and breath and minutes. Nothing for the letter and the row.

With that seen, reading the rest becomes simple. You cannot trust the stressed feeling to judge the stressed feeling. In the doorway the whisper will always vote for a dose, because the whisper is the dose fading. Judgment cannot be left to the minute when judgment is thinnest. It must be left to clear seeing done now, while the papers sit still and tell the truth.

That is why this part asks only one thing of you. Not effort. Not struggle. Not testing yourself at the next row. Only that you let clear seeing carry you, and do exactly what this book asks while we finish the work together. All you have to do is follow all the instructions.

SUMMARY
- The hand often moved toward the pack before any thought about stress formed.
- The sentence about stress taking care of itself described a feeling, not what happened on the desk.
- The papers and the row sat untouched while the doorway minutes passed.
- The fast arrival and fast fade of each dose explains why quiet comes and why it never lasts.
- What quieted for minutes was the edginess left by the previous dose, not the problem that caused the stress.
- Pausing, stepping away and breathing steadied you, and the dose stood beside those acts and took the credit.
