Chapter 3
WHAT DOES IT ACTUALLY DO FOR YOU

**IN THIS CHAPTER** — The kitchen scale, the plate still warm, the step outside in cold air, the crowded table, the hug that won't let go

*I am not asking what smoking costs you. I am asking what it ever did for you.*

### The Scale On The Kitchen Table

Keep the clear watching you brought from the garage and the sofa, because I want to put something else on the table with you.

Up to now we have weighed harm against pleasure. We have all done it. On one side the cough in the morning, the money gone, the smell on the coat, the hiding, the stairs that leave us short of breath. On the other side what we call our reasons. And because the harm side looks heavy, we tell ourselves the pleasure side must be heavy too, or we would not keep paying.

I want to lift that scale off the table entirely.

Not because harm does not matter. It matters. If you feel ill or worried, speak to your own clinician for your own care. I say that plainly so you can look steadily without anxiety. I only want you to see that harm was never the right question. A con does not fail because it costs too much. It fails because it never gave what it promised.

So let us do what we never did while we smoked without thinking. Let us say our reasons out loud, in our own words, and look at them one by one.

I hear you because I said them all myself.

"It calms me down."

"It helps me think."

"It's my reward."

"I love the taste."

"It makes a break a real break."

"It makes food finish properly."

"It's sociable. It goes with company."

We say them quickly, as if reciting a list we learned long ago. Notice how learned it sounds. Did you arrive at those reasons by testing? Did you sit down before your first dose and decide you needed help with thinking and breaks and meals and friendship? We never thought it over when we found it. We copied what we saw, we coughed through the first tries, and then we learned the sentences that made the coughing seem sensible.

That is the large story at work. The Smokescreen takes an empty, slightly restless, slightly edgy little tug between doses and renames it as need and liking and choice. It lends you my voice in your head, so the translation sounds like you.

You already saw the order in your own day. Tug first. Hand second. Story third. The Nipper calls with that faint emptiness. The hand moves to the pack. The mind follows half a second later with, my nerves, my reward, my moment. The nicotine trap needs both keepers, the tiny pull in the body and the big story in thought, or it cannot hold you.

Now ask the question we skipped for years. Not, is the cost too high. But, where was the gift at all?

Would a gift need to be hidden with spray? Would a gift move your hand before you decided? Would a gift leave you sitting divided on your own sofa, half with people you love and half on guard in another room? Choice does not creep and hide. A gift does not need mints.

You do not need to answer yet. Only notice how strange our ledger looks once we stop weighing and start asking.

### What Did It Add To The Plate

Let us stay with the plate, because food is where many of us swear the dose does its best work.

The meal is done. The plate is still warm. The kitchen smells of oil and salt and what you cooked. Your mouth is full of that taste, alive and complete. You push back the chair a little. You feel full and a little drowsy in a good way. And the thought arrives on cue, in your own voice. Now a cigarette to finish it.

So you step out or go to the door and light. Strike, first draw, that brief flare at the tip.

I am not denying that flare. "The moment I light up I'm in heaven, but feel shitty afterwards. So whilst I love smoking I hate being a smoker." I know that sentence from the inside. I felt the seconds where lighting feels like heaven. I am not mocking it. I only ask you to place it where it belongs.

Where did heaven sit? At the tip, for seconds, while you drew. Not across the meal. Not across the evening. Seconds at lighting, then the stale mouth, then the faint heavy feeling and the thought you know too well, loving it and hating being a smoker in the same breath.

Now ask, with the plate still warm in memory.

Did the dose add flavor to the food, or did it replace flavor with smoke? While you drew, what could you taste — the meal lingering, or hot paper and ash? Did the meal become more complete, or did you leave the table where the pleasure was, to stand somewhere colder where the pleasure was not?

And a minute after, when the flare passed, what was left in your mouth? The food you loved, or the stale end that covered it? If the dose finished the meal, why did your mouth feel less clean than before you lit? If it was the finishing touch, why did you need mints to undo the finish?

We tell ourselves we smoke to enjoy food more. Watch what actually happened. The food gave. The dose interrupted. The tongue that could taste went numb for minutes. The nose that could smell filled with smoke. The company at the table went on without you while you stood apart.

I cooked and ate as a smoker for years. I believed the dose closed the meal. Only when I watched did I see the order. Hunger satisfied. Taste bright. Body settling. Then the tug arriving between doses, whisper thin, and the Smokescreen translating it at once as, the meal needs something. The meal needed nothing. The tug needed quiet, for minutes, and took the taste with it as the price.

Was that doing something for the plate? Or to the plate?

Ask yourself honestly. When was the food best — while you ate, or while you smoked? You know the answer because your own mouth told you. The best bite never had smoke in it.

### The Step Outside And The Crowded Table

Take the same question to the pause and to company, because those two feel like proof.

Mid-morning you down tools. Shoulders tight. Eyes tired. You step outside into cold air. The street sounds soften. Your legs stretch. Your chest lifts for a breath. You light. For a minute the rush stops. You call it the break working.

Or the crowded table. Laughter, plates, glasses, talk overlapping. Someone lights. Two others light. The air thickens. You light with them. You feel part of it. You call it belonging.

I do not doubt the good in those moments. The rest from work is real. The warmth of people is real. I only ask who deserves the credit.

On the step, what rested you? The dose, or stopping work, straightening your back, feeling cold air on your face, being alone for five minutes without demands? If you had stood there without lighting, would your shoulders still have dropped? Would your eyes still have rested? Would the sky still have been there?

We gave the credit to the tube because the tube was there. We always paired stopping with lighting, until we could no longer picture stopping without lighting. But the stopping was the rest. The stretching was the rest. The quiet was the rest. The dose only quieted the tug that had been building since the last dose, while the step itself did the restoring.

At the table, what warmed you? The paper burning, or the laughter, the food, the faces, the hour with no work? Did the dose make your friends funnier, or were they funny and the dose rode along? Did it make you belong, or did you belong and then step half outside yourself to feed a tug?

Think of the moments you remember from such nights. Do you remember smoke, or do you remember a story someone told, a look, a laugh that made your sides ache? The memory that stays has no ash in it.

Here are the questions whose only honest answers finish this part.

If breaks need smoke, why do non-smokers come back from breaks rested? If company needs smoke, why is the talk still warm when you remember it without a lit end in it? If the dose added something, why can you name the food and the laugh and the cold air in detail, but not a single draw that improved them?

We were not foolish to misplace the credit. We were conned on phoney information before we could judge, and then every pause and every meal and every laugh was paired with a dose until pairing felt like proof. Seen once, the pairing stops looking like proof. It looks like timing.

The work gave you the satisfaction of finishing. The pause gave you rest. The people gave you warmth. The dose gave you seconds at the tip and then took taste and air and minutes with it.

### The Hug That Won't Let Go

I want to show you what that timing hides, because this is where the ledger turns over.

Picture rough water at night. You are out of your depth. Your arms thrash. Your chest burns. Then strong arms close around you from behind and hold you up. You gasp. You cling. You feel flood of gratitude toward whoever holds you. Thank you. You saved me.

Then, in the hold, you feel something cold. The same arms that lift you are the arms that held you under a moment before. The rescue and the drowning come from the same grip. You were not saved from the water. You were let up for air so you would stay in the water.

That is what a dose does in a smoker's day. I say this carefully, because I do not want you to change from fright. I want you to see the shape clearly and then feel the difference it makes.

Between doses the body signals a faint lack. Not pain, exactly. An empty, slightly restless, slightly edgy little tug. Attention thins. Temper shortens. Hands want business. The mind, taught for years, reads it as, I need a pause, I need a reward, I need my friend.

You light. The tug quiets for minutes as the new dose arrives. The quiet feels like calm, like focus, like pleasure returning. You feel grateful toward the tube, as toward arms that lifted you.

But who put you in the water? Who created the tug that needed quieting? The previous dose, fading. The trap that guarantees the low returns a short while after every lift. The calm you credit to lighting is the brief muting of a restlessness lighting created. The focus is the brief muting of a scatter lighting created. The pleasure is the brief muting of an emptiness lighting created.

It never lifted you above where you stood before you started. It let you up for air and called the air a gift.

Ask when you were ever calm in a way a non-smoker is not. Ask when you ever thought more clearly than a clear head thinks. Ask when food ever tasted richer through smoke than without it. You never did. You only returned toward normal for minutes, from a low the last dose made certain, and paid with stale mouth and divided evenings and breath that would not come easy on the stairs.

This is why the garage needed spray and the plate needed leaving and the step needed lighting to feel like a step. Not because those moments are empty without smoke. Because the tug arrives between doses wherever you are, and the story follows to claim whatever good is near — the meal, the pause, the laugh — as if the dose made it.

Once you see the arms clearly, you cannot thank them the same way.

You smoked to close the evening and woke to open it with smoke. You smoked to mark work done and taught yourself work felt undone without it. You smoked to join the table and left the table to do it. At every point the rescuer was the perpetrator wearing rescue clothes.

The fact is simple, and it holds across plate and step and crowded table. It does plenty TO you. It does nothing FOR you.

Read that again in your own mouth, not as a slogan but as what you just watched. TO your taste, TO your breath, TO your evenings divided, TO your hands moving before thought. FOR your hunger, FOR your rest, FOR your friendship — nothing added that was not already there before you lit.

### Starting Glad Instead Of Gloomy

See what you now understand about your own list.

You understand the reasons arrived after the reach, not before it. You understand the seconds at lighting are not the day. You understand the meal gave, the pause restored, the people warmed, and the dose rode along and took credit. You understand a rescuer that puts you under is not a friend.

With that understanding, gloom would be the wrong clothing. Gloom belongs to weighing harm against pleasure and fearing the pleasure must be missed. You are no longer weighing. You are asking, and the asking has an answer you felt in your own mouth.

There is no loss here to mourn. There is only a taking seen clearly, so that stepping out feels like sense, not effort. You do not need to force a bright mood. You only need to let the mood match what you know. A person who finds nothing was taken does not begin in doom. He begins glad to have looked.

Let that gladness be how you read what comes next. Not braced, not sad, not counting what you will miss, but curious and upright, watching each dose show itself while we finish the work. The body you bring is the same body that tasted food fully before smoke covered it. The breaks you love are still breaks. The people you love are still yours.

Begin from that ground, as a person examining a con with clear eyes rather than as a person saying farewell to a friend.

I-03 — BEGIN BY FEELING GREAT TO BE ESCAPING
There is no doom here, only freedom ahead.

**SUMMARY**
- The harm-against-pleasure scale asks the wrong question and hides that no gift was given.
- Reasons said out loud sound learned, and the hand often moved before any reason formed.
- Seconds at lighting do not make the day better and leave a stale mouth after.
- Food tasted best while eating, not while smoke covered taste.
- Rest came from stopping, stretching and quiet, not from the dose paired with them.
- Warmth came from people and laughter, and the dose only stood nearby while it happened.
- The quiet after lighting was the brief muting of a tug the previous dose left behind.
- Starting glad fits what was seen, not braced or gloomy.
