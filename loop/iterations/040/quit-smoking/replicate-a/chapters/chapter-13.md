Chapter 13
YOUR DAYS WERE NEVER MADE OF SMOKE

*You have not lost your ordinary days. You get to live them exactly as they are.*

### COLD LINO AND KETTLE STEAM

Feet touch the cold kitchen floor while the kettle rises.

You stand a moment with the mug warming your palms and the window showing the same street it always showed. Teeth brushed. Face washed with cold water. Mouth tasting of sleep and toothpaste. Nothing tapping the ribs for a gap. Nothing counting minutes in the pocket. The day starts with tap and mug and latch, and you are in it from the first step.

You dress with the radio on. Shirt over head. Socks on the cold lino. Shoes laced while the kettle clicks off. You pour and watch the steam stand up off the mug and thin into the light from the window. You drink it standing, then with the second half sitting at the table where the post lay last night. Bills. A flyer for pizza. A letter you will answer at lunch. The house ticks as houses tick in the morning. Pipes. A neighbour's door. A bus pulling off down the road.

We used to smoke this hour before we lived it. Hand to pack before feet found floor. Lit end on empty lungs while the kettle boiled. Eyes half shut against the day while ash grew long over the sink. We called it starting. It was only feeding before starting.

You start now by starting. Rinse the cup from last night and set it to drain. Pull the coat from the hook. Check phone and keys. Pat the pockets and feel phone and keys and nothing that ticks. Turn the key in the latch and step out with the mouth tasting of toothpaste and the coat smelling of cupboard and night, and the morning doing what mornings do.

The step outside is the same step. Frost on the rail where frost belongs in season. Bin lids shut along the row. A cat picking its line along the wall. Your breath shows a second in the air and is gone. You walk to the corner with the collar up and the mug heat still in your palms from holding it, and the bus comes with its lights on and its windows fogged, and you climb on and tap the card and take the seat by the window where you always sit.

Look out as it pulls off. Shops with shutters half up. A man hosing the pavement outside the bakery. School bags bouncing. The same announcements crackling overhead. Nothing in you is waiting for the stop where you used to get off early to stand at the wind end. You ride past that stop and stay seated, and the ride is only a ride, and the morning holds together without a cut in the middle.

### THE SHOP AT LUNCH

By lunch the street does its ordinary turn.

You walk in for food because you are hungry, and the glass is there behind the till, rows of packs standing shoulder to shoulder under the light. The old price list you knew by heart is still there. The bell still dings when the door opens. The floor still squeaks by the cold cabinet. A man ahead pays for fuel for his car and a sandwich and steps aside to check his phone. A woman counts coins for bread and milk. The line moves the way lines move.

You stand in that line with your own list in your head. Ham and cheese, or soup in a pot, or the salad box with extra chicken you have taken before. The basket in your hand holds what you came for. The pocket of your coat holds phone and keys and nothing that ticks.

The girl at the till nods as she scans. Beep. Beep. The drawer opens and shuts. Someone asks for a lottery ticket. Someone asks for the toilet key. The glass door of the cold cabinet sighs as the man before you takes a drink and shuts it. You pay and take the coins and drop them back where they belong. No second purchase. No counting whether the box and the sandwich can both be afforded. No stepping aside to check whether one is left for after.

Take your food. Feel the light pocket. Walk out with both hands holding what you came for. The till keeps its glass. You keep your afternoon.

That afternoon is not an idea. It is hours with shape and weight, and you will live them through to the evening without a timetable cutting them into forty-minute slices.

Stand a second outside with the bag in your hand and let the street settle. Vans double-parked. Pigeons working the crumbs by the bench. The chemist sign swinging. The air smells of rain and chips and diesel and fresh bread from the bakery two doors down. Your coat smells of your morning, of bus and office and cold. That is how coats should smell at midday.

### EATING IT WHERE YOU SIT

Find your bench, your wall, your corner of the canteen, your seat by the window where the light falls across the table.

Sit down and open what you bought. Let the paper crackle. Let the steam rise off the soup. Let the bread give under your teeth. This is the part of the day we once hurried.

We used to eat with one eye on the door. Half the sandwich, then a look at the clock, then the last bites taken standing because the hands wanted to be outside managing ash. Food was fuel taken quickly so the real appointment could be kept at the wind end of the building. Taste was something we talked about while barely staying for it.

Stay for it now.

Take the first bite slowly and notice what is actually there. Salt. Butter. The sharp edge of cheese. The soft crumb that sticks for a second to the roof of the mouth. The heat of soup that warms the throat on the way down. Drink water between bites and taste how water lifts the mouth clear for the next bite. Nothing dry laid over it. Nothing bitter sitting behind it that water will not lift. Your tongue does on its own what it always knew how to do when no heat was pushed through it.

I sat on a low wall last spring with a pork pie and an apple and watched the street go by, and what struck me was how long lunch is when you stay inside it. No standing up halfway. No checking the pack to see if one is left for after. No doing sums about whether you can afford the box and the sandwich both. The pie took the time the pie takes. The apple took the time the apple takes. The street took its own time, and I had time to watch it.

You will notice the small things because you are not leaving them. The way the cheese pulls. The way the crust flakes onto your coat and you brush it off with your fingers. The way the coffee from the kiosk tastes of coffee itself, hot and a little bitter and milky at the edges, not of something laid on top of it. The way your nose finds the pickle before your teeth do. These are not grand pleasures. They are ordinary ones, and ordinary is exactly what we once missed while stepping out.

Eat to the end. Fold the paper. Push the crumbs into a pile with your thumb. Drink the last of the water. Sit a minute with your back against the bench and your shoulders where they are, not pulled up toward your ears waiting for the next gap. The clock above the shops moves and you let it move without chasing it. That unchased minute is what afternoons are made of now — not watched, not counted, just lived.

When you stand, you stand because you have finished, not because a tip has burned down. Pick up your rubbish. Put the bottle in the bin. Wipe your mouth with the back of your hand the way you always have. Your fingers smell of bread and salt. They smell of what you just held. That is all they need to smell of.

### THE WALK BACK WITH BOTH HANDS FULL

The walk back is part of lunch too.

We used to make that walk a corridor between two lit ends. Shop to doorway with one, doorway to desk with the thought of the next. Eyes down. Steps quick. Talk cut half short because the tip was burning and the wind was taking it. We told ourselves we liked the stroll. What we liked was the pause from the desk, and we let paper stand beside the pause and take the bow.

Walk it now with both hands holding what they hold.

Bag in one hand. Phone in the other, or keys, or nothing at all. Coat buttoned. Collar up if the wind is up. Feel the pavement under your shoes. Hear your own steps and the traffic and the shutters coming down and up. Look in the windows you always passed without seeing — the hardware shop with buckets stacked, the charity shop with the blue vase, the barber turning his sign to open. The street has been there all along. You are seeing more of it because you are not watching a burning tip.

I know that stretch by my old office where the shelter stands outside the chemist. For years my feet turned there without asking. Rain or shine, the feet turned, the hand went to the pocket, the eyes looked for a dry corner. The first week after my last stubbed end, the feet still turned. Not from pain. From habit wearing my voice. I did not argue with the feet. I kept walking past the shelter to the corner and crossed where the light is better. By the third day the turn had softened. By the second week the shelter was just a shelter where other people stood, and my walk had its own line again.

Yours will find its own line too. Cross where you like. Stop where you like. If the legs want a longer way back, take the longer way. There is no tip to finish before you go in, so the length of the walk is yours to choose. Five minutes or fifteen. Past the park or past the station. The air on the face does the work the pause always did — the stand, the distance, the minute away from the screen. Your legs wanted standing. Your eyes wanted distance. That is what they get.

Notice your chest as you walk. It takes the hill without the shallow sip and the cough to clear. It takes the cold without heat on top of it. Nothing grand. Nothing forced. Just air moving where smoke used to push through. Shoulders sit lower now than they did between doses, because there is no next gap to watch for.

Go back in through the door you always use. Hold it for the person behind you. Stamp your feet if it is wet. Put your bag down where you always put it. The building smells the same — dust and coffee and cleaning fluid on Mondays. You smell of the street and of lunch, not of a wind end. That is not a small detail. Coats and curtains keep the score long after we have stopped noticing.

### THE DESK THROUGH THE AFTERNOON

The desk is where the day is won.

Papers where you left them. Screen asleep. Mug with the ring inside. Phone blinking with two messages you missed at lunch. For years this return was the hardest minute. Sit down, wake the screen, feel the shoulders tighten, eyes slide to the clock and the doorway.

Sit down now and start where you left off.

Wake the screen. Open the top paper. Read the first line again to find your place. Take a sip of water. The work sits where you left it, untouched by smoke and untouched by lack of it. The minute does what minutes do when no one sets them on fire.

The afternoon has its own rhythm, and you will feel it return. First hour, quick and bright from food and movement. Second hour, slower, with the light shifting across the desk. Third hour, with tired eyes and a stiff neck asking for a stand and a stretch. None of this needs interpreting. It is what afternoons do to people who sit at desks.

You know the papers sit untouched by smoke and untouched by lack of it.

Pick up the phone and say the difficult sentence you have been putting off. Your voice sounds plain in the room, and the answer comes back plain, and you write the figure down in the margin with the date beside it. Open the long report and put it into three piles — done, doing, waiting — and square the done pile with the palm of your hand. Answer the short email first to clear the deck. Two lines. Sent. Stand to file and feel the floor cold through your shoes on the way to the cabinet. Walk to the printer and back while the pages warm and drop. Fill the mug at the tap and drink half standing by the window, looking for thirty seconds at roofs and gulls and washing hung two streets over. Talk to the person two desks down about the job, about the delivery date and the missing invoice, and laugh if there is something to laugh at while you stand there.

These are the hinges of a workday. The drawer opening and shutting. The pen uncapped and capped. The chair pushed back and pulled in. The folder carried from desk to shelf and laid flat. You swing the day along hinge by hinge, line by line, and the pile gets smaller under your hands.

When the clock shows half past, stand if you want to stand. Roll the shoulders. Drink water. Move. Then sit and finish the line you were on. Go back in when you are ready, not when a tip burns down. That difference sounds small until you live it for a week. No watching the burn. No nursing it to make it last. No hurrying it because the boss is looking. The pause ends when you decide the pause has done its job.

By four o'clock the light thins and the office hums lower. You will feel tired in the honest way that food and work and hours bring. Tired eyes. Tired feet. A head full of words. Take a sip and stand and let the legs take the weight a minute. The day closes the way days close — with the last email sent and the drawer shut and the chair pushed in.

### HANDS IN ORDINARY WORK

Watch your hands through that afternoon and you will see something you missed for years.

They are never empty. They hold hammer, pen, mug, steering wheel, phone, rail, paper, keyboard. They button coats. They turn keys. They peel oranges. They tap numbers. For years we taught them one more grip and then told ourselves the grip was needed to make the hands feel right. It never made them feel right. It cut talk in half to watch ash.

Yours hold what the job asks them to hold, and they put it down when the job is done. That putting down is worth noticing. Pen down. Mug down. Paper squared. Hands flat on the desk for a second doing nothing at all. We forgot how to let hands do nothing. Every gap had to be filled with something between fingers. Now a gap is just a gap — a minute with hands still, eyes on the window, chest taking air without commentary.

I watched a driver at the lights last month with both hands on the wheel, drumming his thumbs to the radio, window down an inch for the cool. For years my own right hand at those lights would have been out of the window managing a tip, left hand tight on the wheel, eyes on the mirror for ash. I did not envy him his lit end. I pitied the old timetable that once made me miss the song for the sake of the tip. My hands on the wheel now stay where they belong, and the song plays to the end.

Yours will learn the same stillness again, because stillness was their first nature before the timetable taught them its fidget. Holding a mug with both hands while the tea cools enough to drink. Holding the rail on the stairs. Holding the bag while you wait for the lift. Holding nothing while you listen to someone finish their sentence. Each hold is complete without anything added to it.

If the old half-lift comes — hand half-rising before thought, eyes sliding to the pocket — see it for what it is. Memory wearing your voice. It lived in your mouth for years, so of course it pipes up in its old accent. You do not need to debate it. You know that livery too well to walk behind it. Stay with the conversation, the task, the street you are on. Take a sip of water and keep the hands in the work they were in. Unanswered, that pull loses interest, and one morning you notice the morning asked nothing at all.

That noticing will come quietly. Not with thunder. With the ordinary surprise of reaching the end of a meeting without once thinking of the door. With finishing a drive without once checking the mirror for a place to stop. With closing a drawer and finding nothing in it that taps. Each quiet is a receipt. It tells you, in the body before big words arrive, that there was nothing to keep and therefore no wound in laying it down.

### THE SECOND SHOP AND THE EVENING TABLE

On the way home the shops open their second test, and it is kinder than you expect.

You go in for milk and bread and something for tomorrow's lunch. The queue is longer now, with school bags and tired feet and baskets full of evening meals. The glass is there again behind the till. The till keeps its glass. You keep what you came for — the carton cold in your hand, the loaf warm under your arm.

Walk out with both hands holding the evening. Feel the bag pull at your fingers. Feel the keys find their place. The street smells of rain on warm pavement and chips from the corner shop and diesel from the bus pulling away. Your coat smells of your day, not of a wind end. That is how coats should smell at six o'clock.

Home is the same rooms with the same light. Table where the post piles. Kettle where the hand finds it without looking. Chair that knows your weight. For years the first act on coming in was the step to the back door or the window, coat still on, bag still on the shoulder, to keep the timetable quiet before the evening could start. The evening started late and started with ash.

Start it now with the bag on the table.

Put the milk away. Put the bread in the bin. Rinse the mug from this morning and set it to drain. Change your shoes. Wash your hands with hot water and soap and watch the day thin and go down the plughole. Sit for five minutes with tea before you start on supper, not because a rule says to pause, but because legs like sitting after standing all day.

Supper is chopping and hearing the knife on the board. Onion sweetening in oil. Salt pinched and added. The steam on the glass is from the pot. Sit to eat while it is hot. Chew. Drink water. Have seconds if you are still hungry.

Push the plate away when hunger is finished. Carry the plates to the sink and run the hot tap and watch the steam stand up again in the evening light. Scrape. Stack. Wash the pan while it is still warm so the oil lifts without scrubbing. Wipe the board and wring the cloth and hang it over the tap. Put the leftovers in the tub with the lid pressed down for tomorrow. Sweep the crumbs from the table into your palm with the side of your hand and tip them into the bin. Put the dry plates back on the shelf where they live. The kitchen ticks back into order the way kitchens do when hands stay with them to the end.

Then sit. Lamp on. Shoes off. Cushion pulled to your back. The room holds its evening shape around you — curtains not yet drawn, sky going blue at the window, radiator clicking, clock moving without being watched. Hold tea with both hands and drink it while it is hot enough to warm the palms. Watch the programme you always watch. Answer the message you missed at lunch. Talk about the day in plain sentences — the queue, the bench, the invoice, the bus pulling away. Fold the washing that has sat on the chair since morning. Put one pile away and leave one for tomorrow.

That late-night patio with friends and drinks holds its laughter without asking for anything laid over it.

The hours pass the way owned hours pass. Nine with the second tea. Ten with the windows locked and the bins taken to the step for morning. Teeth brushed. Face washed. Bed turned down. The house goes quiet room by room, and you go through it switching lights off the way you switch off any finished work. No doorway chosen. No pocket weight checked. No step to the back door with coat still on. The night outside the window is still yours — rest, breath, sleep ahead — held now without the hourly tap on the ribs.

When an old picture drifts by in your own accent, you recognise the old uniform and stay with the day you are in.

The days ahead are not tests. They are mornings with kettle steam and cold lino underfoot. They are shop queues with baskets holding what you came for. They are desks with piles that shrink under your hands. They are walks with both hands holding the evening. They are tables with talk that carries without help. Live them exactly as they are, and let each ordinary hour hand its proof to the next.

YIPPEE! I'M FREE!

SUMMARY
- Mornings start with kettle and water and light, and the hands have their ordinary work from the first step.
- The shop glass stays where it is while the basket holds lunch and the pocket stays light through the afternoon.
- Lunch is eaten to the end, tasting of itself, finished when hunger is finished.
- The walk back is chosen for its own sake, with legs and eyes taking what they always wanted from a pause.
- The desk day moves by doing, with piles shrinking under the hands and pauses ending when they have done their job.
- Hands hold what the job asks and rest when the job is done, without an added grip.
- Evenings hold food and talk and company exactly as they are, with old pictures arriving pre-labelled and left to pass.
