### THE CONNOISSEUR'S CLAIM — WHY TASTE FEELS LIKE PROOF

We left the kitchen of deserving. You saw that the kindness was yours. The lodger was not.

But now another voice steps forward. Cooler. More confident. A connoisseur, not a comforter.

It says:

"I just like it. I love the taste. That's why I do it."

This is the voice that feels most honest. P-02 says it with a shrug — "just a treat, I love chocolate, what's wrong with that?" P-01 says it after the packet is empty — "I just crave sweets and when i eat them, i feel like i cant stop" — and hears the craving as proof the taste must be wonderful. If the taste were not wonderful, why would you crave it?

We have all said it. I said it for years. We say it because it feels rude to deny it. Who are you to tell me what I like?

I am not telling you. I am asking you to watch what you do.

Do you hear how strange the claim becomes the moment we examine it like investigators, not defendants? If BAD SUGAR were a genuine pleasure or treat because of its taste, why does the entire behaviour surround itself with elaborate disguises? Why would a true taste pleasure need so much help to be tasted at all?

The fact is you have never tasted BAD SUGAR alone and called it wonderful. Not once.

Think. When did you last sit down to a spoonful of white sugar from the bag and sigh with pleasure? When did you chew plain white flour and close your eyes and say delicious? When did you drink sugar dissolved in hot water and call it a treat?

You didn't. You don't. No one does.

So what do you love?

You will answer quickly: "Chocolate. Biscuits. Ice cream. Pastries. Cola. White bread warm with butter."

Good. Keep that list. We will test it. Keep it in front of you while we look more closely, because the list itself will give the game away.

Is it sugar you love? Or is it the company sugar arrives with?

Do you love sugar — or do you love chocolate, butter, cream, salt, crunch, cold, warmth, and memory?

Ask honestly. Your answer will be the end of this illusion. It is simple. It is joyful. And it is not sacrifice at all.

### YOUR OWN EVIDENCE AGAINST YOU — THE BITTER YOU HIDE

The strongest evidence is not my argument. It is your own behaviour. Watch what we do when we claim we love the taste.

We hide BAD SUGAR. Then we hide behind it.

Take your daily coffee or tea. Many of you who swear you love sugar add it precisely where taste is most unpleasant. Coffee is bitter. Tea is bitter. Grapefruit is bitter. You add BAD SUGAR not because the sugar tastes glorious, but because the bitter tastes unwelcome. You are not revealing a love. You are masking a dislike.

If sugar were delicious on its own, you would not need the bitter drink to carry it. You would take it neat. Do you?

Or take white bread, white pasta, white rice — the lonely carbs we count inside BAD SUGAR. On their own, what are they? Try them. Cold white bread with nothing on it. Plain boiled white rice with no salt, no oil, no sauce. Plain pasta with nothing. Is that the taste you dream about at 10pm? Is that the taste you crave in the cinema queue?

Of course not. Never.

We never eat them lonely. We invite company over to make them palatable.

Butter. Cheese. Olive oil. Salt. Tomato sauce rich with fat. Chocolate — which is mostly fat. Cream in ice cream. The crunch of biscuit, which is fat and salt as much as sugar.

Without the company, those lonely carbs would be tasteless. You would leave them on the plate. You know this because you do leave them. Nobody steals the plain rice cakes from the cupboard. Everybody steals the chocolate biscuits.

Does that sound like a love of sugar?

If you loved the taste of BAD SUGAR itself, why would BAD SUGAR need butter to be edible?

If BAD SUGAR were the pleasure, why would you need chocolate to want it?

You see the inversion already. It is not there yet, but it is coming. Keep watching.

Think of the sweets you say you love most. Unwrap one in your mind. Now mentally peel the layers away.

A milk chocolate: cocoa mass which is bitter, cocoa butter which is fat, milk powder which is fat and protein, vanilla. The sugar is the loudest sweet note, but remove the fat and the chocolate becomes cocoa powder — bitter, dry, inedible. Do you love that powder by the spoonful?

A biscuit: flour, butter, salt. Remove butter and salt and you have dry flour and sugar. Lovely?

Ice cream: cream, cold, vanilla. Cold itself anaesthetises taste. Remove cream and cold and you have syrup.

You have never loved BAD SUGAR. You have loved fat, salt, chocolate, cream, crunch, cold, aroma, and context — and BAD SUGAR was sneaking a ride on all of them.

Here is a question whose only honest answer concedes the point:

If BAD SUGAR tastes so good, why do we have to make it taste like something else before we want it?

And another:

If you truly loved the taste, why would one bite so often become the box — not because each bite gets more delicious, but because each bite leaves you wanting another?

The trap wants you to read that want as proof of deliciousness. It is not. A delicious taste satisfies. You have a ripe peach and you sigh. Lovely. Enough. You have BAD SUGAR and you hover. Again? Is that pleasure? Or is that the faint, slightly restless hum asking for another hit?

We will come to that hum later. For now stay with taste. Stay with your tongue. Your tongue does not crave. Your mind was taught to interpret the hum as love of taste.

I know this is hard to accept. I know the connoisseur in you says, "But I am a foodie. I appreciate sweetness." I did too. We learned connoisseurship from the very industry that profits when we call our craving taste. They gave us tasting notes for sugar the way dealers give tasting notes for product.

But look at what children do before brainwashing is complete. Offer a child very dark unsweetened cocoa, or black coffee. They pull a face. Offer the same child a strawberry, warm from the sun. They light up. Their bodies know sweetness. Natural sweetness — whole fruit, milk — is gentle, fragrant, fleeting. BAD SUGAR is not gentle. It is loud. It paints over flavour. Have you noticed that after a week of heavy sweets, fruit tastes oddly dull? Then after a break, the same fruit tastes vivid?

That is not your taste proving BAD SUGAR is delicious. That is BAD SUGAR dulling the taste that was delicious before it arrived.

Is it not obvious you were taught to call the paint the picture?

### THE HOUSE-PARTY GATECRASHER — WHO IS ACTUALLY DELICIOUS?

Let me give you the image and then you can never un-see it.

Lonely carbs invite company over.

Picture a flat. Bare. White walls. Empty fridge. This is plain white flour, plain rice, plain sugar. Nothing there you would cross the street for. No music. No laughter. You would not come to this party. No one would.

So the lonely carbs send out invitations to make themselves palatable. They invite butter. They invite chocolate. They invite cream, cheese, salt, roast aroma, crunch. The flat fills up. Music plays. The room smells wonderful. People are laughing. Now you want to come. Of course you do.

Who makes the party good? The guests. The company. Not the flat.

BAD SUGAR is the flat. Fat, chocolate, butter, salt, aroma and texture are the guests. You never loved the flat. You loved the party. But BAD SUGAR arrived first on the invitation list, so you credited the flat for the music.

That is the gatecrasher truth in one breath: BAD SUGAR was sneaking a ride on flavours you already loved, and taking the applause when you thanked the host.

Once you see the gatecrasher, you cannot taste the same way again.

Do the demonstration with me. Real, in your kitchen, not as theory. You do not need to starve yourself or use willpower. You are only investigating. Carry on as normal until you finish this book. For now, taste in your head or, if you like, for real — but as a scientist.

First, taste the company without BAD SUGAR. A spoonful of cocoa powder alone. Dry. Bitter. Do you love it? A pat of butter on warm toast without jam — is that delicious? Yes. Plain salted nuts? Yes. A cold strawberry? Yes. Good cheese? Yes. The company is delicious even without the gatecrasher.

Now taste the gatecrasher without company. A teaspoon of white sugar alone. White flour paste. A sugary drink made only of sugar and water, no flavour, no fizz, no colour. Do you dream of that? Would you pay for it in a restaurant?

You would not. You would push it away.

So who was delicious all along?

It was the company. Not the sugar.

This is why the willpower method has always failed you around taste, and why it was cruel to blame you. The willpower method says, "Resist delicious sugar." It tells you to white-knuckle past a treat that tastes wonderful. That frame keeps the illusion at full strength. You feel deprived because you believe you are depriving yourself of flavour.

But this method says something kinder and truer: there is nothing delicious to resist. BAD SUGAR is not delicious — it hitches a lift on what is delicious and claims the credit. You do not need the willpower method to avoid an empty flat. You just need to see who actually brought the music.

Three questions. Answer them privately and honestly. No one is scoring you. The answers change everything.

If BAD SUGAR were the delicious part, why does chocolate without sugar still smell like chocolate, and sugar without chocolate smell like nothing?

If BAD SUGAR were the treat, why does removing BAD SUGAR from bread leave you with bread you still enjoy, while removing the company from BAD SUGAR leaves you with something you would never eat?

If your favourite biscuit's pleasure came from BAD SUGAR, why does a plain biscuit eaten when you are truly hungry taste of butter and salt first, sugar second — and sugar never at all when the biscuit is good?

You know. You have always known. The fat carries aroma. Salt lifts flavour. Chocolate is rich. Crunch is joy. Warmth is comfort.

BAD SUGAR borrows all of it.

Call it plainly and you will feel amusement where shame used to sit. The confident connoisseur softens and laughs: "I was applauding the gatecrasher for the band's music."

That laugh is freedom beginning.

### THE NON-USER NOSE TEST — WHAT HAPPENS WHEN COMPANY LEAVES

Now let us isolate the variable in another way — through the nose, not the tongue. Because taste is not only tongue. It is smell, memory, hunger, and cue.

You know the cue. Wrapper crinkle. Bakery air. The bakery near the station that smells like warm butter at 7am. The petrol station sweets by the till. The cinema smell before the film. Even before you taste, your mouth waters. The trap says, "See? Your body loves sugar's taste. It knows."

Does it?

Ask the non-user test.

Does that bakery smell alluring to someone who has not been eating BAD SUGAR? Or does it smell heavy, cloying, faintly sickly? Have you noticed how a person who eats simply — lots of whole food, little BAD SUGAR — walks past the sweets aisle with nothing, while you, on a day of grazing, cannot walk past without a pull?

If BAD SUGAR's taste were objectively wonderful, its smell would be objectively wonderful to all noses. It is not. The smell is wonderful to a nose that has been trained to expect a hit, and ordinary or sickly to a nose that has not.

That difference matters. It proves the allure is not in the substance, but in the brain's learned anticipation. The wrapper, the smell, the logo — these are cues that fire because the last dose taught them to fire. They are not flavour reports. They are memory triggers.

Modern brain imaging work gives a plausible picture for why this feels so physical. Refined carbs and fats can evoke activity in deep reward areas, and the combination of fat plus sugar seems to have a stronger effect than either alone — "seems to" because the picture is mixed and comes largely from animal work and from imaging in people who carry a lot of commercial weight, and imaging alone cannot prove cause. The consistent story those studies are consistent with — not proven by — is that with frequent hits the background reward tone can get a little crowded and blunted while the cue itself — the wrapper, the smell, the sight — gets extra hot and jumpy.

I must keep the limit plain, because honesty outranks force. That escalation and that trigger are mechanistically plausible and match what you describe — needing the family-size pack now where a small bar once did, and feeling the pull before the first bite — but they are interpretation, not settled proof, and they do not mean you have a brain disease. They mean the trap has a plausible way to make a want feel like a need.

Do you see how kind that framing is? You are not weak-willed for needing more to feel the same, or for firing at the sight of a logo. You were responding to plausible biology being played like an instrument by an industry that designs bliss points. Your nose was trained.

And here is the release in that same fact: if the cue fires because it learned to fire, it can learn to quiet when the hits stop and the belief changes. You do not have to fight smell with willpower. You just need to stop crediting the gatecrasher for the bakery's butter, for the chocolate's richness, for the crunch you love.

Isolate the variable one final time. Tonight, not as a diet, not as the willpower method, simply as curiosity — take something you think proves your love of taste and split it.

You think you love the biscuit? Have butter on toast without sugar and notice the flavour. Have the same biscuit but read the label and taste the fat and salt separately. Which tongue-note was pleasure? Which was just sweet loudness painting over it?

You think you love cola? Drink sparkling water with a squeeze of fresh citrus cold. What remains of the pleasure when sweet paint is removed? The cold, the fizz, the occasion, the relief of a pause — or the sugar?

The answer will amuse you. It is so clear once you look.

There is no genuine pleasure or treat in BAD SUGAR. There is pleasure in fat, in chocolate, in butter, in salt, in cold, in crunch, in aroma, in warmth, in being with people, in a pause you gave yourself. BAD SUGAR only ever got the credit because it arrived stapled to the invitation.

Say the two sentences that land this. Say them as fact, not affirmation:

It is you that loves flavour. Not the sugar.

It is the company that was delicious. Not the gatecrasher.

### THE FIREWALL — WHAT TO IGNORE WHILE YOU FINISH

You have just demolished one of the trap's most persuasive pillars on your own evidence. You did not resist taste. You understood it. That is the whole method.

But precisely now, the trap will try to send messengers to rebuild what you just took down.

They sound helpful. They sound kind. They sound like science, like care, like lived experience.

A headline: "A little of what you love does you good." A friend who loves you: "Just cut down — I cut down and I feel great." An influencer with a before-and-after: "I quit sugar by willpower and suffering, and so can you — watch me resist." A diet: "Allow yourself one treat meal, you've earned it, enjoy BAD SUGAR in moderation." A voice in your own head rehearsed since childhood: "Just one won't hurt, I deserve a genuine pleasure or treat."

Each messenger carries the same script: that taste pleasure is real, that control is a matter of discipline, that your freedom will come through resisting what you love.

You have just seen that the pleasure was the company, not the gatecrasher. You do not need a messenger to re-convince your tongue. Your tongue knows. Guard the belief, not the behaviour. Do not let an outside voice talk you back into crediting the lodger for the host's music.

The second thing those messengers do is smuggle the willpower method back in as the route. I name it plainly now because this chapter carries its funeral: the willpower method — diets, rules, restriction, "be good this week," day-counting, resisting a pleasure you still believe is real — is the anti-method that kept you trapped. It tells you taste is wonderful and then asks you to be strong. That is cruelty disguised as advice. It guarantees you feel deprived, then blames you for caving.

Do you see? No wonder it failed. No wonder you failed on it. The method failed you. The taste was never wonderful in the way it claimed, and willpower can never solve a misunderstanding about taste.

So we put a firewall around the method while you read. Not forever. Just until you finish and walk free with the whole picture. Keep eating exactly as normal while you read, if you wish — carry on. But do not let other explanations rent space in your head until you have finished investigating this one.

This is not closed-mindedness. It is open-mindedness done properly: question what you think you know about sugar, test it against your own mouth and nose and memory, finish the experiment, then decide. All you have to do is follow all the instructions while you read, including this next one. It is easy. It costs nothing. It protects the quiet amusement you just felt when the gatecrasher was caught.

Here it is, exactly as you must follow it, with warmth and with total confidence that it makes your escape easier, immediately:

5. Read that again. It is not a moral rule. It is a thinking rule. Diets and willpower tips and suffering stories all re-install the very belief you just removed — that BAD SUGAR is a genuine pleasure or treat to be managed by strength. Let them talk elsewhere. While you are with this book, stay with the evidence of your own tongue, your own nose, your own bored bakery walk.

And the medical line matters. If you live with diabetes, with an eating disorder, if you take medication that food affects, or if a clinician guides your eating, keep following that guidance — this book does not replace medical care. Freedom from BAD SUGAR happens inside medical safety, never against it. The firewall is for brainwashing, never for clinical advice.

Follow this and the taste illusion cannot rebuild itself behind your back. You isolated the variable. You saw who was delicious. Keep seeing it. When a messenger says, "But you love the taste," answer with what you now know, warmly, factually, without fight:

That taste was sneaking a ride.

Keep that verdict handy. It will do more work for you than any diet sheet ever did.
