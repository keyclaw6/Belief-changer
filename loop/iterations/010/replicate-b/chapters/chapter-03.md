### THE WRONG SUM — WHY WE KEEP WEIGHING HARM AGAINST PLEASURE

We have just cleared one lie together.

You were not freely choosing. You were conned before you could choose, and the Sugar Trap has been choosing for you while letting you hold the packet. That was Chapter 2. Feel that relief again for a second. You were trapped, not weak.

And yet — I can hear the next thought forming before you have even phrased it.

I know you can. I have heard it from thousands of readers in this exact spot.

"Okay, so I was conned. Okay, so the choice wasn't free. But it still feels good. So even if it does some harm, isn't the pleasure worth it?"

Isn't that the sum we all learned to do?

On one side we put the costs: a bit heavier, a bit more tired, teeth that need work, the crash at three, the money, the guilt after the box is empty. On the other side we put the benefit: taste, lift, comfort, treat, reward. Then we weigh them.

And we tell ourselves the benefit side wins.

You know that calculation? It feels reasonable. It feels adult. It even feels honest. "I know sugar isn't great for me, but I enjoy it. Life is short. A little pleasure is worth it."

That is the voice of P-02, the in-denial moderate in all of us. The one who says, "Just a treat." "I don't keep it in the house — except when I do." "Once a week is fine." And it is also P-04, the yo-yoer, who says, "I need something right now to get through."

But notice what that calculation quietly assumes.

It assumes there IS a benefit to weigh.

It assumes BAD SUGAR gives you something real — a genuine pleasure or treat — and the only question is whether that pleasure outweighs the harm.

What if that assumption is false?

I want you to feel the difference between two questions. The first is the one you have been asking for years:

"Does the harm outweigh the pleasure?"

The second is the one we are asking now:

"What good is it at all?"

Do you see the axis switch?

The first question hands sugar a win before the debate starts. It credits it with pleasure. It says, "We know it gives you something wonderful, we are just haggling over the price."

The second question refuses that gift. It says, "Prove the good. Name it. Put it on the table. Let me see it."

That is the move this chapter performs. We are not here to argue that harm is bigger than benefit. We are here to look, together, at the benefit side of the ledger — and find it empty.

If it is empty, the weighing trick collapses. You cannot outweigh harm with nothing. You cannot pay a bill with an empty account. And you do not need willpower to walk away from nothing. You just need to see it.

Is that too stark to accept so quickly? I understand. You have been told since childhood that sweetness is pleasure. So let us not assert it and ask you to believe. Let us put a real ledger on the table and let you fill it in.

### THE LEDGER ON THE TABLE — doing TO you vs doing FOR you

Take any bank statement. Two columns. Money in. Money out.

Now imagine we do the same for BAD SUGAR. Not in theory. In your body, in your day.

On the left, we write: What is it doing TO you?
On the right, we write: What is it doing FOR you?

The left is the cost. The right is the claimed benefit.

We write the heading for the right column carefully. This is where we debut a phrase you will use for the rest of this book. It is simple and it does a lot of work. It is: doing TO you vs doing FOR you.

You will hear me say that many times. Keep those little words TO and FOR capitalized in your head, because they carry the whole distinction. TO is what the dose extracts from you. FOR is what it genuinely gives to you that you could not get otherwise in any other way.

We have lived with the trap so long we blur them. We say "it gives me energy" when in fact it takes energy and then briefly relieves the low it created. We say "it comforts me" when in fact it creates unease and then briefly quietens it. The trap trains us to list TO items in the FOR column.

So we will be strict.

For something to count in the FOR column, it must meet three tests.

One — it must be real, not just the ending of a discomfort sugar itself caused. Relief from tight shoes is not a benefit of tight shoes.

Two — it must be unique. If the same pleasure happens just as well without BAD SUGAR — the laugh with friends, the rest, the warm room — then BAD SUGAR is sneaking a ride, not driving.

Three — it must be yours at the time, not borrowed from later. If the lift now buys a crash later, that is not FOR you. That is TO you, deferred.

Fair?

I will go first and fill the left column. You will fill the right.

The left — doing TO you — is not my opinion. It is what bodies do. And I will state it flatly, as it is, because we need to see what we are weighing nothing against.

Then I will ask you, with warmth and with total confidence in your honesty, to put a single item in the right column that survives those three tests.

Can you do that?

Let us look.

### WHAT IT IS DOING TO YOU — THE BILL THAT ARRIVES LATER

I want you to see the left column without fear and without lecture. Just as facts about what happens when a lot of free sugars become the normal daily pattern.

First, teeth.

The fact is, free sugars drive acid decay. You eat or drink something with BAD SUGAR, the bacteria in plaque feed on it and make acid, and that acid softens enamel. Do it often enough and the surface cavities. This is not mysterious and it is not contested. It is the mechanism global health bodies point to when they give healthy-diet guidance, and the reason a taste for sweetness that looks innocent in childhood becomes a dental bill in adulthood.

Does that mean every sweet guarantees a cavity no matter how you brush, whether you use fluoride, how often you eat? No. Brushing with fluoride and dental care matter. Plaque, time, hygiene — all mediate. But the direction is fixed: more frequent free sugars push the acid bath more often TO you. That is a real TO. It is doing something TO your mouth while you tell yourself it is doing something FOR your mood.

Second, load.

High added sugar, taken often and in quantity, carries a metabolic and cardiovascular load. Institutional summaries — the kind that look at whole populations — link a pattern high in added sugars with higher odds of heart and stroke trouble over years. The pathways they name are not exotic: the liver gets more than it handles easily, blood pressure tends to sit higher, chronic low-grade inflammation smoulders, and appetite control gets bypassed so you eat more than you meant.

I say that without hedging the population finding and without exaggerating your personal tomorrow. No single biscuit gives you heart disease. No one number predicts you. These are population patterns, teaspoon figures are approximate, bodies differ. But the pattern is not "free energy." The lift you feel after a sweet is not free fuel. It is a deferred load. The body pays interest later.

You have felt that interest, haven't you? The roller coaster. You reach for sugary somethings to get through the afternoon. There is a short lift. Then a fast drop. Then you need another sweet to feel okay again. That cycle is not fuel. That is borrowing.

Third, ceiling.

We live in a world where normal already exceeds what guidance says is healthy. A major health body sets a ceiling for free sugars — the sugars added to food plus those in syrups, honey, juices and sugary drinks — of less than ten percent of daily energy, roughly fifty grams, with an additional benefit below five percent. That ceiling is not a prescription for you personally, not a diet, not a zero on fruit — intrinsic sugars inside whole fruit, vegetables and plain dairy are not the target here. It is a population ceiling. And our normal diet, with biscuits and bread and pastries and sweet drinks and packaged snacks, lives above it without us noticing. When normal is over the ceiling, "I just have a normal amount" cannot be your guide.

Do you see what is gathering in the left column?

Acid TO your teeth. Load TO your vessels and liver and appetite system. Crash TO your afternoon. Guidance that says your normal is already high — TO your definition of "moderate."

That is what BAD SUGAR is doing TO you.

Now I must do what I promised in the earlier chapters and fully keep. I frightened you a little. That was on purpose, because the truth about the TO side is frightening if you stay inside the old sum. And now I disown fear as the reason to stop.

Listen carefully. I do not want you to quit because you are scared of cavities or of a heart statistic. If you quit out of fear, you still believe you are giving up a genuine pleasure or treat and are being forced by scare to sacrifice it. That is the willpower story. It leaves the FOR column intact and makes you feel deprived. Fear makes the trap tighter.

We looked at TO not to scare you into stopping. We looked so you cannot pretend the left column is light while you hunt for something in the right to outweigh it. The TO column is heavy enough. It proves the price is real.

But price means nothing if the good is real. So we must now turn, honestly, to the right column.

What is BAD SUGAR actually doing FOR you?

### WHAT IS IT DOING FOR YOU — NAME ONE THING THAT SURVIVES

I will not answer this for you. You must try.

I want you to speak to me as you speak to yourself. Use your own lines. I know them because you have lent them to me.

What does BAD SUGAR do FOR you?

Does it give you energy?

Does it give you a treat?

Does it taste wonderful?

Does it calm you, comfort you, reward you, help you connect?

Pick the best one. The one you would defend to a friend. Put it in the FOR column.

Now let us test it, together, with the three rules. Not to trick you. To be fair to you.

Take the first favourite: "It gives me energy. I need something right now."

You know this one if you are P-04. Three o'clock. Desk. Eyes flat. You reach for something sweet. You feel a lift. You tell yourself that proves it works.

Ask: is that lift genuine energy added on top, or is it relief from a low?

The fact is, bodies make glucose themselves from protein and glycerol — you are not dependent on imported sugar for fuel. That single fact ends the deprivation fear: stopping BAD SUGAR is not nutritional deprivation. You are not removing fuel. So what was the lift?

Look at the sequence you described yourself: "I would have something quick and sweet but now know that will only give a short term lift which will inevitably be followed by a fast drop." That is not my line. That is your line, recovered writers use it exactly. Short lift. Fast drop. Inevitable. The second part is not optional. It is the sugar's own after-effect.

And look at the other line you know intimately: "I just crave sweets and when I eat them, I feel like I can't stop." One bite becomes the box. Does that read like refuelling? Or does that read like a loop being triggered?

If it were fuel, one serving would satisfy. You would be steady. You would not need another within the hour. The lift would last like food lasts.

Does it?

Or does the "energy" feel like taking tight shoes off for a second — relief from the restlessness the last sweet created — then the shoes tighten again?

Second favourite: "It's my treat. I deserve it. I have managed to be good this week."

You know this one if you are P-03, and P-02 borrows it daily. Celebrating, rewarding myself, going to the cinema with chocolate. The language of deserve.

Ask: did you fear missing love or celebration when you were a child before the first sweet was handed to you with the story "this is a treat"?

No. You learned that story at the same time as you tasted the sweet. The treat-meaning was glued on.

And ask the isolate-variable test. Same hard day. Same kitchen. Same deserved kindness. One night you give yourself twenty quiet minutes, a warm drink without sugar, a note to a friend, a walk. Another night you give yourself the packet. Is the warmth, the relief, the "I am caring for myself" — is that coming from BAD SUGAR, or is BAD SUGAR sneaking a ride on the self-recognition, the rest, the moment's company?

If it were a genuine pleasure or treat, you would feel satisfied after, not a little hollow and ready to make a rule about tomorrow. Does the treat leave you satisfied? Or does it leave you negotiating?

"I've managed to be good this week" — does that sound like a person who has just received love? Or a person who just fed a loop and is already planning the next fee?

Third favourite: "I love the taste."

We will demolish that fully in its own chapter — taste will get its day in court. But even here, ask the quick test: do you love the taste of white sugar by the spoonful? Do you eat plain bagels of white flour for the taste alone? Or do you love sugar when it has invited company — butter, chocolate, cream, salt, crunch, context — lonely carbs that invite the party over to make themselves palatable?

If the taste were genuine and unique, you would not need to hide bitter tastes with sweetness to make them go down.

Does this count as a genuine pleasure or treat doing something FOR you that nothing else could do?

Or is it relief and context, mislabelled?

I am not asking you to decide forever. I am asking you to do what a fair judge would do: put the claimed benefit on the table and see if it survives the three tests.

What FOR remains?

You put energy? It fails test one — it is relief from the low, then a crash. Borrowed.

You put treat? It fails test two — the warmth was the occasion, the rest, the self-kindness. Sugar sneaked a ride.

You put taste alone? It fails test two again — the company makes the taste.

Name one that passes. Name one benefit in the FOR column that is real, unique, and not borrowed from later.

Can you?

Take your time. Be honest.

We were trained from childhood to call BAD SUGAR a genuine pleasure or treat. That phrase was installed before we could question it. It felt true because we kept feeling the relief of taking the tight shoes off — and we called that relief pleasure.

But relief is not benefit.

Relief is the trap quietening.

### THE EMPTY COLUMN — WHERE THE WHOLE NEGOTIATION COLLAPSES

Let us land this with a clean image you can keep.

Picture the ledger after you have tried. Left column heavy. Right column… empty.

Not light. Empty.

Not "a bit of pleasure but not worth it." Empty.

Not "some benefits but we should cut down." Empty.

Empty means there is nothing TO weigh against harm. The scale trick — "harm versus benefit" — cannot even begin. You cannot outweigh a mountain with air.

Do you feel the defensiveness soften? This is the emotional turn this chapter exists to produce.

A moment ago you were calculating like a careful adult: "How much can I allow? How do I balance?" Calculation feels responsible. Now the table is bare on one side. Calculation is not responsible. It is absurd. You have been bargaining over a benefit that was never there.

How did we miss it?

Because we credited BAD SUGAR with what the moment gave us.

You had a break at three. You stretched. You drank water. You looked away from the screen. You laughed with a colleague. And you had a biscuit at the same time. Your brain, already brainwashed to call that biscuit a genuine pleasure or treat, tagged the whole relief "biscuit."

We do that a thousand times. We tag the company as chocolate. We tag the rest as sugar. We tag the warmth as the sweet. The ledger learns the lie.

The fact is, BAD SUGAR never takes you above the baseline of a person who never started. At best it drags you down, then briefly stops dragging. You call that lift. It is just return toward normal.

It is the other way around.

Now I want you to hear the leaving belief in one short verdict you can carry.

BAD SUGAR is doing plenty TO you. It is doing nothing FOR you.

That is the switch. Not harm bigger than good. No good.

Do you feel how different that is from "sugar is bad so I should give it up"? We never say give up. There is nothing to give up. There is a trap to escape. Escape is relief, not sacrifice. Freedom is immediate, not earned after days of resistance.

Let me test the new ledger with three questions whose only honest answers concede the point. Answer inwardly.

If BAD SUGAR provided genuine energy, why would you need another dose within the hour and feel you can't stop once you start?

If BAD SUGAR were a genuine pleasure or treat, why would the moment after feel like fast drop, unease and rule-making, not lasting satisfaction?

If a single sweet truly satisfied a real need, why would the normal you already exceed a well-known population ceiling — and still want more?

You know. You have lived the cycle. The lift is short. The drop is fast. The want returns. That is not a benefit that outweighs a cost. That is a cost manufacturing its own desire.

Some of you will think, "But I don't eat that much. I am not like the heavy user. I only have it once a week."

Good. Let us be precise even for you.

Moderate still scores TO hits — acid time, crash time, appetite bypass time — while the FOR column remains empty. Less frequent just means less often empty. It does not make empty into something. And you know from your own rules — "once a week is fine" creeping to twice, "I don't keep it in the house" followed by the shop — that moderation keeps the ledger alive in your mind, which is exactly where the trap wants it.

Others will think, "But I felt fine. My teeth are fine."

The left column's heaviest items are slow. The frog in warming water feels fine too. Fine was never the measure of free. Fine was the trap's quietest setting. And hygiene mediates teeth — good care lowers odds, but it does not make frequent free sugars do something FOR you. It only reduces how much they do TO you.

So where does this leave you?

Not with fear. Not with a vow of suffering. With clarity.

You began this chapter thinking, "Sugar may harm but the pleasure is worth it."

You leave it seeing, from your own attempts to name the pleasure and your own evidence that none survives, that BAD SUGAR does plenty TO you and nothing FOR you.

That phrase — doing TO you vs doing FOR you — is not a slogan. It is a tool. Use it the next time the thought surfaces, "Just one won't hurt." Ask, which column are you putting that in? Is that FOR me, or is that TO me wearing a mask?

Call the benefit by its trained name: a genuine pleasure or treat. Say it plainly in your head when the thought comes: "Ah. That is the a genuine pleasure or treat trick. That column is empty."

Watch what happens when you do. The calculation stops. The negotiation collapses. The desire has nowhere to stand, because you have removed the ground it stood on.

We will now demolish each claimed benefit one by one — energy, treat, taste, cope — and show, scene by scene, how BAD SUGAR was sneaking a ride. You do not need to believe that work ahead of time. You only need to keep this ledger clean.

For now, let this be enough:

The left column is heavy.

The right column is empty.

There is nothing to weigh. There is a trap to leave.

And you have absolutely nothing to lose by leaving it.
