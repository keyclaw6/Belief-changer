Chapter 9
TEN SECONDS UP, TWENTY MINUTES DOWN

IN THIS CHAPTER: yesterday from first light to last, the rush that arrives in seconds, the slide that follows it, three more lightings watched puff by puff, the hour you left unfed, the small itch underneath and the loud voice on top, and the two pictures that show the whole loop plain.

*The want never proved the cigarette was helping you — it only proved the last one was already wearing off.*

### YESTERDAY, PUFF BY PUFF

Let us walk yesterday again, not the good night and not the holiday, just the ordinary Tuesday you already lived with me, and count what actually happened inside your body from one lighting to the next.

You woke and you lit within minutes. You told yourself it steadied you for the day. You drew hard. The smoke went down hot. Within seconds something shifted. The edge softened. The head cleared a fraction. The hands stopped reaching. You called that the day beginning.

It was not the day beginning. It was a dose arriving where a want had been waiting all night.

Through the night you had taken nothing. The level in your blood had fallen hour by hour while you slept. You woke empty, with the faint gnaw already stirring under the ribs. You knew that stir before your feet found the floor. You checked the clock without deciding to check it. You thought about the pack on the chair and the lighter beside it and how long before you could stand outside. That checking and that faint gnaw came before the match, not after it. Remember the order, because the order is the whole proof. First the fall, then the call, then the lighting.

The first draw put nicotine into your lungs and into your blood and up to your brain in seconds, and the gnaw quieted because it was fed. Ten seconds up. That speed is the whole sales trick. Nothing else in your life relieves anything that fast, so you credit the thing that fast with power it does not have.

Watch the first cigarette the way you have never watched it, puff by puff, because you have lived this ten thousand times without ever watching it.

The first puff burns and bites. You cough a little and your eyes water. You draw again at once because the itch is loudest here after the whole night without. The second puff goes down deeper and the shoulders drop. The third puff brings the small clearing in the head, as if a window had been opened half an inch. You stand a little straighter. You tell yourself you can face the morning now. By the fourth and fifth puffs you are no longer really tasting anything. You are smoking it to its middle because that is what you do, drawing at the same pace, watching the end burn, feeling the lift already thinning even while you still hold it. By the last third it tastes sour and hot and you stub it with a small twist and a faint disgust you have learned not to notice. From lighting to stubbing, perhaps six minutes. From stubbing to quiet, perhaps twenty minutes more when you feel almost like yourself. Then the stir starts again, faint as a draught under a door.

Within minutes the lift is already thinning. Within the hour the gnaw is back. Not the stress of the day. Not your personality turning difficult. Just the dose leaving. The pleasurable effect fades fast, and as it fades it leaves the same empty feeling that sent you to light in the first place. So you light again to end the want the last one created. Then that one fades too. Then the next hour is already calling.

Count it cold. Morning to midnight, every hour or so, the same small ceremony. Reach. Light. Ten seconds of relief. Twenty minutes of quiet. Then the stir. Then the look at the clock. Then the next one. We lived that clock for years and we called it company. It was not company. It was a timetable we carried inside our own chests, wound up again with every match we struck.

You say, "But I felt it calm me. I felt the relief in my own body."

I believe you. I felt it too. The relief is real. The credit is false.

What calmed was not you being helped. What calmed was a want being briefly switched off that the previous dose had switched on. The cigarette takes credit for ending the discomfort it caused, the way a man who keeps tapping you on the shoulder takes credit for stopping when you pay him to stop. You never rose above how a non-smoker feels all day without thinking about it. You only climbed briefly back toward normal, then slid again, then paid again for the next short climb.

Does that really look like help?

When did a non-smoker need that hourly repair? When did you need it before you smoked at all? What exactly are you being given, if an hour later you need giving again?

Keep those questions with you while we walk the rest of yesterday together, because the morning alone could still let you argue that mornings are special. They are not special. The clock rings in every room.

### ELEVEN TWENTY IN THE CORRIDOR

It is half past ten and you have been at your desk for two hours. You have answered messages and sat through a call that ran long. Nothing terrible has happened. Nothing wonderful either. The work is ordinary and your eyes are tired from looking close.

At eleven twenty you push your chair back and you stand. Notice what moves first. It is not a thought. It is the hand toward the pocket and the eyes toward the clock. Twenty past eleven. An hour and forty minutes since the last one outside the shop before work. You do the small arithmetic without words. Will this meeting run over? How long before I can step out? You tell yourself you need air and a pause, and part of that is true, because you do need a pause. But watch the other part underneath it, the faint empty gnaw under the ribs, the slight restlessness in the fingers, the attention narrowing to the door and the stairs and the place where people stand.

You go down to the side door with two others. The air is cool. Someone laughs. You tap the pack and you put one between your lips and you light.

Puff by puff again, slowly this time, because I want you to feel the timing.

The first draw hits fast. The smoke is hot on the tongue and then there is the small loosening at the back of the neck within seconds. Ten seconds up. You breathe out and you feel the shoulders fall half an inch and you mistake that fall for the break doing you good. The second puff deepens it. The talk around you sounds warmer for a minute. The third and fourth puffs level you. You are not climbing higher now. You are only back to where you were an hour ago, back to almost normal, back to the state a non-smoker sat in through the whole meeting without noticing.

Stay with the next minutes, because this is where the trick hides.

By the fifth puff you are smoking on a level, not on a lift. The taste has already dulled. You are watching the end burn down and judging your calm by how much is left, the way we all learned to do. You draw a little harder near the filter to get the last of it, and it burns hotter and tastes bitter, and you stub it against the wall and drop it in the sand tray. You feel settled. You go back inside. You sit. For twenty minutes, perhaps thirty, you do not think about smoking at all. The work goes on. You answer another message. You drink some water.

Then, without any new trouble arriving, the attention narrows again. You glance at the corner of the screen where the time sits. Twelve forty. You shift in the chair. You think about lunch and who is going and whether you will have time after. The gnaw is back, faint, ignorable, but present, like a radio left on in another room. Nothing in the work changed. No new worry arrived. The only thing that changed is that the level fell again.

Tell me honestly, what did that cigarette give you that the corridor and the standing and the cool air did not already give? The legs stretched because you stood. The eyes rested because you looked far away. The mind loosened because you stopped stirring it for five minutes. The dose added one thing only, and it added it at speed. It switched off for twenty minutes the faint noise its own fading had switched on.

You lived that hour as need. It was schedule. The call arrived on the dose-fade timetable, not on the work timetable, and it wore work clothes so you would not see the clock inside it.

### HALF PAST THREE AT THE DESK

Eat lunch and come back and sit through the early afternoon, and you will see the same clock ring in a different room with a different excuse laid over it.

It is half past three. The plate has been cleared an hour ago. The room is warm. The light slants across the desk. Your eyelids feel heavy and your attention keeps sliding off the page. In the old story this is the low part of the day and the dose is the small ladder out of it. You check the time. Three thirty-five. Two hours since the quick one after lunch that you smoked half of while walking back. You feel the familiar tightening, the jaw a fraction tight, the breath a fraction shallow, the thought surfacing that you cannot quite settle to this next piece until you have stepped out.

You step out to the same side door. The afternoon air is softer. You light. You cup the flame though there is no wind, because the hands love the ritual.

First puff, harsh and hot after food and coffee. You cough once. Second puff, deeper, and within seconds the head feels a touch clearer and the heaviness lifts half a degree. Ten seconds up again. Exactly as fast as this morning. Exactly as fast as late morning. Different street, different sky, same seconds. You think, there, I can think again. Third puff and fourth puff and you feel yourself return to yourself. Fifth puff and you are already past the peak and smoking the middle out of habit, looking at your phone, half listening to the traffic, feeling the calm you prize begin to thin even before you stub.

Go back inside and time what happens, because you never timed it while you believed in it.

For perhaps twenty minutes you feel level. You get through two short tasks and you feel almost brisk. Then the level starts its small slide. By half past four the briskness is gone and the heaviness is back exactly where it was, plus the faint gnaw on top of it. You tell yourself the afternoon is long and dull and no wonder you want one. But the non-smoker at the next desk lived the same warm room and the same slanting light and never felt that extra layer, that clock-check, that small debt collecting under the ribs. You feel it because your level fell, not because the afternoon failed you.

Ask yourself what the dose did to the afternoon, puff by puff and minute by minute. Did it remove the natural dip that every body feels at half past three, or did it add a second dip on top and then briefly rent you back the first? You know from your own afternoons that one never held the day level. It held you level for twenty minutes and then handed you back the same afternoon with interest to pay. That is why the afternoons always needed another and then another. Not because afternoons are unlivable. Because each feeding set the timer for the next flag.

We all told ourselves we smoked more on heavy days and less on easy days, as if the day were in charge. Watch yesterday cold and you see the opposite. Heavy morning, light morning, busy afternoon, slow afternoon, the call arrived about every hour whatever the day contained. It arrived in meetings and out of them. It arrived when you were lonely and when you were laughing. It arrives on dose-fade schedule in every situation, and it borrows the clothes of whatever situation it finds so you will blame the situation and praise the dose.

### NINE FIFTEEN ON THE STEP

Come home now and eat and sit through the evening, and you will see the clock follow you through the front door and sit with you at your own table.

It is quarter past nine. Dinner is done. The plates are in the sink. The talk has gone quiet in a good way. You step onto the back step with the pack, because the evening dose is the most rehearsed one of all, the one you once called earned and deserved. The air is cool on your face. The garden is dark at the edges. You light and the flame lights your hands for a second.

Before you lit, mark the order again. You cleared the plates and you felt the settled fullness of having eaten enough. Then, as the fullness settled, the other feeling rose beside it, the faint empty gnaw, the thought of the step and the lighter and the first draw that feels like a sigh. You checked the clock on the oven. Nine fifteen. An hour and a half since the one before dinner that you smoked while the food cooked. Nothing is wrong. The house is warm. The day is done. The want arrived on time, not on trouble.

Light it and watch it puff by puff one last time.

First draw, deep, down to the shoes. Within seconds the shoulders drop and the head softens. Ten seconds up. You breathe out into the dark and you feel the day tie itself with a ribbon. Second draw and the ribbon tightens. You think, this is the moment the day is for. Third and fourth draws and you sit back and you look at the sky and you feel almost tender toward the small burning stick. Fifth draw and sixth and you notice you are no longer rising. You are maintaining. You are keeping the quiet from sliding while you hold it. The last third tastes acrid and you draw harder to finish it properly, and you stub it and you sit for a minute in the true quiet after.

Keep sitting, because the evening will show you the leaving even more plainly than the arriving.

For twenty minutes you feel complete. You go inside and you sit and you talk a little more or you read a page. Then the completeness starts to fray at the edge. Half past ten. You think about whether there is time for one more before bed. You count what is left in the pack. You do the small arithmetic about the morning and the shop. You tell yourself the evening needs rounding off. The evening was round before you lit. What needs rounding off is the level falling again inside you.

That is four lightings in one ordinary day, morning and late morning and afternoon and evening, different places, different people, different moods, same seconds up, same minutes down, same clock-check and same gnaw before each. Work did not order them. Food did not order them. Night did not order them. The fading ordered them. The call arrives on dose-fade schedule in every situation, and one unfed interval would prove it beyond any argument, so let us look at one you already lived.

### THE HOUR YOU DID NOT FEED

You have lived unfed hours. Every smoker has. The long film you did not want to interrupt. The train that ran two hours late with no stop. The meeting that ran over with the door closed. The flight. The morning in the waiting room where you could not step out. Remember one of yours now in detail, because it holds the proof the trap most fears.

Mine was a morning appointment that swallowed three hours. I arrived with three in the pack and the usual gnaw already stirring. I expected misery. I expected the gnaw to grow teeth and become unbearable. I expected to be unable to think of anything else.

Here is what actually happened, and check it against your own memory.

For the first forty minutes I noticed it. The faint empty feeling under the ribs. The thought surfacing that I would light the second I got out. I shifted in the chair. I glanced at the clock. Then the appointment began and my attention was taken, and for the next hour I forgot to check whether it was still there. When I remembered again, near the end of the second hour, it had not grown. It had thinned. It was a faint stir, ignorable, background, less than hunger before lunch, less than thirst. I walked out into the daylight and I lit out of habit and the first puff tasted foul and too strong, and I saw in that second that the three hours had not been endured by force. They had passed on their own while the little itch starved a little and the day carried me.

You know this hour from your own life. The cinema where you forgot halfway through. The long walk with someone you love where the talk took you and two hours went without a check. The night you slept through and woke gnawing but alive. If the want were mighty need, it would grow while unfed until it mastered you. It does not grow. Left unfed for one interval, it leaves only a faint stir. That is not how need behaves. That is how a small dying noise behaves when the loud voice on top of it has nothing to feed on for a while.

Hold that hour beside the four lightings. Fed every hour, the call returns every hour, sharp and seemingly urgent. Left unfed for one stretch, the call softens instead of swelling. The difference is not your character on the two days. The difference is whether you reset the timer. Each dose you feed sets the next call. Each interval you leave unfed lets the level settle and the noise thin. Once you see that, the fear that you will feel this gnaw forever unless you feed it turns upside down. You feel it forever only while you keep feeding it.

You fear the craving proves you need it. The craving proves the last one wore off, and one unfed interval proves how little wearing off truly means.

### THE MAN WHO HOLDS YOU UNDER

I want to show you what that loop looks like from the outside, because from the inside it still wears the face of a friend.

Picture a swimming pool on a hot afternoon. You are in the deep end, laughing, holding the side. A big cheerful man in a white shirt stands above you. He is the lifeguard. He smiles at you. Everybody trusts him.

Without warning he leans down and pushes your head under the water and holds it there. You thrash. Your chest burns. You panic. Just as your lungs are about to burst, he hauls you back up into the air. You gasp. The air rushes in. It is the sweetest breath of your life. You cling to the side and you gulp and you thank him from your heart for saving you.

Then he pushes you under again.

Again you struggle. Again he pulls you up at the last second. Again that wonderful gasp. Again the gratitude. After a while you cannot imagine the poolside without him. Who else gives you that marvellous feeling of air rushing back? Who else is there at the exact second you need him?

Now tell me plainly. Is he rescuing you or is he drowning you in instalments?

You know the answer before your mind has finished the sentence. He creates the panic and sells you the relief. He is the danger and the rescue in one shirt. Your gratitude is real and it is completely conned. Take his hand off the back of your head and you do not need his rescue at all. You can swim. You could always swim. The air was free before he arrived.

That is what each dose does. It pushes you under by letting the level fall and the gnaw return, then it pulls you up for ten seconds and takes your thanks. You felt rescued because you were suffocating, and you were suffocating because you had been pushed. The hand that sells you each breath is the hand that pushed your head under in the first place.

We were taught to love that hand. Everyone around us loved it. Our friends loved it. The faces on the posters loved it. The older boys at school who blew rings loved it. The films where the tired hero lights and thinks clearly loved it. Year after year the same lesson was laid over us until we could not feel the small gnaw without hearing the lesson speak. That lesson is the brainwashing, and its installers were not evil in our eyes at the time. They were family and friends and screens and shops on every corner, all repeating what they themselves had been taught. Do not hate them. See what they handed you. They handed you a lifeguard who holds you under, and taught you to call him a friend.

Once you see him, you cannot unsee him. Every ten-second lift changes its meaning. It is not a gift. It is a pause in a drowning the gift-giver arranged. And a pause you have to pay for twenty times a day, with your breath and your money and your hours outside in the rain, is not a genuine pleasure or crutch. It is a rental on air you already owned.

It is the other way around.

### THE METER ON YOUR OWN CAR

There is a second picture, duller and closer to daily life, and I want you to carry them both because together they leave the loop nowhere to hide.

You buy a fine car. You love it. It runs well. You drive it to work and to the shops and to see people you love. A week later you find a grey metal box bolted to the dashboard that was not there in the showroom. It has a slot in it and a little red flag. A man in a suit knocks on your window and tells you pleasantly that the company that sold you the car installed the box for your convenience. Every forty minutes the flag pops up and the engine starts to cough. You feed the slot and the cough stops. That is all it does. It does not make the car faster. It does not make the journey prettier. It only stops the cough its own timer created.

You would laugh him off the forecourt. Then you would tear the box out. Then you would ask why you were sold a car with a meter on it.

But that is the bargain we all signed without reading it. The car is your body. The journeys are your days. The box is the dependence, fitted by the industry that sold you the sticks, and the only coin it takes is nicotine. You feed it because the cough feels worse than the feeding. You feed it on waking and after meals and in work breaks and on station platforms, and you tell yourself you enjoy feeding it. You do not enjoy feeding it. You enjoy the cough stopping for twenty minutes.

Look at the arithmetic the way you would look at that dashboard.

You feed the meter to stop the flag. The feeding sets the timer for the next flag. The next flag demands the next feeding. There is no journey in that cycle that the meter improves. The coffee tastes worse while you feed it. The break is shorter while you feed it. The evening is cut in half while you feed it. The meter adds nothing to the car. It only rents you back the smooth running you had before it was fitted.

"But the craving is so strong," you say. "If it is only a meter, why does it feel like me? Why does it feel like need?"

That question is the fear that keeps the whole of the nicotine trap alive, so let us answer it fully and put it down for good.

There are two things happening inside you when the flag pops up, and they are not the same size at all, though they speak at the same volume.

The first is tiny. It is a small physical itch, an empty gnaw, a faint restlessness under the ribs when the level falls. Nicotine changes how the brain works so the call can go on for a while even when you want to stop, and you learn to need a little more for the same quiet as the years pass. That is true. I do not deny a cell of it. Men in laboratories can measure that ten-second arrival and that fast fade. They can watch the level peak and fall. But listen to what else they tell you when you read them plain. The physical part is small and it is already dying from the moment you stop feeding it. It peaks in the first days and it thins within weeks. It is not torture. It is not a new personality. It is a little creature starving to death, and its death throes are a faint grumble you can barely find if you are living your day whole. Your own unfed three hours proved it. Left alone for one interval, it softened to a faint stir. Imagine what it does when you leave it alone for good and stop resetting its clock.

The second is loud. It is the voice that says the grumble means you cannot cope, cannot think, cannot enjoy, cannot be yourself. That voice is not chemistry. That is the brainwashing talking, the years of lessons from every side that taught you the gnaw means light up. That voice is the true master, and it borrows the tiny itch as its evidence. Every time the little itch stirs, the big voice shouts, you see, you need it, you will feel like this for the rest of your life unless you feed it. And because the feeding does quiet the itch for twenty minutes, the voice sounds proved. The lifeguard pulls you up and you thank him. The meter stops coughing and you praise the coin.

Separate them once and the fear ends.

The itch is small and dying. The voice is loud and lying. The itch you can barely feel by Thursday if you leave it unfed and live your ordinary hours complete. The voice you can answer the way you would answer any conned thought once you see the con. There it goes again, trying to spend my coin. I am not drowning. I am only hearing the man in the white shirt telling me the water is safer with his hand on my head.

Which one would you obey, once you see them side by side? A faint empty stirring that fades on its own, or a loud old lesson installed by people who profited from your obedience?

Ask yourself honestly and the answer comes before the sentence ends.

I know this because I lived the clock and I watched it stop. I know the first morning without the lighting feels strange for an hour and ordinary by lunch. I know the late-morning pause feels odd once and then feels larger than you remembered, because no flag pops up to cut it. I know the half past three dip comes and goes like weather and leaves you clear on the other side. I know the nine fifteen step calls once like a habit and then the evening stays finished. None of that is brave. None of that is hard in the way we feared. It is only unfamiliar for a day, the way any metered dashboard feels unfamiliar the first morning after you tear the box out, and then you drive and forget to check the place where the flag used to pop up.

So do not change from fear of what the itch might do. Change from seeing what the loop is. The itch is a starving whisper. The voice is an old recording. The relief you praised was only the ending of a want the last dose created. Seen plain, there is almost nothing physical to face and nothing real to miss. The water is shallow. The hand is lifted. The road runs smooth without the coin.

### SUMMARY

- I now see that the fast relief was only replenishment of a fading dose arriving on schedule in every hour, and I want no more of the loop that sells it.
- I now know that the physical itch is small and dying while the loud voice is old teaching, and I hear the difference between them.
- I now feel that one unfed interval leaves only a faint stir, and I trust the quiet that follows when nothing is fed.
