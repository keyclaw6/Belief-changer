Chapter 9
TEN SECONDS UP, TWENTY MINUTES DOWN

IN THIS CHAPTER: yesterday from first light to last, the rush that arrives in seconds, the slide that follows it, the small itch underneath and the loud voice on top, and the two pictures that show the whole loop plain.

*The want never proved the cigarette was helping you — it only proved the last one was already wearing off.*

### YESTERDAY, PUFF BY PUFF

Let us walk yesterday again, not the good night and not the holiday, just the ordinary Tuesday you already lived with me, and count what actually happened inside your body from one lighting to the next.

You woke and you lit within minutes. You told yourself it steadied you for the day. You drew hard. The smoke went down hot. Within seconds something shifted. The edge softened. The head cleared a fraction. The hands stopped reaching. You called that the day beginning.

It was not the day beginning. It was a dose arriving where a want had been waiting all night.

Through the night you had taken nothing. The level in your blood had fallen hour by hour while you slept. You woke empty, with the faint gnaw already stirring under the ribs. The first draw put nicotine into your lungs and into your blood and up to your brain in seconds, and the gnaw quieted because it was fed. Ten seconds up. That speed is the whole sales trick. Nothing else in your life relieves anything that fast, so you credit the thing that fast with power it does not have.

Watch what happens next, because you have lived this ten thousand times without ever watching it.

Within minutes the lift is already thinning. Within the hour the gnaw is back. Not the stress of the day. Not your personality turning difficult. Just the dose leaving. The pleasurable effect fades fast, and as it fades it leaves the same empty feeling that sent you to light in the first place. So you light again to end the want the last one created. Then that one fades too. Then the next hour is already calling.

Count it cold. Morning to midnight, every hour or so, the same small ceremony. Reach. Light. Ten seconds of relief. Twenty minutes of quiet. Then the stir. Then the look at the clock. Then the next one. We lived that clock for years and we called it company. It was not company. It was a timetable we carried inside our own chests, wound up again with every match we struck.

You say, "But I felt it calm me. I felt the relief in my own body."

I believe you. I felt it too. The relief is real. The credit is false.

What calmed was not you being helped. What calmed was a want being briefly switched off that the previous dose had switched on. The cigarette takes credit for ending the discomfort it caused, the way a man who keeps tapping you on the shoulder takes credit for stopping when you pay him to stop. You never rose above how a non-smoker feels all day without thinking about it. You only climbed briefly back toward normal, then slid again, then paid again for the next short climb.

Does that really look like help?

When did a non-smoker need that hourly repair? When did you need it before you smoked at all? What exactly are you being given, if an hour later you need giving again?

That is the loop seen cold. Ten seconds up. Twenty minutes down. The rest is paying for the lift.

### THE MAN WHO HOLDS YOU UNDER

I want to show you what that loop looks like from the outside, because from the inside it still wears the face of a friend.

Picture a swimming pool on a hot afternoon. You are in the deep end, laughing, holding the side. A big cheerful man in a white shirt stands above you. He is the lifeguard. He smiles at you. Everybody trusts him.

Without warning he leans down and pushes your head under the water and holds it there. You thrash. Your chest burns. You panic. Just as your lungs are about to burst, he hauls you back up into the air. You gasp. The air rushes in. It is the sweetest breath of your life. You cling to the side and you gulp and you thank him from your heart for saving you.

Then he pushes you under again.

Again you struggle. Again he pulls you up at the last second. Again that wonderful gasp. Again the gratitude. After a while you cannot imagine the poolside without him. Who else gives you that marvellous feeling of air rushing back? Who else is there at the exact second you need him?

Now tell me plainly. Is he rescuing you or is he drowning you in instalments?

You know the answer before your mind has finished the sentence. He creates the panic and sells you the relief. He is the danger and the rescue in one shirt. Your gratitude is real and it is completely conned. Take his hand off the back of your head and you do not need his rescue at all. You can swim. You could always swim. The air was free before he arrived.

That is what each dose does. It pushes you under by letting the level fall and the gnaw return, then it pulls you up for ten seconds and takes your thanks. You felt rescued because you were suffocating, and you were suffocating because you had been pushed. The hand that sells you each breath is the hand that pushed your head under in the first place.

We were taught to love that hand. Everyone around us loved it. Our friends loved it. The faces on the posters loved it. The older boys at school who blew rings loved it. The films where the tired hero lights and thinks clearly loved it. Year after year the same lesson was laid over us until we could not feel the small gnaw without hearing the lesson speak: this means you need one. That lesson is the brainwashing, and its installers were not evil in our eyes at the time. They were family and friends and screens and shops on every corner, all repeating what they themselves had been taught. Do not hate them. See what they handed you. They handed you a lifeguard who holds you under, and taught you to call him a friend.

Once you see him, you cannot unsee him. Every ten-second lift changes its meaning. It is not a gift. It is a pause in a drowning the gift-giver arranged. And a pause you have to pay for twenty times a day, with your breath and your money and your hours outside in the rain, is not a genuine pleasure or crutch. It is a rental on air you already owned.

It is the other way around.

### THE METER ON YOUR OWN CAR

There is a second picture, duller and closer to daily life, and I want you to carry them both because together they leave the loop nowhere to hide.

You buy a fine car. You love it. It runs well. You drive it to work and to the shops and to see people you love. A week later you find a grey metal box bolted to the dashboard that was not there in the showroom. It has a slot in it and a little red flag. A man in a suit knocks on your window and tells you pleasantly that the company that sold you the car installed the box for your convenience. Every forty minutes the flag pops up and the engine starts to cough. You feed the slot and the cough stops. That is all it does. It does not make the car faster. It does not make the journey prettier. It only stops the cough its own timer created.

You would laugh him off the forecourt. Then you would tear the box out. Then you would ask why you were sold a car with a meter on it.

But that is the bargain we all signed without reading it. The car is your body. The journeys are your days. The box is the dependence, fitted by the industry that sold you the sticks, and the only coin it takes is nicotine. You feed it because the cough feels worse than the feeding. You feed it on waking and after meals and in work breaks and on station platforms, and you tell yourself you enjoy feeding it. You do not enjoy feeding it. You enjoy the cough stopping for twenty minutes.

Look at the arithmetic the way you would look at that dashboard.

You feed the meter to stop the flag. The feeding sets the timer for the next flag. The next flag demands the next feeding. There is no journey in that cycle that the meter improves. The coffee tastes worse while you feed it. The break is shorter while you feed it. The evening is cut in half while you feed it. The meter adds nothing to the car. It only rents you back the smooth running you had before it was fitted.

"But the craving is so strong," you say. "If it is only a meter, why does it feel like me? Why does it feel like need?"

That question is the fear that keeps the whole of the nicotine trap alive, so let us answer it fully and put it down for good.

There are two things happening inside you when the flag pops up, and they are not the same size at all, though they speak at the same volume.

The first is tiny. It is a small physical itch, an empty gnaw, a faint restlessness under the ribs when the level falls. Nicotine changes how the brain works so the call can go on for a while even when you want to stop, and you learn to need a little more for the same quiet as the years pass. That is true. I do not deny a cell of it. Men in laboratories can measure that ten-second arrival and that fast fade. They can watch the level peak and fall. But listen to what else they tell you when you read them plain. The physical part is small and it is already dying from the moment you stop feeding it. It peaks in the first days and it thins within weeks. It is not torture. It is not a new personality. It is a little creature starving to death, and its death throes are a faint grumble you can barely find if you are living your day whole.

The second is loud. It is the voice that says the grumble means you cannot cope, cannot think, cannot enjoy, cannot be yourself. That voice is not chemistry. That is the brainwashing talking, the years of lessons from every side that taught you the gnaw means light up. That voice is the true master, and it borrows the tiny itch as its evidence. Every time the little itch stirs, the big voice shouts, you see, you need it, you will feel like this for the rest of your life unless you feed it. And because the feeding does quiet the itch for twenty minutes, the voice sounds proved. The lifeguard pulls you up and you thank him. The meter stops coughing and you praise the coin.

Separate them once and the fear ends.

The itch is small and dying. The voice is loud and lying. The itch you can barely feel by Thursday if you leave it unfed and live your ordinary hours complete. The voice you can answer the way you would answer any conned thought once you see the con. There it goes again, trying to spend my coin. I am not drowning. I am only hearing the man in the white shirt telling me the water is safer with his hand on my head.

Which one would you obey, once you see them side by side? A faint empty stirring that fades on its own, or a loud old lesson installed by people who profited from your obedience?

Ask yourself honestly and the answer comes before the sentence ends.

### WHY MORE NEVER MEANT ENOUGH

There is one more proof, and it lives in your own history, so no one can argue you out of it.

Think back to the beginning. One on a Friday. One at a farewell. Three on New Year's Eve. Then five at a birthday. Then a pack on holiday. Then a pack a day without noticing when daily became normal.

If each dose gave you something real, why did the dose have to grow? If the first ones were genuine pleasure, why did genuine pleasure need doubling and doubling to stand still? A true gift does not behave like that. Food does not demand twice the food for half the taste. Music does not demand twice the music for half the hearing. Only a trap behaves like that, because tolerance means more is needed for the same brief quiet, and the quiet keeps getting shorter while the price keeps getting longer.

We told ourselves we smoked more because life got harder, because work got heavier, because the children came, because the bills came. Life did get harder in places and lighter in others, the way lives do. But the ladder climbed in good years and bad years alike. It climbed on holiday. It climbed at parties. It climbed when everything was wonderful. It climbed because the mechanism climbs whatever your life does. The more you fed the itch, the sooner it asked again. The sooner it asked, the more you believed you needed it. The circle tightened while we called the tightening proof.

That is why the fear you carry now gets the whole thing backwards.

You fear that without the dose you will feel the gnaw forever. The truth is you feel the gnaw forever only while you keep feeding it. You fear the craving proves you need it. The craving proves the last one wore off. You fear you will have to endure something mighty with a clenched jaw. What you will feel is something small fading, with a loud old voice making a last noise over it, and the noise stops being frightening the moment you know whose voice it is.

I know this because I lived the clock and I watched it stop. I know the first morning without the lighting feels strange for an hour and ordinary by lunch. I know the ten o'clock pause feels odd once and then feels larger than you remembered. I know the after-dinner moment calls once like a habit and then the dinner stays finished. None of that is brave. None of that is hard in the way we feared. It is only unfamiliar for a day, the way any metered dashboard feels unfamiliar the first morning after you tear the box out, and then you drive and forget to check the place where the flag used to pop up.

So do not change from fear of what the itch might do. Change from seeing what the loop is. The itch is a starving whisper. The voice is an old recording. The relief you praised was only the ending of a want the last dose created. Seen plain, there is almost nothing physical to face and nothing real to miss. The water is shallow. The hand is lifted. The road runs smooth without the coin.

### SUMMARY

- I now see that the fast relief was only replenishment of a fading dose, and I want no more of the loop that sells it.
- I now know that the physical itch is small and dying while the loud voice is old teaching, and I hear the difference between them.
- I now feel that craving proves the last dose wore off rather than proving need, and I trust the quiet that follows when nothing is fed.
