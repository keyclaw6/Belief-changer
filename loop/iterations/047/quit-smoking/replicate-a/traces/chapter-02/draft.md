Chapter 2
CONNED, NOT CHOOSING

IN THIS CHAPTER: the morning reach, the stairs, and the clock that calls itself company.

*You never chose this freely; you were tricked into a loop, and your own body has been telling you ever since.*

### THE REACH BEFORE THE FEET

Let me start where your day starts, because your body tells the truth before your mind has time to dress it up.

It is early. The room is still grey. You are not yet awake enough to think a clear thought. You cough, a short rough cough that rattles low in the chest, and before your feet have found the floor your hand is already moving toward the pack on the bedside table. You do not decide. You do not weigh anything. The hand moves. The lighter clicks. The first pull burns and you cough again, and then you take another pull to settle the cough from the first.

We all know that reach. We have all lived it. The eyes half closed. The throat dry. The mind still fogged with sleep and already counting: where is the pack, is there enough for the morning, will I need to stop on the way to work. The day has not begun and the schedule has already begun.

Watch yourself closely tomorrow morning, not to judge, only to notice. Notice the order. Cough first, then reach. Unease first, then the dose. You have not yet faced a single demand of the day. No bad news has arrived. No work has pressed on you. Yet the want is already there, sharp and specific, pointing at one object only.

Ask yourself a plain question. If you smoke because you choose to, why does the choosing happen before you are awake enough to choose anything? You choose your shirt when you stand up. You choose your breakfast when you reach the kitchen. But this choosing happened in the dark, with your eyes shut, driven by a pull you did not invite.

Ask yourself a second question. If the dose gives you something, why does the morning dose taste so foul? You know the taste I mean. Stale mouth, burning throat, a heaviness that sits behind the eyes rather than lifting it. You would never call that taste good if you met it for the first time today. You call it necessary because you have learned to call it so.

And ask yourself a third question. If you are in charge, who set the timetable? Because there is a timetable. You know it by heart. The morning chain. The mid-morning break. The after-meal dose. The afternoon dip. The evening round. Hour by hour, the same small itch returns and calls it time. You do not consult a clock to know. The want rings and you answer.

That is not choice. That is a loop.

I say this with warmth, because I lived inside the same loop for years. I told myself the same story you tell yourself. Nobody holds my arm. Nobody lights it for me. I buy the packs with my own money. I step outside with my own legs. How can a thing I do with my own hands not be my choice?

It can, when the information was false from the start and the loop was built to hide itself.

Think of any other purchase you make freely. You might buy a coat because it keeps you warm. The coat keeps you warm. You might buy bread because it feeds you. The bread feeds you. The benefit arrives and stays. You do not need to buy the same coat again an hour later because the warmth wore off and left you colder than before. You do not wake in the night craving bread to get back to normal.

With cigarettes the pattern runs backward. The unease comes first, the dose follows, the unease lifts for a few minutes, then the unease returns a little stronger. Round and round. We learned to call each brief lift a gift and to ignore the circle that carries it.

You walked into the nicotine trap long before you knew it had a name.

Read that sentence again, because it carries the whole chapter. Not a habit you weighed. Not a pleasure you selected from a menu. A trap with a working mechanism, entered on false information, kept alive because the mechanism points at itself as the cure.

Does that feel true even slightly? You do not have to accept it yet. Only keep it beside the morning reach and watch which one explains the other.

### THE STAIRS DON'T ARGUE

The body keeps records the mind tries to overwrite.

Picture the stairs you climb most often. At work. At the station. At home with shopping in both hands. You remember when you took them without thought. Now there is a calculation. How many flights. Whether the lift is near. How to hide the breathlessness at the top by pausing to check your phone.

I know that calculation because I made it daily. Two flights and the chest tightens. Three flights and the legs feel heavy. A laugh that turns into a cough. A cold that settles and stays for weeks while the non-smokers around you shake theirs in days. The morning throat that needs clearing again and again. The smell in the hair and the coat that you notice when you step back inside from the cold and hope others do not.

We tell ourselves these are small prices. We tell ourselves everyone gets breathless. We tell ourselves the cough means nothing. Yet we also plan around them. We park nearer. We choose the table nearer the door. We carry mints and spray and a spare shirt in the car for the long drive. The planning gives us away. A free choice needs no cover story.

Stand on those stairs with me for a moment in your mind. Feel the chest working a little too hard for the effort. Feel the heart knocking a little too fast. Feel the small voice that says, I should not feel like this for two flights. That voice is not your enemy. That voice is your body reporting plainly, without slogans, what the doses cost.

Now set beside that reporting the story we were taught to repeat: I choose this. I like this. This steadies me.

Can both be true? Can a thing you freely choose leave you breathless on stairs you once ran up, and still be called a free gift? If a friend ate a food he chose freely and every morning he coughed and every staircase winded him and every hour he needed another bite to feel normal, would you say, well, at least he chooses it? Or would you say, something has him, and he calls the holding by the name of choosing to keep his pride intact?

I understand that pride, because it was mine. Smokers are not weak people. We are stubborn, independent people who do not like being told what to do. The idea that we were conned offends us more than the cough does. So we hold tighter to the word choice. Choice sounds strong. Choice sounds like an adult. Choice lets us say to the doctor, to the partner, to the child who wrinkles her nose at the smell on our coat, mind your own business, this is mine.

I am not taking your strength away. I am showing you where it went. Your strength was used against you. The loop borrows your pride and makes it guard the very thing that rents your breath.

Look at the bargain as it is: winded, chained and poorer, every single day.

Breath short on the stairs. Bondage to the hourly ring. Money leaving the pocket in a steady stream for something that leaves only ash. That is not an insult. That is a description. Say it without flinching, because flinching keeps us from seeing. Winded in the body. Chained to the timetable. Poorer by thousands across the years for the privilege of staying in the same place.

If that were a choice made with clear eyes, you would have chosen differently long ago. You did not choose it with clear eyes. You chose the first ones on false information, and the loop did the rest.

### WHO TAUGHT YOU TO CALL THIS CHOICE

Let me take you back to the first ones, because the beginning exposes the con better than any argument.

Nobody wakes one morning with a physical hunger for smoke. No child dreams of inhaling burning paper. The want did not exist before the doses began. What existed was a picture: grown, tough, funny, part of the group, calm under pressure, interesting in the doorway with the light held to the face. We wanted the picture, not the smoke.

Remember your own first attempts honestly. The taste was foul. The throat burned. The head spun. Many of us coughed hard, felt sick, went pale. Some went home and promised never again, then tried again the next Friday because the group was there and we did not want to be the one who stepped back.

"Nobody forces me. I choose to smoke."

I hear that sentence often, and I hear the pride inside it. Let me answer it with care, because you deserve care here, not contempt. Who forced you to persist past the nausea? Not a hand on your arm. A picture in your head, placed there by everyone around you and by men in offices who understood exactly what they were doing. The picture said, this is how you belong. This is how you look grown. This is how you handle a drink, a break, a late night. You believed the picture because you were young or new or lonely or wanting to be liked, which is to say because you were human.

Then something else took over, quiet and chemical. The body learned to expect the dose. Tolerance arrived, which is a plain word for a grim process: what made you dizzy at first stopped making you dizzy, and soon the absence of the dose began to feel like something missing. The endurance turned into demand. What you once suffered to look part of the group, you now sought to quiet a want you did not have before you started.

The fact is, starters begin for image and staying power among friends, not from a built-in hunger, and the first experiences are often unpleasant until tolerance teaches the body to ask for more. That is not my opinion. That is how the recruitment was understood by the very people who profited from it. The cigarette was never sold to you as what it is. It was sold wrapped in company, humour, toughness, calm. The picture brought you in. The dose kept you in.

So when you say, I choose, ask what you are defending. Are you defending the taste, which you hated at first? Are you defending the smell, which you wash off before hugging your children? Are you defending the price, which you complain about every time it rises? Or are you defending the right not to feel pushed around, which is a fine right aimed at the wrong target?

If you truly chose, you could truly lay the pack on the table tonight and feel nothing. No pull. No voice listing reasons to keep one in reserve. No sudden memory of the after-dinner dose arriving uninvited. Try that thought on honestly. Does the idea of never lighting another feel neutral, like the idea of never riding a unicycle? Or does it stir a small panic, a quick inventory of hard moments ahead and how you would face them without the prop? That panic is not choice speaking. That is the loop speaking in your voice.

We were not foolish to fall for the picture. We were young in smoking years even when we were grown in other years, and the picture was everywhere: in films, on walls, in the hands of people we admired, in the doorway where the interesting talk happened. To be conned is not to be stupid. A con works precisely because the person conned is acting reasonably on false information. Give a smart person a false map and she will walk the wrong road with perfect logic.

Once you see the false map, you do not need to blame the walker. You change the map.

That change has already started. You came to this book willing to investigate. You hold three small agreements from our first chapter. You carry the knowledge that past attempts proved the method wrong, not you. You have nothing to lose and everything to gain by letting the next piece fall into place.

### THE PACKAGE, NOT THE PRODUCT

Now look at the object itself without the picture around it.

Hold a cigarette in your mind as if you had never seen one before. White tube. Filter. A leaf shredded and dried. A paper wrapper. A smell when lit that clings to curtains and hair. A taste that bites. If this were sold honestly for its taste and smell alone, as a flavour to enjoy like chocolate or coffee, how many people would persist past the first foul encounter? You know the answer from your own throat. Almost none.

The fact is, the cigarette was conceived by its makers not as a product to taste but as a package to deliver a dose. The tube and the paper and the filter and the flavour are the wrapping. The product inside the wrapping is nicotine. The wrapping exists to make the delivery quick, repeatable, and easy to learn. The flavour exists to mask the harshness enough that you keep inhaling until the dose arrives.

I want you to feel the weight of that inversion, because it dissolves the romance without a single scold. You thought you were buying taste, company, calm, a small pleasure to mark the hour. What was being sold, dose by dose, was a rapid hit to the brain through the lungs, fading fast, calling for another. The ceremony around it — the tap of the pack, the flick of the lighter, the first pull held a second longer — is the ribbon on the package. The business is the dose.

Does that match your experience? Think of the doses you barely tasted. The one smoked in the rain while you sheltered the tip with your hand, tasting mostly wet paper. The one smoked while arguing, while driving, while waiting for a text, where you looked down surprised to find it burned to the filter without you noticing. If taste were the point, those doses would not count. Yet they counted fully. They quieted the itch exactly as the slow savoured ones did. The itch does not care about flavour. The itch cares about timing.

Think of the ones you call habit smokes, the ones you light because coffee arrived, because the phone rang, because the meal ended, because the car started. You light them the way you answer a bell. The bell rings, the hand moves. A pleasure you truly chose would vary with mood and menu. This one fires with the clock, whatever your mood, whatever the menu. That is delivery, not delight.

And here is the plain logic that follows. If the object is a delivery device for a dose, then talk of brands and strengths and flavours is talk of ribbons. Smokers switch to lighter colours or milder names hoping for a smaller bargain and find the same hourly ring. They pull harder without knowing it. They hold longer. They smoke a little more of each one. The body seeks its dose through whatever hole is offered. Punch a hole in a mask to breathe easier and you will pull deeper through the hole and draw more through it, not less. So with the milder package. The package changes. The demand stays.

You do not need to study this. You have lived it. You have tried the lighter kind and found yourself smoking them the same way, or more. You have told yourself you would smoke fewer today and found the evening count unchanged. That is not failure of character. That is a delivery system doing its job on a body it trained.

See the object plainly and the word choice thins a little more. You did not select a flavour the way you select a meal. You were taught a ceremony that delivers a dose, and the ceremony borrowed its charm from every good moment it ever stood beside.

### TWO QUIET PARTS THAT KEEP THE LOOP ALIVE

The loop has two quiet parts, and it helps to name them plainly so we can speak of them without fog.

The first is small and physical. Call it the tiny nicotine itch. A faint empty feeling when the last dose fades. A slight edginess. A sense that something is missing without a name. It is real. I do not deny it. But it is tiny, far smaller than hunger, far smaller than tiredness, the kind of stir you would ignore in any other context. Left unfed, it peaks and passes within days as the body returns to its own baseline. It has no malice. It is a brief complaint from a body that learned to expect a dose.

The second is loud and learned. Call it the brainwashing voice. The lifelong patter that interprets every itch as proof you need the dose for life itself. Stress means light one. Coffee means light one. A finished job means light one. Boredom means light one. Company means light one. The voice did not arrive with your birth. It was installed, line by line, by the picture around smoking and by years of misreading the lift that follows each dose as help rather than as the brief quieting of the itch the previous dose created.

Hold both names lightly, as vocabulary, not as a lesson to memorise. The tiny itch and the brainwashing voice. One in the body, brief. One in the mind, long. The first you will starve without a fight by simply not feeding it again once belief has changed. The second is the true work of this book, and it dissolves chapter by chapter as each false credit is handed back to its rightful owner.

Why does this split matter now? Because smokers fear the first part far beyond its size. We imagine days of torment, sleepless nights, a personality changed. The picture comes from the second part, not the first. The voice shouts, you cannot cope, and we mistake the shouting for the size of the itch. Separate them and the fear shrinks to its true shape. A faint itch for a few days, welcomed as the sound of the loop loosening, is a very different prospect from a lifetime of craving.

I felt that difference myself when I finally saw it. The last days of smoking I paid attention rather than dreaming through the doses. I noticed the itch arrive on schedule, noticed the voice rush in with its list of reasons, noticed how the voice, not the itch, did the bullying. Once seen, the bullying lost its authority. The itch was a whisper. The voice was a loudspeaker. I had spent years obeying the loudspeaker while believing I was answering a real need.

You will feel the same separation in the coming days as you watch your own smoking while you read. Notice the timing. Notice how the want arrives by the clock rather than by sorrow or joy. Notice how quickly the voice supplies a grand reason — nerves, pleasure, focus — for a want that arrived on schedule before any reason existed. The observation itself weakens the voice. You do not need to fight it. You only need to see it clearly.

### THE HUG THAT DOES NOT LET GO

There is a reason the loop feels like friendship, and the reason deserves an honest picture.

Think of an embrace that comforts you at first, arms around the shoulders after a hard day, warm and close. Now imagine the arms do not loosen when you step back. You shift your weight. The grip stays. You smile politely and try to turn toward the door. The hands lock a little tighter. What began as comfort has become holding. You are still being told it is love. Your ribs tell a different story.

That is what the inhale learned to do. The first deep pull feels like arrival. The shoulders drop. The breath goes out slow. For a minute the noise in the head quiets. We call that minute help and we remember it. What we forget, because the voice edits the memory, is the rest of the hour: the slow return of the edginess, the glance at the pack, the planning of the next exit, the faint stale taste that stays after the warmth goes.

A friend who helps you does not need to be seen every hour to prove the help. A friend does not leave you winded on stairs and poorer at the till and still ask to be thanked. A friend does not require you to stand outside in the rain while the party continues inside without you. Measure by actions, not by the minute of warmth, and the embrace shows its true nature. It comforts the unease it keeps recreating, then calls the brief pause love.

I know the grief that rises when I say this, because I felt it. To hear that a companion of years was a captor feels like bereavement. Allow the feeling without obeying it. You are not losing a friend. You are recognising a grip. The warmth you felt was real, but it was your own warmth returning for a minute when the grip eased, not a gift placed in your hand from outside.

Ask the memory honestly. When did the dose truly add to a good hour, and when did it merely interrupt the hour to quiet its own alarm? The coffee was hot and fragrant before the lighter clicked. The talk among friends was funny before you stepped out to feed the itch. The pause after hard work was earned before the smoke entered it. The dose stood beside good things and took credit for them, the way a man who walks beside you in the sun claims he carries the light.

Once seen, the hug cannot feel the same. You still feel the arms, but you know they are arms that do not let go. And what does not let go was never friendship. It was holding.

### THE CLOCK THAT CALLS ITSELF COMPANY

If the hug explains why the loop feels like love, the clock explains why it feels like life.

Picture a clock hung inside your day by someone else, set to ring every hour, loud enough that you cannot ignore it. Each time it rings you feed it a coin and it quietens for a while. After years of feeding, you begin to say the clock keeps you company. You miss it when it is silent. You feel lost without its ringing to divide the hours. You tell visitors the ringing steadies your nerves and marks your pleasures.

Would you call that company? Or would you say a meter was installed in your day by the company that sold you the coins, and you feed it because the alarm feels worse than the coin?

Nicotine is that timekeeper. It rings, you answer, it sleeps, it rings again. The interval shortens across the years as tolerance grows — from the evening-only dose to the pack a day to the pack plus the ones borrowed and bummed and smoked without counting. The day becomes a string of intervals between feeds. We call the string a life with smoking in it. In truth it is a life interrupted by a meter, then credited to the meter for giving the day its shape.

Watch the meter at work in your own hours. The post-meal ring. The coffee ring. The car-door ring. The phone-call ring. The boredom ring that sounds most loudly when there is nothing to do and therefore nothing to need help with. Each ring arrives with the same urgency whatever the situation, which tells you the urgency comes from the schedule, not from the situation. Sorrow does not schedule itself hourly. Joy does not schedule itself hourly. Only a dose fades hourly.

"We are all in the same boat. I smoke because I like the rhythm. It structures my day."

I hear that thought as clearly as the choice thought, because I thought it. Let me answer it the same way. Who structured days before the first pack? You woke, ate, worked, rested, laughed, slept, without a single ring, and the hours had shape. Children run whole days of play without a meter and never complain the day lacked structure. The structure you miss when you imagine stopping is not structure the dose gave. It is attention the dose stole, then sold back in hourly slices.

The meter has one more trick. It borrows the language of need and dresses it as personality. The reliable timekeeper becomes the reliable self. I am a smoker. I need these breaks. I think better with one. I cannot face the morning without one. The sentences sound like self-knowledge. They are schedule-knowledge, learned so well we mistake the ringing for our own voice.

You are not a timetable. You are a person with breath and money and mornings, currently paying a meter to interrupt them. See the clock as a clock — installed, not born — and the idea of choice thins to transparency. You did not choose a companion. You were taught to feed a meter and to call the feeding company.

### WHAT I ASK OF YOU

You see more than you did when you opened this chapter. You see the morning reach happening before choice could operate. You see the stairs reporting what the slogans deny. You see the first foul encounters endured for a picture, then converted by tolerance into demand. You see the package behind the flavour and the dose inside the package. You hold two plain names for the two quiet parts. You feel the hug that holds too long and hear the clock that rings too often.

That seeing is the work. I do not ask you to act on it by force. I ask you to protect it by agreement.

There will be moments while you read when the brainwashing voice tries to win back ground. It will say, this cannot apply to me, my case is different, I really do enjoy it, the author never met my kind of stress. When it speaks, do not argue with it at length. Let the morning reach answer. Let the stairs answer. Let the clock answer. The body and the timetable are witnesses that do not flatter and do not lie.

To keep those witnesses clear, I place one small agreement at the centre of this chapter, where it belongs. It is not hard. It is the whole method in miniature.

4. FOLLOW ALL THE INSTRUCTIONS
Each one is small, and together they are the method.

That is the hinge. An open mind lets the argument in. Keeping the doses steady while you read lets you watch clearly rather than through panic. Beginning with curiosity rather than doom lets you hear. Following every step, even the ones that look too small to matter, lets the trap open like a lock. Miss one tumbler and the door sticks. Turn them all and it swings.

All you have to do is follow all the instructions.

Read that sentence as a plain fact about this book, not as a boast. I am not asking for trust in me. I am asking for completeness in you. Smokers who skipped — who kept the open mind but cut down to prove commitment, who read but braced for misery throughout — told me afterward they missed the very paragraph that would have reached them because they read it through craving or dread. Those who turned every tumbler, small as each seemed, found the door moved easily under the hand.

You may think, can such small agreements carry a habit of years? Ask what carried the habit. Small rings, hourly, each tiny in itself, together a chain. Small steps the other way, each tiny in itself, together a key. The trap was built coin by coin. It opens the same way, without a single act of force.

And if doubt says, reading on in this careful way will cost me something, answer it with the same plain bargain that brought you here. You have nothing to lose and everything to gain. No pain is asked. No decision tonight. Only the next chapter, read with the same open attention you brought to this one, watching your own smoking as you go, letting each piece land before you judge it.

Do you feel the ground shift even slightly under the word choice? You came to this chapter owning the habit as yours. You leave it suspecting the ownership was staged: the picture brought you in, the dose kept you in, the clock divided your day, the hug took credit for warmth that was yours all along. Hold that suspicion. It will grow into certainty without any push from me.

You were not weak. You were not foolish. Warmth belongs to you. Contempt belongs to the meter and the grip that lived off your breath and your pocket and called it company. Keep reading, and watch how thin the word choice becomes when set beside the morning reach and the stairs.
