Chapter 6
WHO YOU WERE BEFORE THE UNIFORM

IN THIS CHAPTER: the garage after the children are in bed, the side door at a party with rain coming down, the first coughing tries behind the school wall, and the shop windows that taught you what to want.

*You did not smoke because you were that kind of person — you looked like that kind of person because you smoked.*

### THE GARAGE AFTER BEDTIME

I want you to stand with me where no one who loves you can see you, because you have stood there a thousand times and I have stood there too.

It is half past ten. The children are in bed. The house is warm and quiet and the television murmurs in the next room. You say you are just taking something to the garage. You slip a pack and a lighter into a pocket so they do not show. The garage is cold. The concrete bites through your socks. A bare bulb hangs over the car. Paint tins crowd the shelf. A bicycle leans with one flat tyre. You close the door behind you so the smell will not follow you back inside, and you light.

You do not enjoy it the way you tell yourself you enjoy the others. You smoke it quickly, half angry at the cold, half angry at the hiding, listening for a small voice calling Dad or Mum. You blow the smoke toward the crack under the up-and-over door. You grind the end under your shoe and stare at it for a second. You wave your hands about your coat. You open the door a crack to let the air move. You practise a face in the dark that has not just done this. Then you walk back into the warmth and sit down and hope no one leans too close.

I am not writing that to make you feel low. I know that garage from the inside. I know the man who stood in it was not a hypocrite and not a fool. He was a kind father doing a cold, lonely thing he would never have chosen if he had seen it whole on the first day. I write it because I want you to hold that picture beside the other picture you carry, the one where smoking makes you belong.

You tell yourself, and I told myself for years, that cigarettes are part of who I am. You tell yourself that without them you are nobody in the group. You picture yourself in the circle, quick with a light, someone who knows the doorways and the jokes.

Now look at the garage.

If this is belonging, why does so much of it happen alone? If this is identity, why must it be hidden from the very people whose good opinion matters most to you? If this is being yourself, why do you leave yourself — the warm room, the talk, the chair that is yours — to stand in the cold with paint tins to be yourself for six minutes?

I ask that with all the warmth I have for you, and with contempt only for the trick that put you there.

We learned to call that hiding love. We said we hid it to spare them. Part of that is true and good — you are considerate. But look one layer deeper with me. The never-smoker in your house does not need to spare anyone. He does not rehearse a face. He does not wash the night off his hands before a goodnight kiss. The hiding is not proof of your character. It is the rent you pay for a uniform you thought made you yourself, and the rent is paid in small exiles, night after night.

That is the nicotine trap. It dresses you up and then sends you outside.

### STANDING OUT IN THE RAIN

Come with me to the second exile, because you know this one even better and it hurts more.

A party. Not a bad party — a good one. Music low enough to talk over. Old friends you do not see often enough. Food on the sideboard. Laughter from the kitchen. Someone tells a story you want to hear the end of. Halfway through, the want stirs under the ribs. You try to ignore it. It stirs again. You glance toward the others who smoke. One nods toward the back door. You go with them because staying feels fidgety and going feels rude, and both feelings arrive together.

Outside it is raining. Not romantic rain. Thin, cold rain that finds the neck. The patio chairs are wet so you stand. The light from the kitchen falls across the slabs and shows the damp on your shoulders. Inside, the story goes on without you. You hear a burst of laughter through the glass and you miss the line that caused it. You will ask what was so funny when you come back in, and someone will say, oh, you had to be there, and everyone will smile kindly and the moment will be gone.

You light anyway. The end hisses faintly in the damp. Your fingers get cold. You smoke a little faster than you want to because you want to get back. The others make talk, but it is not the talk from inside — it is doorway talk, short sentences between draws, eyes half on the door. When you finish you stub it under the wall where other ends lie blackened in a puddle. You breathe into your hand to check. You go back in, a little wet, a little smelling, smiling as if you had not missed anything.

I want you to stay inside that picture with me for one honest minute, because this is where the belonging story falls apart and I will be gentle with you while it does.

You went outside to belong and you spent the best ten minutes of the evening apart. You left warmth to stand in wet. You left talk to make small talk. You left the people you love to stand with people you like less, for the sake of doing something together that none of you would do if none of you did it.

Ask yourself, plainly, as a fair investigator would.

If smoking makes you part of the group, why is the smoker always the one who leaves the group? If it makes you grown and sure, why does it make you hover by a back door in the rain like a boy who has not been invited in? If it makes you yourself, why do you come back in a little damp and a little apologetic and pretend you missed nothing?

There is only one answer that fits both the garage and the rain without twisting. You do not smoke because you belong. You leave belonging to smoke. The uniform never put you inside the circle. It kept calling you out of it, and you called the calling loyalty.

It was never a genuine pleasure or crutch. A pleasure does not interrupt pleasure to be enjoyed in the rain.

### AT THE TIME EVERYONE DID

I know what rises in you now, because it rose in me, and I want to give it its full voice before we look at it together.

You say, ah, but at the start it was different. At the time everyone I hung out with smoked, so I HAD to also. You say, I remember thinking I could never do it because it was part of who I was.

I am not dismissing a word of that. I remember the canteen and the bus stop and the yard behind the hall where not holding one felt like being the only one without the joke. I remember learning the shrug and the laugh and the way to offer the pack open like you had done it all your life. I remember how quickly the coughing face turned into the cool face. Of course you thought it was part of who you were. We all did. We were young and we wanted to be someone, and someone was standing there with the door open.

Let me show you what that door was, because once you see it you cannot unsee it, and seeing it takes nothing from you.

You did not start because a want inside you cried out for smoke. No child wakes one morning with a physical hunger for burning leaves. You started for image and company and a little defiance — to look grown, to not be left out, to stand with the others rather than watch them. The first ones were dreadful. They burned. They made the head spin. They made you cough until your eyes watered. You persisted not because you liked them but because you wanted what they seemed to promise: a place, a look, a self.

That is not my guess about you to make you feel simple. The men who planned for new smokers to arrive wrote it down in their own papers: starters begin for image and going along and pushing back a little, not from a craving already there, and the early tries are unpleasant until the body learns to stand them. Read that again in your own memory and see if your own start does not match it. The dizziness. The bitter mouth. The determination to look as if it were nothing. You endured the dose to rent the uniform, and then the uniform began to charge you daily.

Here is the turn that matters most, and I want you to follow it slowly because it changes everything about who you think you are.

After a few weeks the body adapted. What burned began to feel normal. What made you cough began to feel like relief. But the relief was not the uniform giving you something new. It was the first whisper of the trap closing: each dose began to leave a small empty gnaw behind it, and the next dose briefly quieted the gnaw. You mistook the quieting for belonging and for being yourself. In truth the want had arrived after the uniform, not before it. The cause came first in a paper packet. The effect — the fidget, the need to step out, the sense that something is missing without one — came after, and you read it backwards for years.

Think of your own group honestly.

Did all of you laugh the same laugh before you smoked? Did all of you love the same music, tell the same jokes, want the same evenings? Of course not. You were different people who happened to feed the same small itch on the same schedule. The itch made you stand in the same doorways and make the same jokes about needing one. The shared schedule looked like a shared soul. It was not a shared soul. It was a shared timetable wearing your faces.

I see this clearly now in my own old circle. Two of us were shy and used the lighting and the puffing as something to do with our hands while others talked. One was loud and used the stepping out as a stage for stories. One was anxious and used the going outside as a reason to leave a loud room without saying he needed air. We told ourselves smoking was part of our personalities. In truth our personalities were doing their own good work — the shy one listening well, the loud one cheering the room, the anxious one knowing his own limits — and the dose stood beside each one and took the credit. Take the dose away and the shy is still thoughtful, the loud is still funny, the careful one still careful. Nothing real leaves. Only the timetable leaves.

So put the two honest questions to your own life and answer from what you have actually lived.

Who were you before the first one? Picture yourself at twelve or fourteen, before any packet touched your pocket. Were you nobody? Had you no humour, no loyalty, no place among your friends? You belonged at a table and on a wall and in games with rules you all knew by heart, with empty hands and a clear chest. Where did that person go? No one took him. You covered him with a uniform and then mistook the uniform for the skin.

And if the uniform is truly you, why did you have to learn you? Why the coughing and the nausea and the practised face in the mirror? You never had to learn your laugh. You never had to train your love for your team or your food or your people. What must be endured until tolerance arrives is not identity. It is costume, broken in until the rubbing feels normal.

Here is the verdict stated flat, as settled fact between friends. The traits you call a smoker's nature are the trap's results, not its causes.

### WHAT THE SHOP WINDOWS TAUGHT

There is a second half to this history and I will not spare the people who wrote it, because they deserve no sparing and you deserve no blame.

You did not dream up the uniform alone in your bedroom. It was sewn for you and hung where you could not miss it.

When you were young enough to learn what grown looked like, grown looked like smoke. Films let the hero light before he spoke. Posters put a lit hand beside every good night. Shops were paid to keep the bright packs low and forward where young eyes travel, at money that ran to more than a million dollars an hour across the trade in those spending years. Characters made to make children laugh taught children to recognise a smoking camel as readily as they recognised a famous cartoon mouse. I do not tell you that to frighten you into changing. I tell you so you stop believing you chose the image freely from nowhere. The method takes care of fear by taking care of the belief underneath it.

Look at that history the way a fair witness would.

A child does not connect a packet with freedom by accident. He connects it because every picture around him made the connection first, day after day, year after year, until the lie felt like memory. A teenager does not decide alone that stepping outside together means friendship. She decides it because she has watched a thousand doorways where the interesting talk seemed to happen in the smoke. By the time a hand offered you your first one behind a wall, your mind had already been fitted for the uniform long before your shoulders had grown into it. You were conned, not foolish. You were brainwashed, not weak. The brainwashing voice in your head that still says this is who I am is not your voice. It is their advertisement, still playing years after they stopped paying for the slot.

I want to be precise here so no one can say I overclaim and so you can trust me everywhere else.

I am not saying a named poster made you personally light on a named day. I am saying the trade as a whole worked at population scale, year after year, to make the young connect smoke with grown and fun and belonging, and that work set the stage on which your own small offer arrived and felt like your own idea. That is what their papers show in their own words, and what your own memory of coughing through the first ones confirms from the other end. You wanted to belong. They sold you standing outside as belonging. Both things can be true, and both leave your worth untouched.

Feel the weight of that for one second and then feel it lift off you.

If the want was planted before you could weigh it, then the shame never belonged to you. If the uniform was cut by men who counted packs the way other men count harvests, then taking it off is not losing yourself. It is refusing to be worn by them a day longer. The contempt belongs to the image-makers who fed on the young to keep old customers and raise new ones. The warmth belongs to you, who stood in their costume in good faith and paid for it in garages and rain.

And here is the concession I make gladly, because the method needs none of your history to be perfect to free you.

Perhaps you will say, but I am different — I liked the image, I chose it, I would have smoked even without the posters. Very well. Keep that thought if you need it. Suppose every poster had never existed and you had still picked one up to look grown among friends. The argument still stands whole. The first ones still burned. The relief still arrived after, not before. The garage and the rain still followed. The timetable still dressed itself as your nature. Whether the uniform was handed to you by a thousand windows or sewn by your own young hands to fit in for one term, it is still a uniform, and a uniform can be taken off without taking off the skin.

You do not need to settle who is more to blame — you or they — to walk out. You only need to see that what you call yourself in smoke is what the trap made you look like after it caught you, not what made you catchable in the first place.

### THE TALK IN THE SMOKING ROOM

There is one more knot to cut before you leave this chapter, and it is knotted tight around your future, so I will cut it cleanly.

From the day you first think of stopping, other smokers will tell you how hard it is.

You will hear it at work by the side door. You will hear it in the pub garden. You will hear it from people who love you and want you to succeed. Oh, I tried that, they will say. The cravings were terrible. I lasted three weeks and nearly climbed the walls. I put on weight. I could not think. I was miserable. You need a strong will. You need to be ready to suffer. Take it from me.

I know you believe them, because I believed them, and because they mean it. They are not lying about what they felt. They felt exactly what they describe. But they are telling you about the wrong method, not about your future, and hearing their story as your forecast is like taking directions for this road from someone who drove a different road in the dark.

Their suffering was real and it had a cause, and the cause was not the small physical itch. The itch is trivial — a faint empty feeling that peaks in days and fades in weeks, noticed mostly because the mind listens for it. Their suffering came from the belief still standing behind the itch: that they had lost a genuine support, that evenings would be duller, that stress would have no answer, that part of themselves had been cut away. With that belief intact, every stir of the itch sounded like proof of loss. With that belief intact, anyone would climb the walls. I would. You would. The pain they describe is the pain of mourning a friend while trying to keep off a captor — the tug felt on both ends because both ends were held by the same hands.

You are not doing what they did. You are not covering the itch while keeping the belief. You are removing the belief that made the itch sound like a voice. When the uniform is seen as costume, the stir between doses stops sounding like yourself calling and starts sounding like what it is: a dying whisper from a tiny creature already starving, and a loud old advertisement still playing in an empty room. You do not need to fight either sound. You only need to hear them truly.

So hear their talk with kindness and leave it where it belongs.

Pity the circle outside in the rain rather than envying it. Love the friend who warns you and do not take his forecast. He is describing what happens when the belief is left alive. You are doing the other thing — letting the belief die first, so there is nothing left to miss and nothing to fight.

Let that stand as your last move in this chapter, spoken plainly at the point where you need it most.

6. TAKE NO NOTICE OF OTHER SMOKERS' QUITTING TALK
Their struggle is the wrong method talking, not your future.

Keep that instruction with the others and follow it all. Each one is small, and together they are the way out. When the talk starts by the door, smile and stay inside your own knowing. You have nothing to prove to the doorway and nothing to learn from its hardship stories. Your path is not theirs, because your seeing is not theirs.

Look once more at the two pictures with which we began, and see them now with the uniform off.

The garage is just a garage again — paint tins, a bicycle, a cold floor that has no power over you. The man who stood there was never a closet smoker by nature. He was a father in a rented costume, paying night after night to stand apart from his own warmth. The party is just a party again — music, food, a story told well in a warm room. The figures outside in the rain are not the insiders. They are kind people in wet shoulders, feeding a timetable they mistake for themselves, missing the laughter through glass.

You were that figure. You are not that figure by nature. Before the uniform there was a boy or girl who belonged with empty hands, and that person never left. He stood in you through every doorway joke. She breathed in you between every dose. The laugh you bring to the circle is yours. The loyalty you bring to friends is yours. The steadiness you bring to hard hours is yours. The dose added nothing to any of them. It only taught you to read yourself as less without it.

Take the uniform off in your mind now, and walk back inside while the evening is still warm.

### SUMMARY

- I now see that the belonging I credited to cigarettes was paid for in hiding and exile, and the uniform was costume, not me.
- I now know that I started for image and company through coughing and dizziness, and the want arrived after the uniform, not before.
- I now understand that other smokers' hardship stories describe the wrong method, not my future, and I leave their talk by the door.
