Chapter 11
THE TWO VILLAINS

IN THIS CHAPTER: the drawer full of old methods, the memo room, the two ropes in one pair of hands, the friend in the doorway, and the quiet question about this book itself.

*You did not fail twenty times. You were failed twice, and neither failure was yours.*

You closed every softer exit in the last chapter.

No cutting down. No mild pack. No social-only. No just one. No substitute. No tomorrow. You saw each bargain to its end and you hold totality as plain logic now, not as severity.

And still one weight sits on your chest.

If it was all so plain, why did you fail so often? Why did you try the gum and the patch and the cutting down and the white-knuckle weeks, and end up back at the same pack in the same pocket? And if you try again now, what stops the same ending? Part of you fears failing again. Part of you fears something stranger, that succeeding will leave you missing something for the rest of your days.

I know that double fear. Every long-term smoker I ever sat with knew it. Let us lift it off you now, once, and put it where it belongs.

Not on you. On the two villains that put it there.

### THE DRAWER FULL OF OLD METHODS

Open your own drawer with me.

Not a metaphorical drawer. The real one. The kitchen drawer, the bathroom cabinet, the coat pocket. The half-used cards of gum with the foil pushed through. The patches that itched and curled at the edges. The lighter you threw away and bought again. The notebook where you marked days. The vape pen with the sweet liquid still in it.

You told me once, in your own words, that you had tried quitting cold turkey, slowly cutting back, nicotine gum, patches, vaping, and nothing had worked long-term. Another smoker told me he had probably attempted at least twenty times before and used most of the replacements and meds on the market with no success.

I believe you both. I do not doubt the number. I doubt only what you were told the number meant.

You were told it meant you are weak. That you lack will. That part of you does not really want to be free.

That is a lie, and it is the trap talking to protect itself.

Look at what those twenty times actually prove, seen straight.

Who keeps trying twenty times at anything if he is weak? A weak man tries once, shrugs, and stays where he is. You tried, and were hurt, and tried again with a new object, and were hurt again, and still kept the wish alive enough to be reading this page now. Is that weakness? Or is that persistence misused?

It is persistence. Strong will, spent in the wrong direction.

Ask yourself, while the drawer stands open, what exactly failed each time? Was it you, or was it the belief you were left holding?

Every one of those attempts left the same belief intact. That cigarettes calm you, complete your pleasures, and help you cope. That stopping means deprivation. Then it asked you to stand the deprivation by force. By counting. By waiting. By chewing a different coin while wanting the old one. By holding your breath through evenings that felt cut in half.

No wonder you failed. Anyone would fail on those terms, because the terms keep the want alive and then blame you for wanting.

That is the Willpower Method. It changes the package and keeps the belief. It removes the cigarette and keeps the hunger for what the cigarette was supposed to give. It tells you to resist a loss that was never a loss, and then counts your natural refusal to go on resisting as proof you cannot quit.

Do you see the cruelty in that? You were set a task that cannot be done, and then told your failure to do it was your character.

The task cannot be done because you cannot resist your way out of an illusion. You can only see through it. Once you see there is no genuine pleasure or crutch in any of it, there is nothing to resist. The twenty attempts did not test your strength. They tested a wrong method twenty times and got the same answer twenty times.

What would you think of a man who blames the key for not opening a door when he was given the wrong door? Would you call him weak? Would you tell him never to try a door again?

Then do not say it to yourself.

You are not the villain of those twenty stories. You are the person who kept knocking while someone else kept moving the lock.

### THE MEMO ROOM

Come with me now into a plain room you never saw, because the second villain worked there.

No smoke in this room. Fluorescent light. A table. Paper. Men in suits who never coughed in the morning and never stood in the rain outside a party to feed a clock in their chest. Their job was not to smoke. Their job was to make sure you would.

I want you to read two kinds of paper they left behind, beside your own biography, so you feel the weight shift off your shoulders where it belongs.

The first paper is about worry.

When smokers began to worry about health, when the cough deepened and the stairs told the truth, the planners for the milder brands wrote down their answer. Not how to remove the danger. How to calm the worry so the smoker would stay. Advertising for low-delivery products should ease anxiety about health and enable the smoker to feel assured about maintaining the habit.

Read that again in plain words. Concern was managed as a retention asset, not answered as a health question. The paler pack, the gentle name, the numbers on the side, the promise of lighter air, all of it was designed to let you keep feeding the meter while feeling you had done something careful.

That was their planning then for those products. I do not say every message on every pack today was written with that sentence on the wall. I say the sentence was written, and the packs you held were part of its harvest. You were not foolish to reach for the mild one when you felt the morning rattle. You were met exactly where you worried, by men paid to meet you there.

Does that really look like free choice? Or does it look like a hand on your shoulder steering you back inside whenever you neared the door?

The second paper is about starting.

You did not start because you needed nicotine. No child lights his first cigarette because his body misses a drug it never had. He lights it because everyone he hangs out with smoked, because it looked grown, because it looked like belonging, because refusing felt like being nobody in the group. He coughs. His eyes water. It tastes foul. He persists because the image pays him while the body protests.

Then tolerance converts endurance into demand. The little itch learns its timetable. The loud voice learns its lines. What began for image stays for the hourly call, and years later the man swears it is part of who he is.

They knew this. Their planners wrote that starters begin for image and conformity and defiance, not for a pre-existing craving, and that the first experiences are often unpleasant until tolerance arrives. That was their research planning then. I do not say your first cigarette followed their memo line by line. I say you coughed your way in for the same reason thousands coughed their way in, and they studied that cough as recruitment.

And the third paper is about the dose itself. They spoke among themselves not about taste or ceremony but about delivery. How much, how fast, in what wrapping. The pack and the puff as layers around a want they could wind up and keep wound.

Put those papers beside your drawer.

On one side, your twenty attempts, your money, your mornings, your hiding in the garage so the children would not see, your standing alone outside while the talk went on inside. On the other side, men who designed the coin, softened the worry that might have freed you, and taught the next generation to cough for image.

Who is the villain in that picture? Are you weak for getting caught? Or were you outgunned from the first party onward by an industry that needed you chained to stay rich?

Feel the blame move. Let it move all the way. There is none left for you to carry. Warmth for you. Contempt for the trap and the hands that built it.

You were conned, not choosing. You were kept, not weak.

### THE TWO ROPES

Now the fear itself, because clearing the past still leaves the tremble about the next try.

Name it plain. You fear failing again like the twenty times before. And you fear, in the same hour, succeeding into strangeness. That life without cigarettes will feel flat and grey. That stress will have no off-switch. That evenings will lose their glow. That you will spend the rest of your life missing a friend.

Two fears. Opposite directions. Both loud at once.

Have you ever watched a tug-of-war inside your own chest? On one end, do not try, you will only fail again. On the other end, do not succeed, you will lose your comfort forever. You pull and pull and call the pulling proof something is wrong with you, that part of you does not want to succeed.

Let me show you the geometry you are standing in, because once seen it cannot be unseen.

Picture a thick rope. You stand in the middle holding it with both hands, one end in each fist, leaning back and straining. Your shoulders ache. Your feet slip. You think you are torn between two truths.

Now look past your hands. Both ends of that rope run the same way, into the same pair of hands behind you. The trap holds both ends. It pulls the failure end by replaying your drawer full of old methods and saying, remember last time. It pulls the success end by whispering that cigarettes calm you and complete your pleasures, and asking how you will cope without them.

It is one rope, one holder, two directions of pull. That is why you feel torn apart no matter which way you lean. The tearing is the point. A torn smoker does not walk out. He stays to stop the pulling by feeding the hands that pull.

Ask yourself, while you feel the burn in your shoulders, what exactly would fail now? The method that failed twenty times was force against a believed pleasure. This book does not ask force. It removes the believed pleasure. All you have to do is follow all the instructions. Small steps, each one plain. Can you fail at seeing what you have already seen, that cutting down is worship, that mild is a hole, that just one is the whole cliff?

And ask yourself the other way, what exactly would you lose if you succeeded? Which part of the smoking you examined puff by puff would you miss? The burn? The sour last third? The clock-check? The twenty minutes down you pay for again an hour later? If there is no genuine pleasure or crutch in any of it, what does success take from you except the rope burn?

Do you see? Both fears are the same fear wearing two coats. Fear of the trap's pain if you try, and fear of the trap's pain if you stop trying. Neither fear is yours. Both are withdrawal of belief, not prophecy.

You do not need to be unafraid to walk out. You need only see whose hands hold the rope. The moment you see it, you can do what no torn man can do while he thinks the pull is his own character. You can let go.

Let go. The hands behind you can pull an empty rope all day. There is no one on your end any more.

It is the other way around. You feared trying because you believed you were the problem. You were never the problem. You were the hostage, and the hostage does not need courage to stop paying ransom once he sees the kidnapping.

### THE FRIEND IN THE DOORWAY

One grief remains, and I will not bulldoze it. I will honour it first, because it is real.

Many smokers say, toward the end, I feel I am losing my best friend. I said it myself. The little parties after every finished thing. The companion on long drives. The timekeeper that rang every hour and called it company. The hand on the shoulder in hard moments that seemed to take care of it all.

If you feel that grief rising now that the door stands open, I do not mock it. Of course you grieve. You spent years with something you believed stood by you. You paid it daily. You hid for it. You stood in the cold for it. Only a cold heart would walk away without a pang.

Now look plain at the friend in the doorway, one last time, before he goes.

What did this friend ever actually give you? Take one ordinary day you already lived. The stuck task. The post-meeting exit. The drive-time dose. The grocery-run payoff. Which part of the relief came from the stick, and which part came from the pause, the air, the finished work, the movement? You isolated each variable and saw the work keeps the credit. The break keeps the credit. The moment keeps the credit. The dose only sneaked a ride.

Take the hard moment you trusted most. If anything stressful happens, a cigarette takes care of it. You felt the taken-care-of wash over you in seconds. Then you watched the ten seconds up and the twenty minutes down, and you saw the wash was replenishment speed, the ending of a want the last dose lit, not help with the trouble itself. The trouble sat untouched beside the ashtray while you thanked the hand that left it there.

Swapping that hand for gum or patch or cloud does not change the hand. Quitting whisky by drinking gin is still sitting in the bar. You saw that too. Same product, different package. The meter stays bolted to the car no matter what flavour coin you feed it.

So who was this friend? An embrace that did not let go after you exhaled. A timekeeper you praised for reliability while it rang only because it had been wound by its own ringing. A lifeguard you thanked for each gasp after he held you under.

Would you call a man who pushes your head under and then hauls you up for a second's air your best friend? Would you visit his house after you escaped it to tell him you miss him? Would you let grief for his company talk you back into his cell after the door stands open?

Grief for a captor is the last trick of the brainwashing voice. It dresses absence as loss. But absence of a burden is not loss. It is relief. The pang you feel is not proof cigarettes gave you something. It is the little itch clearing its throat and the loud voice translating the throat-clear as sorrow. Both pass. Both starve within days when unfed, while the body returns to its own baseline on its own.

Let the friend go. He was never one. What you mourn is the picture he sold you of himself, and pictures cannot keep you warm.

When the thought comes next week, as a stray offer, as a joke, as a sigh after a meal, hear it pre-labelled. There goes the lost-friend script again. I know that line. It is not mine any more. Answer it with the pity you now own for smokers still inside, not with envy. Does that really look like enjoyment, the huddle outside in the rain while life goes on inside? You do not miss it. You escaped it.

### IS THIS JUST ANOTHER TRICK?

Now the cleverest objection, the one the sharpest readers save for last, often without saying it aloud.

Very well, you think. You have shown me I was brainwashed once. How do I know you are not brainwashing me again, only in the opposite direction? Words are cheap. Every seller says his method is different.

I welcome that question. It is the right question. It is the investigator in you waking up, and I want you to keep him awake for these last pages together.

Here is the difference, and you can test it without taking my word for anything.

The old brainwashing asked you to believe against your own experience. It told you cigarettes calm you, while your own mornings grew more rattled every year. It told you they help you think, while you needed more to sit through the same page. It told you mild means safer, while your hands pulled deeper without asking you. It told you one will not hurt, while every ladder in your life started with one that would not hurt.

This book asks the opposite. It asks you to check everything against your own yesterday. Did the calm last, or did the timer restart? Did cutting down feel like halfway out, or like worship? Did the unfed hour you once lived swell forever, or soften on its own like a radio left on in another room until you forgot to check? Did the coffee taste less when nothing burned over it, or did it taste of coffee?

I do not ask you to trust me. I ask you to trust the unfed hour you already survived, the stairs you already climbed winded, chained and poorer, the ladder you already climbed from party to daily. Those are not my phrases. They are your life.

And notice who profits. The industry profited when you believed. The sellers of substitutes profit when you keep believing and change packages. Who profits if you see there is nothing to buy, nothing to chew, nothing to charge, nothing to count? No one but you. I sell you no object. I give you no new schedule. I do not ask you to avoid your friends or your evenings or your coffee. Change nothing else in life except the belief, and inhabit every old context to prove freedom.

If this were a trick, it would be the strangest trick ever sold. A trick that removes the customer. A trick that ends the rent.

All you have to do is follow all the instructions. That is not a boast. That is the whole lightness of it. Keep an open mind. Finish the book before the final cigarette. Begin with excitement, not doom. Ignore advice that conflicts with this method. Take no notice of other smokers' quitting talk, for their struggle is the wrong method talking, not your future. Use no substitutes. Smoke the final one consciously when the time comes, then close the door. Small steps. Together they are the escape.

You came to this chapter carrying souvenirs of shame and calling them proof you are weak. Leave them here. They are not yours. One villain built the want. The other farmed the failures. You were the person in the middle who kept trying with a strong will in a false fight.

You are routeless no longer. The route is total leaving because there is nothing real to moderate, to milden, to socialise, to postpone, or to replace. The fear of trying again was the last rope, and you have seen whose hands hold it.

There is nothing to lose and everything to gain in reading on. Not as a slogan now. As your own accounting.

The cleared reader walks into the next room lighter than he came. Someone exactly like you already walked out ahead, on an ordinary dog walk, with two left in the pack and no ceremony, and told no one for a week. You will meet him next. Listen to him the way you would listen to your own future, because that is what he is.

### SUMMARY

- I now see that my twenty failures were the work of an industry that built the want and a method that kept the belief alive, not proof I am weak.
- I now know that fear of failing and fear of succeeding are both ends of one rope held by the nicotine trap, and I let go by seeing, not by force.
- I now feel grief for a companion fad­ing into recognition that the friend held me under, and I walk on without him.
