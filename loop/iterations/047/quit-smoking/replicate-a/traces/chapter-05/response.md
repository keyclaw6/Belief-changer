Chapter 5
THE REWARD THAT STOLE THE WORK

IN THIS CHAPTER: one ordinary workday from first task to last errand, the stuck mind and the lit pause, the doorway after the meeting, five hours of smoke and a petrol-station wash, and the grocery run that was supposed to be a payoff.

*The work did the work, the pause did the resting, and the day was full already — the dose only stood beside them and asked to be thanked.*

### THE DAY OF LITTLE REWARDS

Take an ordinary Tuesday of mine, back when I still smoked, because yours will match it closely enough to feel familiar.

I taught a class in the morning. I finished it and lit one. I sat down to write a story and got a paragraph down. I lit one. I got stuck halfway down the second page and lit one to think. I drove to see someone, all windows up at first and then cracked because the air turned thick, and I smoked two on the way there and two on the way back. I drove to the shops for nothing urgent and told myself one for the road both ways.

Cigarettes were my reward for — well, almost everything. Teaching a class. Finishing a story. Finishing a paragraph. Driving 500 miles. Driving to the shops.

Do you recognise your own day inside mine? The finished thing gets a light. The stuck thing gets a light. The empty stretch gets a light. The drive gets a light. We called them by cheerful names in our heads. I know I did.

"You've earned this."

"Take five."

"Think it over with one."

"Nothing to do? Fill the time with a cigarette, right?"

"I always tell myself that it makes me think better."

I am not mocking a single one of those sentences. I lived inside every one of them. I want to take them apart with you carefully, one variable at a time, until you see what did what, because the feeling of being rewarded is real and the credit is false, and that difference holds your whole working life inside it.

Start with the reward itself, because that is the one you will defend longest.

You finish something hard. A class taught. A paragraph wrestled down. A phone call got through without losing your temper. Relief moves through the chest. Shoulders drop. The mind says done, for a minute, and that done-feeling is sweet. In that sweet minute you light up. The hand is already moving while the sweetness is still spreading. Smoke goes down. The minute passes with taste and throat and hand busy. Afterwards you remember the whole bundle together — relief plus smoke — and you file it under the smoke rewarded me.

We all filed it that way for years. I did.

Now isolate the variable honestly, the way you would test anything else you wanted to understand.

Do the same finished thing tomorrow with coffee. Do it the next day with tea. Do it the next day with water. Do it the next day with nothing in your hands at all. The relief after finishing still arrives. The shoulders still drop. The done-feeling still spreads for a minute. The sweetness was in the finishing. The situation gave it with any drink or none.

Watch children for the same proof from the other end. A child finishes a drawing and runs to show it, chest out, face lit. A child builds something from boxes and stands back to admire it, buzzing head to foot. A child carries the same rush of done through the body drug-free, with no dose on the end of it. The energy to finish and to feel finishing is built in. It does not come in a pack.

So what did the cigarette add to that minute?

It added taste and throat-hit and hand business while the minute passed. It added a small lift of the gnaw going quiet underneath, which we mistook for a bigger glow on top. And then, quietly, it stole something much larger. Each time we paid the finished moment with a dose, we taught the mind that finishing means lighting. After a thousand payments, finishing without lighting feels thin, not because the finishing went flat, but because the mind is listening for the second half of a ritual we stitched onto it.

That is how a celebration shrinks down to a pellet.

At first a cigarette after work meant a party. Then a cigarette after each task meant a party. Then a cigarette after each paragraph meant a party. Then the drive needed one and the shops needed one and the wait for the kettle needed one. We kept calling them little parties long after they stopped feeling like parties at all. They were pellets fed on a schedule, each one sold as a reward until the day meant nothing without them and the reward meant nothing either — just a hand to mouth while the day ticked on.

Ask yourself, with kindness and without flinching, what kind of reward leaves you shorter of breath and shorter of money every time it congratulates you? What kind of prize needs repeating twenty times a day and still leaves the winner hungry for the next one? A true reward leaves you richer than it found you. This one left you with an ashtray to empty and a throat to clear and the same desk waiting.

It was never a genuine pleasure or crutch. A pleasure adds. This one only rode along while the moment added, and then sent the bill.

### THE STUCK MIND

The second defence is harder to let go of because it feels like evidence in the body. You will say, all right, perhaps the after-task one adds nothing, but the mid-task one helps me think. When I am stuck, one unlocks me. Many great writers smoked. I do my best puzzling with one going.

I said all of that. I believed it longer than any other piece, because I earned my living with sentences and I was frightened of losing them.

Let me take you into the stuck minute as it really happens, not as we remember it afterwards.

You sit with a hard paragraph. The sense will not come. You read the last line four times. Attention frays. Legs jiggle. A faint edginess sits behind the eyes. You reach. You light. You lean back. You stare out of the window while the smoke curls. After a minute or two the next line comes, or half a line, and you type it. You stub out and work on. You file it under the cigarette unlocked me.

Isolate the variable again and watch what else was in that minute.

You stopped staring at the line. You leaned back. You breathed more slowly. You looked at distance instead of print. You gave the mind a pause from forcing. Any mind, smoker or never-smoker, thinks better after a pause than inside a stare. The pause did the unlocking. The dose only stood beside the pause while it worked and claimed the wage.

There is a second thing in that minute, quieter, that we never counted at the time.

That frayed, edgy, cannot-settle feeling that made you reach — where was it on mornings before you ever smoked? Where is it in the child trying a hard sum, tongue out, fully absorbed, asking for no dose to think? Watch your own day and you will see the pattern. The edginess rises between doses on calm days and hard days alike. It wears the clothes of the hour — writer's block at the desk, impatience in traffic, blankness in the meeting — but it ticks to the schedule of feeds, not to the difficulty of the task. You light, the edginess dips for a few minutes because the want goes quiet, attention settles because the noise underneath stops for a minute, and you call that settling a lift.

If there is a flicker of brighter attention after a dose, it is brief, and it only returns you toward where a never-smoker sat all along. It leaves you no sharper across the morning than the colleague who never lit, and across the years it leaves the memory duller and the concentration shorter, not longer. I say that flatly because it is what the years show when you watch them whole. I do not want you to change from fear of it. I tell you so you stop crediting the flicker as help. The method takes care of the fear by taking care of the belief underneath it.

Put the honest questions to your own desk and answer from your own hours.

If cigarettes make you think better, why do you think just as clearly in places where you cannot smoke? On long flights, in hospitals, through three-hour meetings, through mornings chained to a classroom where lighting was impossible, you handled hard things and found words. Where did the supposed thinking aid go in those hours? You handled them because the thinking was always yours.

If the dose unlocks the line, why does the line so often arrive after the cigarette is out, when you lean forward again and re-read? Why does the best sentence of the day so often arrive in the shower, on the walk to the shops, in the first minute after waking, with no smoke anywhere near it? You know the answer because you lived it. The mind works while you look away. The pause lets it. The cigarette only kept your hands busy while you waited.

And if many great writers smoked, did they write greatly because of the smoke, or in spite of a chain that stole their breath, their money, and half their working hours to doorways and windows? Would their books be thinner if they had kept the pauses and left the doses? You know writers who never smoked and wrote greatly. You know children who learn to read and build whole worlds with no aid at all. The work belongs to the worker in every case.

Here is the verdict stated flat, as settled fact. The work, the break, and the mind were the givers all along. The dose sneaked a ride.

### THE DOORWAY AFTER THE MEETING

Come with me to the most honest five minutes of that workday, because you have lived them too.

The meeting runs long. The air in the room goes stale. Two people talk past each other. You hold your tongue and feel heat behind the ears. When it ends you are first on your feet. You push through the side door into the cold side yard with the others who smoke. Someone laughs too loudly with relief. Lighters click. You strike a match because you like the ceremony of it. The flame dips in the wind. You cup it. You draw.

Measured puffs, the way we learned to take them when others watched. Not too deep at first. Then deeper. You feel the legs stop jiggling. You feel the jaw unclench a fraction. Someone says what the meeting should have decided and everyone nods. You nod. The last drag turns hot and bitter on the tongue. You pinch it out against the wall, taste the bitterness, and walk back to the desk.

What helped in those five minutes?

The cold air helped. The standing helped after an hour of sitting. The laughing helped. The stepping out of the room helped most of all — leaving the stale air and the talked-past talk and hearing one plain sentence from a colleague that ordered the mess. Your own lungs filling with cold air helped. Your own legs moving helped. The pause helped, the way pauses always help thinking.

Watch the variable stripped bare. The never-smoker from the same meeting steps into the same yard, breathes the same cold, hears the same sentence, stretches the same legs, and returns to the same desk steadied. No dose passed their lips. Nothing is missing from their recovery. The yard works with any drink or none, with smoke or without.

What did the dose add? A burn at the back of the throat. Eyes that sting a little in the wind. A mouth that goes dry and faintly sour by the third puff. Fingers that will smell through the next hour of typing. A last drag gone bitter that you would never accept from food or drink, yet you accepted daily from this and called it part of the pleasure. Hold that bitterness honestly for one second. If a biscuit always ended in a bitter, chemical last bite, would you call the biscuit a reward for thinking?

The work of ordering the meeting's mess was done by you and the cold and the colleague's sentence, tired as you were. The dose stood beside your effort in the yard and asked to be paid as the thinker.

You finish those doorway minutes winded, chained and poorer. Breath a little shorter up the stairs back to the desk. Pack a little lighter. Purse a little lighter. Mind no clearer than the never-smoker beside you who only took the air. That triple price is not the cost of thinking. It is the wage the trick charges for letting you pause.

### FIVE HOURS OF SMOKE

The drive home tells the same truth larger, and funnier, if you are willing to laugh at the right party.

I once drove five hours to see family. I will tell it straight because the comedy belongs where it belongs.

Two lit before I left the car park, because a long road deserves stocking. One with the first coffee from the flask. One after the first hour because the road went straight and the mind wandered. One in the traffic jam because traffic means one. One after the traffic clears because clearing means one. One with the second coffee. One because the radio played a good song. One because the radio played a bad song. One to stay sharp. One because sharpness slipped anyway. The ashtray filled. I cracked the window and the rain spat in. I closed it and the air turned blue. I opened it again. My eyes burned a little. My throat felt lined with something dry. By hour four I was not enjoying a single one. I was feeding a clock that rang every half hour and calling the feeding driving.

Then the petrol station, which I want you to picture fully because you have played this scene yourself.

I pull in more for air than for fuel. I dump the ashtray into the bin, tapping it clean, ashamed of the smell when the lid lifts. I change out of the top I wore because it stinks. I wash my hands twice with the gritty soap and they still smell. I buy mints. I stand in the cold forecourt airing the car with all doors open while other drivers stare, breathing in the clean air like a man surfacing. I spray something pine under the seats that mixes with the old smoke into something worse. I get back in, satisfied I have hidden it, and light one ten miles down the road.

Laugh with me, not at yourself, because we all played it. Who is the joke on in that forecourt? Not the driver. The driver is a competent adult who just drove five hours safely through rain and jams. The joke is on the trick that made a grown worker spend twenty minutes deodorising a car, a shirt, hands, and breath, and call the thing he was hiding a reward for the drive.

Isolate the variable on that road the way you did at the desk.

The road is the same with the window shut and the air clean. The radio plays the same songs. The traffic jams the same. The never-smoker drives the same five hours, stops once for fuel and a stretch, arrives with clear eyes and a shirt that smells of nothing, and remembers the same fields and the same bad song. What did your extra twenty doses buy that his drive lacked? A headache behind the eyes. A throat that needs clearing all evening. A car that needs airing. A wallet lighter by the price of the pack that rode shotgun.

Children ride that same road drug-free and find it endless and wonderful by turns, spotting lorries and counting bridges, asleep in the back by hour three with mouths open. Their attention does not need a coin every half hour to stay alive. Yours did not either before the first pack taught it to ask.

Ask the drive its own questions and let it answer.

If cigarettes keep you sharp at the wheel, why did your attention still slide in hour four after a dozen feeds? Why did you still need to slap your own cheek and open the window wide? A sharpness aid would sharpen across the dose. Yours faded between every dose because the fade is what the dose maintains.

If the smoke fills the empty stretches, why did the emptiest stretch feel longest just after stubbing out, and shortest while you were busy with the song or the fields? The emptiness was never in the road. It was the gnaw between feeds, wearing a road's clothes for an hour.

There is only one honest reading that fits the forecourt and the five hours together. The drive gave whatever the drive gives — movement, songs, thoughts arriving unforced, miles ticking down. The doses gave throat-burn, blue air, and a cleaning ritual, then claimed the drive as their party.

There is one more whispered reward smokers name among themselves, the one after love, and I will not stage it here. We will take it apart in its own room where it belongs, on its own ground, and you will see it follows the same order as the doorway and the drive.

### THE SHOPS AND THE EMPTY HOUR

The last errand of the day is the smallest and therefore the most revealing.

Evening. Dinner done. Nothing urgent needed, but the house wants milk and the legs want a walk, so you drive to the shops. You tell yourself one for the road. The shops are ten minutes away. The car park is half empty. You buy the milk and a paper you will barely read. You walk back to the car. You tell yourself one for the way home. Two doses to carry milk across town.

Or the other version. Nothing to do for an hour before bed. The telly murmurs. The phone offers nothing. A faint itch of now-what moves in the chest. You say the old sentence without hearing it — nothing to do, fill the time — and step out back with one. The hour passes with hand to mouth and back. Nothing happens in the hour except the dose. Afterwards you remember the hour as filled.

Both versions run on the same error, and children expose it without trying.

Give a child an empty hour with a cardboard box and watch what fills it. A fort. A shop. A spaceship with a milk-bottletill. Give a child a ten-minute errand and it becomes an expedition — puddles, cats, cracks in the pavement not to be stepped on. The hour was never empty. The errand was never bare. A mind not listening for the next feed finds the hour full on its own. We found our hours empty because we trained them to wait for a pellet to fill them, and then we praised the pellet for filling an emptiness it dug.

Try the isolation once, in life, not in argument, while you still smoke and finish this book.

Take the same milk run tomorrow with no dose but with full attention. Notice the cold on the door handle. Notice the car-park lights coming on. Notice the tired pleasure of an errand done without fuss. Take the same nothing-to-do hour with a chair by the window and nothing in your hands. Notice how the itch rises, peaks like a wave, and passes whether you feed it or not. Notice how the mind, left un-fed for one hour, starts up again on its own — planning, remembering, settling — the way a stream clears when you stop stirring it.

You will find the minute that frightened you was never bare. It was only un-fed, and un-fed minutes feel strange for a while to a mind that has been fed hourly for years. Strange is not empty. Strange is the feel of your own baseline returning.

This is where your strongest objection returns, and I want to meet it head-on because it deserves respect.

You will say, but I felt the reward. I felt the think. I felt the fill. You cannot talk me out of what I felt. I am not trying to talk you out of what you felt. The wash after lighting is real. The dip of the gnaw is real. The pause is real. I felt them a thousand times. I am asking you to reassign them truly, because feeling honestly and crediting truly are different acts, and we did the first well and the second badly for years.

Did the finished task feel sweet before you ever smoked? Yes. Did pauses help thinking before you ever smoked? Yes. Did empty hours fill themselves before you ever smoked? Yes. Did the dose touch the task, the thought, or the hour, or did it only touch the want underneath while those three did their own work beside it? Your own Tuesday answers. The task stood finished either way. The thought arrived in the pause either way. The hour filled with milk and lights and window air either way. The only thing that moved when you lit was the needle of the want, up for a minute and already falling.

That is the inversion for this whole workday stated flat. You never smoked to reward the work. You worked, and then you paid the work to the trick. You never smoked to think. You paused, and the trick charged the pause. You never smoked to fill the time. The time was full, and the trick taught you to read it as empty so it could sell the filling.

Seen whole, the day of little parties was a day of real givings — class taught by you, lines found by you, miles driven by you, milk fetched by you — with a small burning stick carried alongside each one, dulling the taste of the coffee, greying the air of the yard, lining the throat on the road, demanding the forecourt wash, and asking at every step to be thanked as the giver.

Stop thanking it and the givings remain. They were always yours.

Keep smoking steady while you finish this book, and test this one move the way you tested the last. After each finished thing, pause before reaching and ask which hunger is asking. After each stuck minute, take the pause with nothing in your hands and watch whether the line still comes. On the next drive, count what the doses buy besides the wash and the cleaning. On the next milk run, go with empty hands and full attention and see whether the errand still completes.

You will find the same answer each time, and it will settle deeper than argument. The reward never rewarded. It only stood beside the rewarding and held out its hand.

### SUMMARY

- I now see that the lift and reward I credited to cigarettes were done by the task, the pause, and the hour itself.
- I now know that thinking and finishing belong to me and arrive with any drink or none, not with a dose.
- I now understand that empty time fills on its own and the restless itch between doses was the schedule asking, not life lacking.
