Chapter 14
MORNINGS, DRIVES, SHOPS, EVENINGS

IN THIS CHAPTER: the kettle on the first free morning, the car without the window down, the shop queue and the walk home with the bags, the kettle on again and the evening unfolding free, coffee that tastes of coffee, the patio in evening air, and the pub garden where the old circle still lights up.

*You are not waiting for an ambush now — you are living ordinary days as a non-smoker.*

You wake and the hand does not move.

The light is coming through the curtain the way it always did. The kettle clicks. The house makes its small morning sounds. For years this was the hour when you coughed yourself awake and reached for the pack before your feet reached the floor, and the day began with a small empty tug under the ribs that told you when to stand and when to sit. This morning the tug is not giving orders. You notice the quiet because you know what used to fill it.

That knowing is the whole difference.

Get up slowly and feel how ordinary this is. Wash. Dress. Drink water. The chest feels clear in a way you had forgotten was normal, and you know the clearness is yours again, not borrowed for twenty minutes. We used to pay for that first small lift with the rest of the day on the hourly bell. You see now there was never anything in it to miss.

You are not on guard this morning. You are not holding yourself together. You are simply up, and the day is simply there, and that plainness delights you.

### THREE QUIET MORNINGS

The first morning feels strange because you expect it to feel strange, and then you feel almost surprised by how little there is to fight.

You move through the rooms the way you always do. You put the kettle on. You find the clean cup. You stand at the window while the water heats and you watch the street wake. The body does its morning business without a coughing fit to open it. The throat is clear. The mouth tastes of nothing but sleep. You drink the water and you feel the cold go down clean. There is no timetable telling you to hurry the cup so you can get outside. There is no small calculation running underneath about whether there are enough left for the day. You are not missing a step. You are missing a chain.

The second morning you wake and for a second the old thought flickers, have one with coffee, before you are even fully awake. Do not be startled by that flicker. It is only a memory firing while the little physical itch starves out within days. You answer it with joy and you get on with your morning, and you feel relief that you no longer smoke.

You smile at how thin it is. It has no weight behind it. It is the sound of an empty room you used to keep a chair in. You think FANTASTIC! I'M FREE! and the kettle boils and the moment passes and leaves you lighter, not weaker. You pour the water. You stir. You carry the cup to the table and you sit down because you want to sit down, not because you need a reason to stay still for five minutes.

The third morning you forget to remember. You make coffee. You find your keys. You open the door and the air outside smells of rain and bins and someone's toast, and you take a deep breath for nothing and you know freedom feels ordinary, which is exactly why it lasts.

Nothing here needs new rules. You do not need a different morning. You need the same morning, lived free.

That is how you will know this is done. Not by a blaze of triumph every hour. By the ordinariness. By the mornings stacking up behind you, each one a little plainer than the last, each one yours.

### THE DRIVE AND THE SHOP QUEUE

Take the car you always took.

For years a long drive meant five hours of smoke and the window cracked open whatever the weather, and then the dance at the petrol station with the ashtray and the shirt and the hands, trying to leave no trace. You know that dance was never enjoyment. It was housekeeping for a habit.

Drive it now with the window shut. Breathe the clean air in the car and feel how good it is to arrive without needing to air yourself out. When the old thought comes at the red light, that a drive goes better with one, ask yourself what better ever meant. Did the road change? Did the traffic ease? Or did a want quiet for a few minutes because you fed it? You know the answer, and the lights change, and you drive on free.

You park where you always park. You lock the car and you walk in the way you always walk in, and the bell over the door goes as it always goes.

Stand in the shop queue where you used to count the packs behind the glass and work out whether you had enough for the evening. Stand there with empty pockets and feel the lightness. You do not need to avoid the shop. You do not need to look away. Walk in for milk and walk out with milk. Inhabit every old place confidently and let each one prove you. Changing nothing else in your life is how you prove the trap took nothing with it when it went.

You stand in the line and you feel the lino under your shoes and the warm air from the heaters and the small shuffle forward. The basket hangs from your hand. Milk. Bread. Eggs. An onion rolling loose in the bottom. The woman ahead of you is counting coins. The boy at the till is scanning too fast and having to do one twice. The glass doors of the chillers hum. Behind the counter the wall of packs sits where it always sat, neat and coloured and lit, and the price board ticks over it in white and black.

You see it. Of course you see it. You are not pretending it is not there.

For years this was the place where the little calculation ran. Have I got enough for tonight. Will this pack see me to morning. Should I buy two to be safe. Should I get the ones I like or the ones that are on offer. The hand patting the pocket to check the shape of the pack through the cloth. The small tug of unease when the pocket felt too light. The relief when the fresh cellophane crackled in the bag beside the milk. That whole chain ran here, in this queue, a hundred hundred times, so quietly you thought it was part of shopping.

Now there is no chain. There is only the queue moving forward and the basket on your arm and your own breathing, easy and unhurried.

You reach the till. You put the milk on the belt. You put the bread beside it. You put the eggs where they will not roll. The boy scans them. The total comes up. You pay. The money for the packs stays where it is. You feel it stay, not as a triumph you have to recite, but as a plain fact in your pocket. The coins do not leave. The notes do not unfold. The card does not carry that old extra. What stays is yours, and it stays without a speech.

You pick up the bag. The handles cut a little into your fingers the way they always did. The milk is cold through the plastic against your leg as you turn from the counter. You push the door with your shoulder because your hands are full, and the cold outside air meets your face, and you step out onto the pavement with nothing else to buy and nowhere else to go for it.

Walk home with the bags.

Feel how simple this walk is. The street sounds as it always sounds. A bus sighs at the stop. Someone laughs from an open window. A dog pulls at its lead. Your shoes strike the pavement in their ordinary rhythm. The bag swings a little and settles. The milk shifts against the bread. There is no reaching into the pocket as you walk. There is no checking how long since the last one. There is no planning where you will stop to light up out of the wind. No clock runs in your head while your legs move. You are not counting down to anything and you are not holding off anything. You are carrying milk home.

You let yourself notice how light your pockets are. Not light the way an empty pack feels light, with panic under it. Light the way a man feels when he has stopped carrying a weight he had forgotten was heavy. Keys. Coins. Nothing else. No box. No lighter. No smell folded into the lining. Your hand in your pocket finds only what belongs there, and it comes out again, easy.

You turn your key in your lock. You step into your hall. You put the bags down on the kitchen side with the same small thump they always make, and the onion rolls out and you catch it. You put the milk in the fridge. You put the eggs in their tray. You put the bread in the bin. The kitchen fills with the small clatter of things put where they live. Nothing in this room asks you to step outside.

Put the kettle on.

You fill it and you set it down and you click the switch, and it begins its low murmur and then its rising hiss, the sound you have heard ten thousand times. You take down your cup. You spoon in the coffee. You stand and wait while the steam begins to rise, and the window over the sink shows the evening coming down outside, blue at the edges, the street lamps just lit. This is your kitchen. This is your kettle. This is your evening beginning, and there is no second errand hidden inside it.

The kettle clicks off. You pour. The steam warms your face. You carry the cup to the table and you sit, and the bag from the shop lies folded by the bin, done with, needing nothing more from you.

Let the evening unfold.

It unfolds the way evenings unfold. You drink the coffee while it is hot. You cut bread. You butter it standing up because you are hungry and the dinner is not yet made. You put the pan on. You chop the onion you carried home and it stings your eyes a little and you laugh at yourself. You stir. You salt. You taste. The radio talks to the room. The phone lights with a message and you answer it. The plate warms your hands when you carry it through. You eat slowly. You wash the pan. You dry your hands on the towel by the cooker. The clock on the wall moves, but it is only telling the time now. It is not telling you when.

Later you sit down. You stretch your legs out. You turn the pages of what you were reading or you watch what you were watching. The house settles around you. The pipes tick. Someone walks past outside. The cup stands empty beside you. No bell rings to send you to the door. No restlessness gathers under your ribs demanding its interval. No debate starts about whether you have earned one or whether you can afford one or whether one would round the evening off. The evening is already round. It needs no rounding.

When the faint thought comes, as it may, thin and far off, that this is the hour when you used to step out, you do not argue with it and you do not feed it. You recognise it for what it is, a memory firing in a room that no longer keeps that chair, and you answer it the way you answer every such thought now, with joy.

FANTASTIC! I'M FREE!

And the thought passes the way a cloud passes, and the evening goes on being the evening, only free.

Feel how the money stays through all of this. Not as a sum you recite to bribe yourself. As continuity. The price board in the shop stays behind you. The pocket stays light. Tomorrow the same pocket will still hold what it holds tonight, and the week after that a little more, and the year after that enough for something you truly want, a coat, a trip, a dinner with someone you love where the bill does not sting. You do not need to count it. You only need to feel that nothing leaks now. For years a hole ran through every day and every week, and you poured money through it to buy breathlessness and bondage. The hole is closed. What you earn stays with you. That staying is not the reason you stopped. It is the bonus that keeps arriving while you live free for the rest of your life.

And tomorrow there will be another errand.

There will be another queue. The petrol station. The corner shop for stamps. The supermarket run with a longer list. You will walk in the same way and stand in the same warm air and hear the same beep of the till, and the wall of packs will be there again, and you will see it again, and again there will be nothing to debate. The lightness you felt today will be there again, quieter this time, more taken for granted, which is exactly as it should be. Freedom does not need to announce itself every time. It persists.

You will carry those bags home too. You will put that kettle on too. You will watch another evening come down over the sink, blue at the edges, and you will sit with your cup and feel the same completeness, not as a lesson you must remember, but as the way things are. No clock. No debate. No new rule. Only the ordinary continuity of a life that no longer interrupts itself.

That is what the shop proved to you today. Not that you are strong. That there is nothing to be strong about. You walked in for milk and you walked out with milk, and the bags came home, and the kettle boiled, and the evening unfolded, and the money stayed, and the lightness held into the next errand and the one after that. Each old place inhabited confidently proves you again, until proving is no longer needed and living is all that is left.

### COFFEE THAT TASTES OF COFFEE

Sit down with morning coffee and a finished dinner the way you always did, and notice what the moment itself gives you.

We used to believe the cigarette completed them. The coffee. The meal. The pause with company when the plates are cleared and nobody wants the evening to end. You believed the taste was richer and the company warmer because of the smoke beside it.

Taste it now and see where the pleasure always lived. The coffee tastes of coffee. The food tastes of food. The talk is still talk. The completion you feel when the cup is empty is complete without a dose on top of it. The cigarette was only ever sneaking a ride on a moment that was already whole.

Sit a little longer than you need to, not because you must prove anything, but because you enjoy sitting there. That ease is what a non-smoker feels for the rest of your life, and it is yours from now on without rent.

### THE PATIO IN EVENING AIR

Go out to the same back patio in the evening.

You know this place. The chair. The rail. The sound of the street thinning out. For years you stood here to feed a want on a timetable and called it company. Now stand here with nothing in your hand and no clock in your head. Take one concrete breath of the cool air and feel it reach all the way down.

This patio belongs to its owner again.

You do not need to fill the pause. The pause is not empty. It is free time, and you feel glad the timetable no longer rings in it.

When a faint pang comes here, thin and far off, do not turn it into a debate. There are no two sides in you now to debate with. There is only a dying echo and your answer. Think it loudly inside yourself, the way a free person speaks of an escape.

FANTASTIC! I'M FREE!

Each time that thought fires, it becomes a small burst of relief. The reminder that once would have pulled you back now tells you the door is shut and the room beyond is yours.

### THE PUB GARDEN

You will stand in the pub garden among friends while the old circle lights up, and you will feel something you did not expect.

You will not feel envy. You will feel distance and warmth at once. Watch them step out into the cold to stand around a small fire of their own making, watch the talk pause for the lighting and the searching for lighters, watch one go to buy more because the pack is short for the night. Ask yourself honestly what you are seeing.

Does that really look like enjoyment?

You know it does not. It looks like the hourly bell ringing in good company. You feel glad you no longer answer it, and you feel kind toward the people who still do, because you stood exactly where they stand. We all stood there once.

Say nothing about it unless they ask you plainly. I tell you this firmly because I want to protect your freedom. Preaching breeds argument, and argument drags you back into defending what you no longer need to defend. Let your ease do the talking. If someone asks how you stopped, tell them it was easy and you feel wonderful, and leave it there. A happy non-smoker in the circle does more good than a sermon.

Do not avoid your friends and do not avoid the evening. Keep your friendships and your drink and your good nights exactly as they are. The night does not need a dose to be complete, and you prove that by staying in it, free.

There is one piece of language I want you to drop for good. Never say instead. Never say I can't have one so I'll have this. A sweet, a vape, a chew, a deep-breathing ritual used as a replacement all whisper the same lie, that something was taken and something must fill the gap. Nothing was taken. You escaped. Say what you truly want in positive words. Great, I'll have coffee. Lovely, I'll take a walk. You do not substitute for a dead enemy. You rejoice that the enemy is dead.

You feel a small sadness sometimes when you think of it as an old companion, and I understand that sadness. For years it stood beside work and breaks and talk. See it clearly now and let the sadness turn. You are not mourning a friend. You are celebrating the death of a captor that posed as one. A dead captor deserves no tears. It deserves relief.

11. WHEN THE THOUGHT OF A CIGARETTE COMES, ANSWER IT WITH JOY
Let every reminder fire the replacement thought.

You own that answer now, and you use it once each time, and you move on. You never reopen the decision. The deciding was finished over the ashtray. A free person does not hold a meeting about every memory.

### IF YOU LIGHT ONE

Let me tell you what to do if you ever light one, so one mistake never becomes a chain.

Do not tell yourself you have failed. Do not tell yourself you have got away with it either. Both sentences are the same lie wearing different coats.

If it happens, see it for what it is. A warning rumble, never a failure and never a license to repeat. It tastes foul because it is foul. It gives you nothing because there was never anything in it. What matters is never letting the belief back in. Remind yourself at once that you are a non-smoker who made one mistaken puff, not a smoker again, and think FANTASTIC! I'M FREE! and go on free from that minute.

Do not smoke another to punish yourself or to test yourself. There is no test. There is only the door, and you keep it shut.

You came out of the last chapter free. You walk through these ordinary days and you find them ordinary, which delights you more each time. Mornings without the reach. Drives without the window down. Shops without the count. Coffee that tastes of itself. Evenings on a patio that is yours. Company you keep without stepping out into the cold.

Live them exactly as you would have lived them, only free.

### SUMMARY

- I now know ordinary moments were always complete in themselves, and I live them free without needing new rules.
- I now feel joy whenever smoking crosses my mind, and I answer the thought with relief that I no longer smoke.
- I now see a lit cigarette among friends as bondage rather than pleasure, and I pity the circle without envying it.
