Chapter 5
REWARD

IN THIS CHAPTER: the bakery door with its bell, the smile-face cookies, the pay-day sweet, the tin from England, the Friday drawer, the table with candles, the tight shoes.

*The love lived in the people and the pause lived in the rest, and the sweet only rode along and took the praise.*

## THE BAKERY DOOR

You know this door. I know it too. We all know it because we were led through it by the hand.

You are six or seven. Your mother holds your hand tight on a wet street. The bakery bell rings too loud for so small a shop. Heat breathes out from the trays. Light sits low in the glass case. Rows of buns shine under the strip. And there they are at your eye level, the smile-face cookies with pink cheeks and currant eyes, grinning up like small friends.

You do not choose them the way a grown man chooses food. You point because the face points back. Your mother laughs, and that laugh does more work than any taste ever could. She buys one. She breaks it in half. She eats the eyes first to make you laugh again. You eat warm sweet dough on a cold day while her hand stays on your shoulder. Tell me straight, what made that hour sweet. Was it the dough, or was it her hand.

I keep that hour warm on purpose. I do not mock it. I do not touch your mother with one cold word. She loved you and she paid love in the coin the street taught her. We all learned that coin before we could spell it. Food meant love because love kept arriving with food in its hands.

Follow the coin as it grows with you. Pay day comes. Your father walks in with a paper bag that crackles. Or your mother counts coins on the step and holds back a little for a bar each for the week that nearly broke her. You hear the rustle from the hall and you run. You know that run. Knees on lino. Hands up. Mouth open before you even see the wrapper. The sweet lands in a house tired from work and bills and damp, and for ten minutes the house softens. Talk turns kind. Shoulders drop. You learn without words that hard weeks end in chocolate.

Then the tin. You know the tin I mean. The round Christmas tin with the snowy inn on the lid, tied with string to England trips and late boats and grandmother gifts. Your gran kept it on the sideboard long past Christmas. When you came through her door after the long drive, cold to the bone, she lifted the lid like she lifted a lid on love itself. Fingers of shortbread. Toffees that pulled teeth. Sweets gone soft at the edges from months shut. You ate them on her rug while the fire spat and the grown folk talked low and kind above you. You think you taste tin when you think of her. You do not. You taste rug warmth and fire spit and safe talk and arms that never hurried you.

That is how the wire got laid. Sweet arrived always in the arms of love, rest, relief, pride, safe return. No child could split the two. No child should have to. We swallowed both at once and called both love.

See it now with grown eyes and you see what we did. We were conned the way all kind folk get conned, not by our people but by the hour itself. The people brought the love. The pause brought the rest. The fire brought the warmth. The pay day brought the pride. BAD SUGAR rode each of those like a small thief on a big cart and claimed the whole ride as its gift.

You called it a genuine pleasure, comfort or lift.

Ask with me, and answer plain. If the comfort lived in the sweet, why did the same sweet eaten alone at the sink, cold, quick, hid from sight, bring no comfort at all. Why did the smile-face cookie bought by no one, eaten with no hand on your shoulder, taste flat and too sweet and leave you sadder. You know why though the old voice begs you to blink. The hand made the hour. The sweet only sat in it.

That is the sugar trap at its softest and its strongest, not the two o'clock droop you have already seen through, but the warm wire laid in childhood that still sings when you feel good or tired or kind.

Think how that wire still pulls your grown hand. You pass a bakery with your own child and your chest tugs before your head speaks. You smell warm dough through a door and you feel loved though no one in that shop loves you. You see a tin on a shelf and your throat thickens for a gran long gone. That tug feels like proof that sweet holds love. I tell you it proves the reverse. It proves love once held sweet so tight that the smell still calls the love back. Honour the call. Keep the love. Let the dough go. You know from your own life that the tug fades once you see it, the way a song heard plain loses its spell yet keeps its tune.

The wire was laid in love. It can be lifted in love.

## THE PAY SLIP AND THE FRIDAY DRAWER

Now walk with me into your grown week, for here the old wire pulls hardest in plain daylight.

Monday to Thursday you haul. You rise early. You answer hard mail. You sit through talk that drains the face. You hold your tongue when you want to shout. You do the school run in rain. You mend what broke. By Friday your head feels thin and your back feels old. Someone in the office says what they always say, we have been good this week, we have earned it. A drawer opens. A bag lands on the desk. Chocolate for the room. Cake for the team. A bottle for the train home.

You hear your own lines come up word for word. I know them because I spoke them for years.

"I deserve a little treat now and then."

"Sugar is the reward for a hard earned paycheck."

"celebrating, rewarding myself, going to the cinema, etc with chocolate."

Say them out loud and hear how kind they sound. They do not sound like trap talk. They sound like fair pay for fair work. That fair sound is the whole con, and you can see it once you hold the week still.

Who paid you for that week. Was it the bar in the drawer, or was it the lass who saved you the corner piece and told you that you carried the team. Was it the sweet, or was it the ten minutes with feet up while the kettle boiled and no one asked you for a thing. Was it the chocolate, or was it the walk out into low sun with your mate, coats flapping, laughing at the boss in a way you would never dare indoors. Peel the hour apart and mark what did what. People praised you. Rest eased you. Laughter aired you. Light lifted you. The dose sat in the middle of all that like a small loud guest and took the bow for the whole room.

I lived this pay week too. I kept a drawer the way you keep a drawer. Bottom right, under the pens, two bars deep in case one ran out. I told myself the drawer got me through. I told myself Friday needed chocolate the way lungs need air. Then one Friday the shop shut early and the machine stood empty and I had to take my break plain. I sat with tea. I put my feet up. I heard the room laugh. I walked the same low sun walk. My week still closed. My shoulders still dropped. My head still cleared a notch. Nothing true failed to arrive. Only the wrapper failed, and no one mourned it but the old voice in my head.

Think of your own Fridays with a cold eye. Which Fridays warmed you most. Was it the Fridays with most sweet, or the Fridays with most kind talk and early close and sun on the roofs. You know the answer though you flinch to say it. The sweetest Fridays often ended heaviest, mouth clagged, head thick, belly blown, temper short on the train, though the week deserved better. The plainer Fridays, a proper meal, a walk, a laugh, left you lighter to meet the night. If chocolate paid the week, more pay would mean more peace. With true pay that holds. With wages that holds. With thanks that holds. With BAD SUGAR the reverse holds, and the reverse proves the pay false.

"The sweetness of chocolate can hint at love." Keep that line and turn it slowly, for it tells on itself. Hint at love. Not love. Hint. A postcard of a fire is not a fire. You do not warm your hands at a postcard. You warm them at the grate while the postcard sits on the shelf. The bar hints at the warmth the room already holds, then claims it made the warmth. That is the whole trick of reward sweet, to stand near true pay and take the wage.

"Dessert is the best part of every meal." Is it. Or is it the part where work stops and talk loosens and no one watches the clock. Mark your best meals in memory. The food matters less than you swear it does. What you name best is the hour the table laughed longest, the night no one hurried the plates, the lunch that ran over because no one wanted to leave. The sweet came at the end of that and got named best the way the last man in gets named tallest. Take the sweet off that table in your head and replay the hour. Talk still laughs. Light still hangs low. Faces still glow. Nothing true leaves. Only the clag leaves.

"chocolate makes me feel like I am in heaven." I hear the ache under that line, for heaven is a big word for a small bar. What heaven did you mean. Quiet. No bills. No noise. Arms round you. Time unhurried. Music low. Sun through glass. I grant you heaven. I want heaven for you too. Only ask with me, did the bar build that heaven or borrow it. You ate that bar once in a traffic jam with horns all round and it built no heaven at all. You ate it once after a row and it built no heaven then. Heaven came when people, rest, music and sunlight came. The bar rode heaven and signed its name on the door.

Mark too how the pay tale grows with tiredness, which tells you where it comes from. On bright weeks when sleep held and work ran fair, you thought less of the drawer. The bar sat unopened for days and you never missed it. On black weeks when sleep broke and the boss pressed and the bills piled, the drawer called loud as a bell. If the bar paid true for work done, bright weeks would call loudest, for most work got done then. The reverse calls. The worst weeks shout most because want shouts most when the pinch bites most. You know this curve from your own drawer. Full drawer, hard week. Full heart, quiet drawer. That curve proves pay was a name we gave want.

The day did the work. The bar took the praise.

## THE TIGHT SHOES

We must meet the comfort claim head on, for it holds many kind folk long past sense. Not the pay claim now. The soft claim. It comforts me. It calms me. It holds me when the day shakes.

I do not brush off your shake. I felt it too. Long days fret the nerves. Kids cry. Money thins. News darkens the room. You reach for sweet and for ten minutes the noise drops a notch. You think, there, that soothed me, I was right, sweet comforts. I read it the same way for years, and I tell you flat we both read it wrong.

Picture a man who wears tight shoes from dawn to dusk. Pinch at the toes. Rub at the heel. Leather biting the bone by noon. He limps through his day. He curses the stairs. At night he sits on his bed and kicks the shoes off across the room. Blood runs back to his feet. Air hits the sore skin. He groans with joy. He tells his wife, those two minutes of taking shoes off are the best part of my day, I live for that comfort.

You would laugh kind and tell him, friend, the comfort is not in the shoes, the comfort is in the taking off, and the taking off only delights because the shoes hurt all day. Buy shoes that fit and you can have feet at peace all day with no nightly groan. He would see it at once for shoes. He cannot yet see it for sweet because the pinch sits inside and the taking off looks like taste.

That is your comfort sweet. That is the sugar trap in its tight shoes.

You wear the pinch all day from dose to dose. Mouth primed for the next hit. Head humming low for want. Nerves edged by the dip the last dose laid. Then you take the dose and the hum shuts for ten minutes. Blood seems to ease. Noise seems to drop. You call that drop comfort. It is not comfort laid on. It is pinch lifted off. Relief from a rub the loop itself keeps tying.

Test it on your own day the way I tested it on mine. When does sweet comfort most. Is it not when you feel worst, worn, low, frayed, an hour or two after the last dose, when the pinch bites hardest. When does sweet comfort least. Is it not when you feel well, fed on plain food, rested, among folk you love, when the pinch lies quiet. If sweet gave comfort the way blankets give warmth, it would comfort most when you feel well and add warmth to warmth. Blankets work like that. Baths work like that. Kind words work like that. This so-called comfort only shines against the ache it just fed. That order damns it.

Think of lads before they learn sweet comfort. They fall and graze a knee. They run to mother for arms and voice and lap. Arms comfort. Voice comforts. Lap comforts. No one hands them a bar to mend a grazed knee and no one needs to. Arms do the work. Then we teach them the wrapper tale and by twenty they swear only chocolate holds them when the boss shouts. Did their hearts change, or did the talk change. You know it was the talk. Hearts still mend on arms and voice and rest and air. They learn to ask for tight shoes to enjoy taking them off.

I speak blunt here because blunt serves you. Sweet does not calm the nerves. It frets them then rents you ten minutes off. Pinch, lift, pinch, lift, round and round, each lift named comfort, each pinch blamed on life. That is not comfort. That is rent.

It soothed nothing. It lent the ache.

You ask me, but the ten minutes feel real. I grant they feel real. The man with tight shoes feels real joy for two minutes on his bed. I do not deny his groan. I deny what he pays for it. To buy two minutes of taking off by paying twelve hours of pinch is a fool's trade, and you would tell him so to his face with love. Tell it to yourself with the same love. A comfort you must rebuy each hour while the ache grows month on month is no comfort. True comfort leaves you calmer after. This leaves you edgier after, hungrier after, less held after. Call things by right names and the comfort tale dies.

Take the shoes off for good and keep the evening groan. That is what plain days give, feet at peace all day with no nightly price.

## THE TABLE WITH CANDLES

Now let us stand a breath at the table you fear most, for I would not leave you thinking I dodge it.

Birthday dinner. Low light. Faces you love round wood. Rich cake carried in with candles shaking, all sing, all laugh, one small joke that you are no fun if you pass. You take a slice to keep the hour kind. You eat mousse to avoid standing out while talk runs on. The sweet looks like love itself in that light, and to leave it looks like leaving them.

I keep this glimpse short on purpose. The full hour at that table needs its own room later when your eyes stand stronger, and I will meet it there without blink. For now hold only this much, for it is enough to loosen the wire.

Mark what made that night shine before cake ever came. Who cooked and called you by your old name. Who saved you the chair by the window. Who told the tale of your youth wrong on purpose to make the table roar. Who held your eye across the candles and smiled without words. Music low. Room warm. Bellies full of plain food eaten with thanks. That shine stood before the box opened. The box walked into shine and got named shine.

Ask the two asks that split reward open, and answer straight for you have the proof in your own life. First, if the joy lived in the cake, why does the same cake eaten cold next dawn, alone, by the bin, bring no joy at all, only clag and a thick head. Same cake. Same mouth. No joy. Second, if the love lived in the sweet, why do the nights you remember best keep their glow in memory though you cannot name one bite you took. You remember faces, jokes, songs, light. You cannot taste a crumb. Memory kept what mattered and let the wrapper fall.

The people paid. The sweet rode.

Keep the people loved. Only the sweet gets demoted. That tenderness matters, for I am not asking you to scorn one birthday or one gran or one mother at a bakery door. Scorn nothing warm. Love them harder. See clearer what did what, and the sweet shrinks to its true size, a loud small guest at a great table that would have shone without it.

The table shone. The cake borrowed shine.

## WANTING PAST LIKING

Now see the reward lie from the other end, where the mouth tells the truth though the old voice talks on.

You know this hour too. Pay day bar. Cinema bag. Desk drawer treat after a hard week. You break the corner. First bite sings. Sweet floods. Head lifts a notch. You think, there, deserved, this is mine, I have been good this week. Second bite still sings but lower. Third bite clags. Fourth bite pastes the tongue. By half the bar your jaw aches and your thirst wakes, yet your hand keeps going. You finish to the foil. You lick the crumbs. You feel heavier, thicker, less paid than at first bite, yet tomorrow you will swear the bar paid you.

Hold that hour still with me, for it proves more than ten talks.

If the bar truly paid and pleased, pleasure would grow with each bite or hold even. Good things work like that. Kind talk holds. Music holds. Sun holds. A proper meal satisfies and shuts with thanks. This so-called reward fades bite by bite while want stays loud. Like dies early. Want marches on. You finish without liking, driven not by joy in the mouth but by pull in the head. That split gives the game away, and you can see it once you look straight.

"I need something sweet." "treat when you deserve one." "I have been good this week." You have said all three. I said all three. Mark what the lines share. Each one points at a feeling, not at a taste. Need. Deserve. Good. None of them praise flavour. None of them name hunger. Each confesses the want came first and the sweet came second to answer it. That order damns the reward tale. True rewards get chosen for themselves. False rewards get grabbed because want shouts.

Think of your own drawer with a cold eye. When did you enjoy the bar most. First two bites, always first two. When did you want it most. Last six, when taste had dulled and mouth felt thick, want still drove the hand. If reward lived in taste, want would die as taste died. It does not die. It grows. That growth proves want runs apart from like. Want is the loop calling for its hourly ticket. Like is the mouth tiring of the flood. You obey the call and blame the mouth, and the loop banks another day.

You say to me, but I chose it, no one forced my hand. I hear the pride under that line, for choice matters to grown folk. I felt it too. Only ask with me, what choice takes without hunger, tastes without joy past bite three, finishes past comfort, then mourns the empty foil it just emptied. Hungry men choose food and stop with thanks. Paid men take pay and feel paid. Trapped men take a fix and plan the next while they chew. You stood chewing bar one while eyeing bar two in the drawer. I stood there too. We all stood there. That forward lean is not choice. That forward lean is the loop feeding itself while choice watches.

The fact is plain though the old talk paints it deep. Want outlives like because want belongs to the loop and like belongs to the mouth, and the mouth tires long before the loop sleeps. Once you see that split you cannot unsee it. Every finished packet past joy, every foil licked though the tongue felt sick, every cinema bag emptied though the film held your heart, testifies the same. The reward did not please to the end. It pulled to the end.

It pleased at first bite. It pulled to the last.

Think of the cinema to see the split work in the dark. Lights drop. Music swells. Hearts lift at the brave face on the screen. The bag crackles by your ear. First handful sings with the film. Second handful still sings. By the middle chase your mouth feels dry and your fingers feel sticky, yet your hand dips again though your eyes never leave the screen. You leave with a sore tongue and a full bag remembered as empty, and you swear the bag made the night. I sat in that row too. I know that dip. Only ask with me, what made your chest beat fast at the brave part, the sugar or the tale. You know it was the tale. The bag only kept your jaw busy while your heart did the work. That busy jaw proves nothing but pull.

See too how the pull dresses as need to keep its pay. Tired calls for a bar. Cross talk calls for a bar. Boredom calls for a bar. Good news calls for a bar. Bad news calls for a bar. Pay day calls for a bar. Friday calls for a bar. A pay that answers all hours answers none. True pay fits its hour. Rest fits tired. Food fits hunger. Company fits lonely. Sun fits gloom. A bar that claims to fit all fits none, and you know it because you live the misfit. Tired stays tired under the flash. Hunger stays hungry under the sweet. Lonely stays lonely in a full mouth. Only want gets fed, and want wakes hungrier.

That is the sugar trap in reward dress, not fuel now but pay, not lift now but love, same loop, same pinch, same pull, new mask.

The mask paid nothing. The hour paid all.

## THE OCCASION PAID

Let me gather the rooms and set them side by side, for here the whole turn stands in one picture.

Bakery door. Pay-day bag. Tin from England. Friday drawer. Cinema row. Table with candles. Music low. Sun through glass. Rest with feet up. Laugh that airs the chest. Praise that steadies the hands.

You thought sweet bound these into joy. I tell you flat it bound nothing. Joy bound them. People bound them. Pause bound them. Work done bound them. Sweet stood near each and took the bow.

Isolate the sweet the way you would lift one thread from a weave to see what holds. Take the bakery walk without sweet and keep the hand. Does love fail. It does not. The hand still warms. The laugh still lifts. Take Friday without drawer and keep feet up, tea, talk, sun. Does the week fail to close. It does not. Shoulders still drop. Head still clears. Take the film without bag and keep dark hush, bright faces, held breath at the brave part. Does the night fail. It does not. Heart still beats fast at the right scene. Take the birthday without slice and keep faces, songs, light. Does love fail. It does not. Love stands though cake sits uncut.

Now take the sweet without the rooms and see what stays. Bar at sink alone. Cake cold by bin. Bag in traffic. Tin prised open with no gran, no fire, no talk. What heaven arrives. None. Mouth floods then clags. Head lifts then thickens. Hand reaches for more though joy never came. That single test kills the reward tale stone dead, and no talk can raise it. Joy without sweet holds. Sweet without joy falls flat.

You fear a grey life without reward sweet. I know that fear. I carried it too. Generous folk fear most, for they pay others in sweet as well as self, and to leave sweet looks like leaving kindness. Hear me as one who stood where you stand. Colour never lived in the wrapper. Colour lived in faces, food, air, movement, rest, music, light. Those stay. Those grow brighter when no loud hit shouts over them. Think of the plain table you already love without knowing it, bread torn, soup steaming, fruit sharp and cold, talk running long. No one photographs that table, yet all linger at it. That linger tells you what feast means. You do not lose the feast by leaving BAD SUGAR. You find the feast the sweet kept shouting down.

The occasion paid. The sweet took the wage.

## SUMMARY

- You now see the comfort and pay you felt from sweet belonged to people, rest, music and sunlight while the sweet rode along and took the praise.
- You now know wanting outlives liking because the loop pulls to the last bite long after the mouth tires, which proves reward was pull not pleasure.
- You now believe loving hours lose nothing when the sweet leaves them because the table shone before the box opened and memory keeps faces not wrappers.
