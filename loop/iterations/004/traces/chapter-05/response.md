### WHY "I DESERVE A TREAT" FEELS LIKE LOVE

Do you hear that voice?

It is soft. It is kind. After a long day, after a win, after holding everything together, it says, very sweetly:

"I deserve a treat."

Or its close cousins: "I deserve one." And, "I've been good this week so I deserve it."

Do you recognise those words in your own voice?

We all do. I did too. We say them without thinking. We feel entitled, tender, even virtuous. We have been good. We have earned it. Surely love should give something. Surely effort should be paid.

And on a low evening, the same soft voice says something even tenderer. You know the line. You have said it to yourself in exactly these words:

"It helps me concentrate / cope with stress / comforts me / cheers me up"

That whole string is how we bundle stress, focus, comfort and cheer into one dose. Did you notice you use one sentence for four different jobs?

We will demolish only part of that line in this chapter. We will demolish the reward story — "I deserve a treat / I deserve one / I've been good this week so I deserve it" — and the comfort/cheer story — the "comforts me / cheers me up" part. We will explicitly leave the "helps me concentrate / cope with stress" part alone. That facet belongs to Chapter 06, where we will test it on its own ground. Is that fair?

Good. One job at a time.

For now, stay with P-03, the comfort eater in all of us. The person who uses BAD SUGAR as reward, as love, as deserved pay-off. Does that sound like you sometimes?

We were told this is self-love. Is it?

Think of how we reward a child. We give attention. We clap. We make time. We say, you did wonderfully. The child feels seen. Would you tell that child, the only way to mark your pride is with a dose that does nothing FOR you?

We would not. We know reward is attention, pause, praise. So why do we give ourselves a dose?

Because we were taught to miscredit the moment. We lived through a warm, real reward — a pause after effort, a kind word, a break, company — and BAD SUGAR was there at the same time. Like a gatecrasher in a photo, it stood in the corner and later claimed it was the occasion.

Have you ever doubted that?

Let me ask you two trap questions. Answer honestly, in your head.

If BAD SUGAR were truly a reward, would you need to tell yourself you deserve it?

And if BAD SUGAR truly comforted and cheered you, would you feel lower an hour later than before you had it?

You know the answers. You have lived them.

The fact is reward is not a thing you swallow. Reward is a thing you receive: rest, recognition, affection, relief from pressure. BAD SUGAR cannot give you those. It can only ride along while you give them to yourself.

Shall we test that? Not in theory. In your own memories.

### THE CINEMA TEST: THE SAME REWARD WITHOUT THE DOSE

Picture this with me. Vividly. Use your senses.

It is Saturday. You have had a good week. Work went well. You kept promises. You sorted things you had been putting off. You feel that quiet glow of having been good. You say to yourself, correctly, "I've been good this week so I deserve it." You decide to celebrate.

You go to the cinema. Lights low. Big screen. You are with someone you like, or happily alone with your own company at last. You have time. No hurry. You breathe out. The week drops off your shoulders. You feel cared for, by yourself and by the evening.

And, as we have all been taught, you add chocolate. One of the most common lived patterns we hear is exactly this: "celebrating, rewarding myself, going to the cinema, etc with chocolate." Sweet as earned love. Sweet as pay. Does that sound familiar?

Now, keep everything. Keep the glow. Keep the company. Keep the lights low and the story on screen. Keep the pause you earned. Keep the pride. Remove only the dose. No BAD SUGAR in your hand. Nothing sweet to unwrap.

Does the reward disappear?

Be honest.

Do you still feel proud? Do you still feel the relief of a week completed? Do you still enjoy the film? Do you still feel you have marked the moment with kindness?

You do. The reward remains. Because the reward was never the dose.

Now run the variable the other way. Keep the dose. Remove the reward.

Bad day. Nothing went right. You feel flat, unseen, a little raw. You did not have a good week. You do not feel you deserve praise. But you give yourself BAD SUGAR anyway, exactly the same chocolate, same box, same cinema seat, same sofa. Does it feel like a reward now?

No. It feels like a fix. A hit taken in a low mood. It is eating, not celebrating. You can feel the difference instantly. Same dose. Different moment. No reward.

Do you see what that proves?

The dose did not create the reward. The moment did. We were simply taught to credit the dose for what the moment gave us.

Ask the inversion plainly. Who really rewarded you that Saturday?

Was it BAD SUGAR? Or was it you — giving yourself time, attention, company, rest, and the quiet word, well done?

It was you. It was always you.

How much of that chocolate did you taste after the third bite? Really taste? Or did you taste relief, warmth, permission to stop trying for an hour?

We call it "I deserve a treat." But what we mean, in honest language, is "I deserve a pause and some praise." BAD SUGAR hears that and says, I'll take the credit.

And we let it. Why?

Because the Treat Spell taught us to. That spell is not a small physical craving. That is the brainwashing that interprets any warm feeling as proof that BAD SUGAR gave it to us. We will name that spell fully in a later chapter. For now, notice its fingerprint: wrappers and smells can fire wanting before a single bite, even when we are not hungry. That cue-driven wanting has been shown clearly in animal studies, where cue and reactivity circuits light up before the dose. That is a rodent finding, not proof of human physiologic withdrawal, and I will not claim it as a diagnosis in you. I mention it only as a token: the wanting can fire from a cue, not from a need. The cinema foyer smell is not love. It is a bell.

Do you want to try that same test at home, where reward lives most nights?

### THE SOFA TEST: THE SAME COMFORT WITHOUT THE DOSE

Now picture the other ritual. The tender one.

It is evening. Light low. You are a little low yourself. Nothing dramatic. Just the ache of a day that took a lot out of you. You want to feel soothed, steadied, cheered. You want comfort.

We say the line: "It helps me concentrate / cope with stress / comforts me / cheers me up." Today we isolate the last part: comforts me, cheers me up. Forget concentration and coping with stress for now. That will have its own stripping exercise in Chapter 06. Does that make sense?

Good. So: comforts me. Cheers me up.

What do you actually do when you seek that comfort?

You stop. You sit. You make the room softer. You put on something familiar. You give yourself permission to not solve anything for an hour. You breathe. You are warm. You are still. Perhaps someone you love is nearby. Perhaps the house is quiet and that quiet itself is kindness.

And, as we were all taught, you add BAD SUGAR. Biscuits. Ice cream. Something sweet to mark the shift from effort to ease.

Now do the isolation. Keep every comfort channel. Keep the stop. Keep the soft light. Keep the blanket, the sofa, the show, the quiet, the company, the permission, the sigh. Remove only the dose. Eat real food if you are hungry — food eaten as food, not as a hit. Otherwise just be there, comforted by the occasion you created.

Does comfort remain? Does cheer return, at least a little?

Yes. Gently, but truly. Because comfort was the pause. Cheer was the kindness and the rest.

Now flip it. Keep the dose. Remove every comfort channel. No pause. No soft light. No blanket. No show. No permission. Stand at the kitchen counter, tired, bright light, still on duty, and feed yourself the same sweet dose quickly, alone, while thinking about tomorrow's list. Does it comfort you? Does it cheer you?

No. It adds a flat, slightly restless edge. A mild, insecure hum. You feel less soothed, not more.

So what comforted you on the sofa?

Was it BAD SUGAR, a genuine pleasure, treat or fuel that you would be deprived without? Or was it the rest you gave yourself, plus the company and the warmth, while BAD SUGAR sneaked a ride and asked to be thanked?

It was rest. It was warmth. It was you being kind to you.

We confuse two things because they arrive together. The occasion soothes. The dose follows. We credit the dose for the soothing. That is the whole con in one habit.

And the after-feeling proves the miscredit.

When true comfort has happened — real rest, real affection, real relief — you feel steadier an hour later. Calmer. Lighter. When BAD SUGAR has been the supposed comfort, how do you feel an hour later? Often lower. Flatter. A little foggy. A little more in need of something.

If the dose were a genuine comfort and cheer, would it predict lower mood later?

The fact is we have a large, long-running population study that helps here, honestly stated. Among men followed over time, higher intake of sugary sweets was linked to higher later odds of a common mental disorder — feeling low, anxious, not yourself — even after checking that low mood did not simply drive sweet eating first. This is observational. In men only. Not proof that one sweet causes low mood in you or in anyone, and not proof for women or for everyone. But its shape matters: the daily "treat" predicted feeling worse later, not feeling relieved. That is the opposite of what a genuine comfort would do. A true comfort leaves you better. This pattern leaves you lower.

Do you feel that click? The thing you reached for to cheer you up is associated, over time, with needing more cheering up. Not because you are weak. Because the lift borrows and the bill arrives later.

And still we say, "I deserve one." Of course you deserve one — one what? One pause. One clap on the back. One hour of not having to be strong. You deserve that richly. Why pay for it with a dose that does nothing FOR you?

It is doing plenty TO you. It is doing nothing FOR you.

Write that again in your mind, not as a slogan but as audit result. The TO column: the need for more, the flat hour after, the extra wanting triggered by wrappers. The FOR column: what remains when you strip the dose and keep the pause? Nothing that was not already there.

Again.

It is doing plenty TO you. It is doing nothing FOR you.

That is not deprivation. That is seeing.

### WHAT REALLY REWARDED YOU AND WHAT REALLY COMFORTED YOU

Let's land this cleanly, without hedging, without willpower.

We entered believing two soft stories: sugar is love, reward, deserved pay-off; and sugar comforts and cheers me when I am low. We spoke both in your own dialect, verbatim, because you must hear your own voice to test it:

"I deserve a treat / I deserve one / I've been good this week so I deserve it"

and

"It helps me concentrate / cope with stress / comforts me / cheers me up" — of which this chapter has tested only the comforts/cheers facet, explicitly reserving concentrate/cope-with-stress for Chapter 06.

We tested both with the isolating variable. Same ritual, dose removed — reward and comfort remain. Same dose, ritual removed — reward and comfort vanish. The variable is not the dose. The variable is the occasion, the pause, the praise, the company, the permission.

So we reassign credit where it belongs.

You rewarded you. You comforted you.

BAD SUGAR did not.

Do you see how that removes sacrifice?

If BAD SUGAR never was a genuine pleasure, treat or fuel — those three words as one collapsed promise — then what are you giving up by not having it with the film or on the sofa? Nothing. You keep the film. You keep the sofa. You keep the pride and the soft light and the laugh and the hour to yourself. You lose only the flat after-feeling and the next wanting.

One killer pair, then a question.

We did not love BAD SUGAR. We loved the moments and BAD SUGAR stole the love.

We were not comforted by BAD SUGAR. We were comforted despite it.

Is there any reward in a dose that cannot reward you on a bad day and is not needed on a good day?

No. That is not a reward. That is a tax on rewards you already earned.

Do you feel the spell weaken, just a little, around love and comfort triggers?

Good. We will weaken it further when we meet stress and concentration separately, and again when we strip the sunny dessert table down to sun and company. For now, let this verdict stand as settled belief, not as coaching.

The treat was never the sweet. The sweet was never the cheer.

The treat was time. The cheer was rest. The love was yours.

Keep your ledger open. In the FOR column, under reward and under comfort/cheer, write: nothing added by the dose. In your mind, replace "I deserve a treat" with the truth: I deserve a pause and some praise, and I will give it to myself cleanly, without paying a dose that does nothing FOR me.

You have absolutely not lost anything. You have seen through a con that charged you twice for a kindness you gave yourself once.

Shall we prove the same for energy next?
