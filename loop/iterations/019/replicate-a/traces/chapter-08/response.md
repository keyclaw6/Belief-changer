Chapter 8
WHO BUILT THE WANT

**IN THIS CHAPTER**
The supermarket aisle at eye level — The mirror that says personality — The open cell and the pacing lion

*You did not invent this want. It was built in you, and the fear you feel about leaving it was built with it.*

**THE AISLE THAT TAUGHT YOU TO WANT**

Walk with me down an ordinary aisle.

You went in for bread. At eye level, bright packs shout in cartoon voices. The family size glows a little cheaper per bite. At the till, where you must queue with tired children, small bars wait like helpful friends. Nothing here is accidental. Crunch tuned, melt tuned, sweetness tuned to the exact point that lights the mouth and leaves it asking.

We lived inside that design for years without seeing it. We told ourselves we chose it.

Did you? Think as an investigator now.

When did you first decide that a dull afternoon required sugary somethings to get me through? Did you weigh evidence and elect craving as a philosophy? Or did packets, adverts, kind parents, tired jokes — treat when you feel you deserve one — elect it for you, long before you could vote?

Ask one honest question:

If this want were truly yours, why does it speak in their slogans?

Ask another:

If BAD SUGAR were truly normal because everyone eats it, why do you need a book to defend eating it?

And a third that finishes it:

Would a child who had never been trained by that aisle pace and whine for a fix at 3pm — or did the aisle train the pace?

You know the answers. The mouth waters before the bite because the wrapper taught it. The hour calls because the hour was paired with a dose a thousand times. A larger bar is needed now for the same brief glow, and that need for more leaves you flat, foggy and never satisfied. The bell rings, the mouth answers. A rung bell orders nothing. Only belief marches the feet to the shelf.

This is not your weakness. This is manufacture.

On straightforward questionnaires about highly palatable food, roughly 14 in every 100 adults and 12 in every 100 children score in that addiction-like range — a figure close to alcohol and tobacco. That is not a label on you, only proof you are not odd and not weak. The trap is common because the building of it was common.

I was conned the same way. We all were. Armies of clever, well-paid people spent decades learning how to make a dose hit faster, fade faster, and call you back sooner, then paid for sunshine adverts to call that call-back love. The fact is the daily sweet predicts a lower mood later rather than lifting mood now — seen clearly in men followed over years — and I am not telling you a bar dooms you to low mood, only that the rescuer story runs the wrong way. What claims to cheer you dims you after. In labs where sweet access comes and goes, animals fall into bingeing pattern on that schedule. I mention that only to show the shape of scheduled hits, never to label you.

So be angry at the builders, never at yourself. The Sweet Con was installed from outside. The Sugar Trap is an engineered room you walked into as a child holding someone's hand.

See it built and you stop defending it as yours.

**ARE YOU THE ADDICTIVE TYPE?**

“I have no off-switch. My family are all the same. I am just that personality.”

I hear you. It feels true because the trap taught you to read the evidence backwards.

The fact is shared traits among heavy users are the result of living in the same trap, not the cause of entering it. Put clever, steady, sociable people on the roller coaster of lift then drop, dose then gnaw, promise then fog, and they all develop the same restlessness, the same short term lift then fast drop, the same “I feel like i cant stop” once the packet is open. Take them out of the trap and the traits thin with the dosing. The personality did not summon the doses. The doses rehearsed the personality.

Look at your own life for proof.

Do you binge fruit until the bowl is empty and hide the skins in shame? Do you set rules, break rules, and restart on Monday for roast dinners? Did your so-called addictive streak exist before BAD SUGAR became daily — or did it arrive dose by dose, year by year, as the loop tightened?

It is the other way around.

And even if you were the most cue-sensitive person in your street — even if wrappers shout louder to you than to your neighbour — the way out stays the same. Bells do not compel legs. Belief does. Correct the belief and the most sensitive bell becomes mere noise, then silence.

You were never the addictive type. You were the advertised-to type, the scheduled-dose type, the conned type. We all were.

There is nothing broken in you to fix. There is a story in you to drop.

**THE OPEN CELL AND THE PACING LION**

If want was built, why does leaving feel frightening?

Because the trap holds you with two ropes that look like wisdom. Together they make the tug-of-war of craving and fear.

One rope pulls forward: fear of failing. “What if I cannot do evenings without a treat? What if I cave at the birthday table and prove I am hopeless?”

One rope pulls back: fear of succeeding. “What if life without sweetness is grey? What if I am dull company without dessert to share? What if I lose my little reward for getting through hard days?”

Both ropes burn the hands. Both feel like you thinking. Both belong to the trap.

Picture a cell with its door wide ajar. Daylight on the corridor floor. No guard. The prisoner sits on the bunk, clutching the blanket, begging to stay. Outside feels too bright, too open, too unsure. “At least in here I know the hours,” he says. “At least I know the taste of the gruel.”

Picture beside him a lion born in a travelling cage, now freed on grass. The gate stands open. The lion paces three steps, turns, paces back, wearing an invisible groove by the open gate — not from hunger, not from locks, but from habit. Passers-by think he loves the cage. He only knows the pacing.

That is us at the edge of freedom. The door is open. The pacing is habit, not need.

Succumb to the fear of failing and you guarantee the very failure you fear, by keeping the belief that failure is likely. Cling to the fear of succeeding and you guarantee the grey life you fear, by keeping the belief that joy lived in the dose. Stay in and you pay with gnaw and fog for the rest of your life. Walk out and you live clear and easy for the rest of your life.

Which rest-of-life do you want?

The wobble after the door opens proves nothing except that pacing takes a day or two to fade. The Nibbler squeaks. The bell rings once. You smile because you know the ropes. The thought crosses, you greet it with relief that you no longer obey it, and the groove grass grows over.

Your body copes with an accidental crumb the way a seatbelt copes with a jolt — there for accidents, never for steering toward them.

So hear no other voice on this. Friends will mean well and speak from inside their own cells. Articles will sell you moderation, willpower weeks, “I’ve managed to be good this week” rules that rebuild the loudspeaker you just switched off. Every outside method re-installs the idea that the dose held value and that you must wrestle it. You know now the dose held no value and there is nothing to wrestle.

**8. IGNORE ANY ADVICE THAT CONFLICTS WITH THIS METHOD**
Other voices rebuild the Con.

**SUMMARY**

* The ache for sweetness was engineered by design, packets and praise, not born in your character.
* Needing more for the same brief glow and salivating at wrappers and hours are learned trap features, not proof of compulsion.
* Low mood follows the daily treat rather than being fixed by it, which exposes the reward story as backwards.
* So-called addictive traits are the footprint of the loop, not its cause, and they fade with the loop.
* Fear of failing and fear of succeeding are both ropes held by the trap, and pacing by an open door is habit dying, not need speaking.
