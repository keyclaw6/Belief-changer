Chapter 2
ARE YOU REALLY CHOOSING THIS?

IN THIS CHAPTER
The garage door at midnight. The mint tin and the sprayed jacket. The hand that lights before you decide. The first draw after an hour without. The two quiet partners.

*You did not choose to stay a smoker — you were caught, and the catch keeps you lighting.*

## The Man Outside His Own House

I want you to stand with me for a moment outside your own house.

It is late. The kitchen is warm behind you. Your partner is inside, perhaps the children are asleep upstairs. You say you are just taking the rubbish out, or getting some air, or checking the car. You slip into the garage, or down the side passage, or onto the cold step by the back door. You cup your hand around the tip so the glow does not show at the window.

You smoke quickly, not with pleasure, but with one ear listening for the door. When you finish you stamp it, hide the end, chew a mint, spray your jacket and your hands, wave the air with your palm, and breathe into your sleeve to check. You Febreeze the hell out of yourself before you go back in. You practise your face in the dark.

We have all done that walk. I have done it in rain that soaked my socks, in snow that burned my fingers, in summer when the evening was lovely and I still chose the alley behind the bins because I did not want the look.

I stood once for twenty minutes under a dripping carport at my own birthday party. Music inside. Friends inside. My own house. And I stood outside with water running down my neck to finish a dose I did not even want, because I did not want to be seen wanting it. When I came back in, my wife smiled and said I smelled of fresh air. I smiled back as if I had been clever.

I am not telling you this to make you feel low. I have only warmth for the man or woman on that cold step, because I was him. I know the care you take not to be smelled. I know the little lies you tell — not cruel lies, tired lies — to buy five minutes alone with a paper tube. I know the mint tin rattling in the pocket, the spray in the glove box, the mints in the desk drawer, the window opened a crack in January while you shiver and tell yourself this is your pleasure.

Now let me ask you plainly, as a friend who stood on that same step.

Does that look like choice?

If cigarettes are your pleasure, your friend, your right, why do you hide them like a fault? No one hides a cup of tea in the garage. No one sprays his coat after eating a biscuit. No one lies to his wife about having breathed fresh air. Choice does not need Febreeze.

You may answer, as I used to: I hide it to keep the peace. My partner nags. The kids should not see. Work does not allow it.

Listen to what you are really saying. You are saying: I do something I value so much that I will stand in the cold to do it, and I am so ashamed of doing it that I will lie about it minutes later. Can both of those be freedom?

Think of the planning that goes with the hiding. Counting how many are left before a long flight. Stepping out of a restaurant between courses into the rain while your food cools. Leaving a film before the end to stand with strangers under a metal shelter. Choosing the outdoor table in October, not because you love October, but because you need the dose. Checking the packet at midnight and doing quiet arithmetic: will these last till morning, can I make the shop, should I borrow one.

A free man does not plan his evening around a packet. A free man does not leave warmth and company to serve a need and then rush back pretending he was never gone.

The fact is, a free chooser does not live divided like that. A free chooser lights where he likes, enjoys where he likes, and goes inside undivided. The closet smoker is not free. He is serving two masters — the wish to smoke and the wish not to be seen smoking — and pleasing neither.

That division is your first clear sight of the nicotine trap.

You entered it as a teenager on false information, copying smokers who looked certain and grown. You stayed in it because the trap removed your choice while leaving you the story that you still choose. That story is precious to the trap. While you believe you choose, you never question why a chooser would hide.

## The Confidence Trick

“I choose to smoke. I like it. I could stop if I wanted.”

I know that voice. I used it for thirty years. I used it loudly in pubs when a non-smoker raised an eyebrow. I used it quietly to myself after the third dose before breakfast. It is the voice of a defensive chooser, and I have complete warmth for him, because defending is what we do when we sense we are not free but cannot yet see how we lost our freedom.

Let me put it to you gently, as one who stood where you stand, packet in pocket.

You did not choose to become a smoker. You chose to copy the older boys, to look grown, to join in, to try one. You coughed on that first one. Your eyes watered. Your head spun. Your body said, clearly, no. Would you call that enjoyment? A child tasting his first beer pulls the same face, and we laugh because we know the taste is not pleasure, it is poison the body rightly rejects.

We persisted. Not because it was good, but because everyone around us did it, because the grown world seemed to promise calm and belonging in smoke. That was phoney information. We were conned before we knew what a con was.

A con does not need a villain in a dark coat. It needs only false promise repeated often enough that a young mind takes it for truth. Films taught it. Break rooms taught it. Fathers and mothers on doorsteps taught it without meaning to. The world held up a paper tube and said: this steadies you, this rewards you, this makes you one of us.

Once the first weeks of coughing passed, the trap closed. And here is the clever part of the trap: it left you the language of choice after it took the fact of choice away.

You say my cigarette, my break, my moment. Listen to that word my. My implies ownership. Do you own it, or does it own your feet? Your feet carry you to the garage in the rain against your own comfort. Your hand searches your coat before your mind has spoken. Your evening bends around shop hours. What kind of possession possesses its owner?

If a man told you he freely chose to stand in the cold every night, to pay thousands over the years for the privilege, to cough each morning and hide the smell from his own children, you would not call him free. You would say something was driving him.

Something is. We will give it names shortly, in passing, so you can see it when it moves. For now it is enough to see the shape: a good person, conned as a youngster, now repeating what he would never have chosen had he seen it clear.

It is the other way around. You do not smoke because you choose. You say you choose because you smoke.

## The Hand That Moves Before You

Watch yourself today, not with dread, with curiosity. You promised me an open mind, and this is where that open mind pays.

The phone goes down after a sharp exchange. The kettle boils. The meal ends. The car stops at lights. The pub door opens to the garden. Notice what your hand does.

It moves before you decide. It is in your pocket, at the pack, tapping, turning the dose, finding the lighter, before any thought you would call thinking has happened. By the time you say to yourself, I fancy one, the cigarette is already lit.

I used to catch myself with the thing in my mouth and no memory of deciding. I would think: did I choose that? I could not point to the moment of choosing. There was only a pull and a movement and then smoke. Then, a second later, the mind hurried to supply a reason — break, stress, boredom, habit — like a secretary inventing minutes for a meeting that never happened.

Ask yourself three honest questions, and answer only to yourself.

If you truly choose each cigarette, why do you light many you do not even want, that taste stale and leave you coughing, and then light the next one the same way? A chooser who dislikes a thing stops choosing it. Have you ever lit one, drawn twice, thought this tastes foul, and smoked it to the end anyway? What choice is that?

If you truly smoke because you enjoy it, why do you need this book in your hands at all? A man happily pursuing a hobby does not buy a book on how to stop the hobby. No fisherman buys How To Stop Fishing Easily. The fact you are reading proves something in you already knows you are not choosing.

And if you are in charge, why does the hand move first and the reason arrive afterwards? In charge means the decision comes first. Here the movement comes first and the excuse is fitted after, like a coat thrown over what has already happened.

I know this is hard to accept. Your mind will say: but I do enjoy some of them, but I only smoke when I want, but my friend smokes five a day and stops when he likes. Bring those thoughts. Do not push them away.

Have you ever tried to prove your freedom by going a few hours without, only to think about smoking for the whole few hours? Is that freedom or is that a leash stretched a little longer? Have you ever told yourself you will have one less today, and spent the day bargaining with yourself about which one to miss, wanting each one more because one was forbidden? Does a free chooser bargain with himself about tea?

We were all conned in the same way. You chose the first experiment on phoney information. Repetition did its quiet work, and soon the choice was gone while the language of choice remained.

See that today without trying to change it. See the reach. See the light before the thought. That seeing loosens more than any struggle ever did.

## Heaven For A Second, A Smoker For The Day

“I love smoking. I just hate being a smoker.”

Have you heard yourself say that? I have heard it from hundreds of smokers, and I said it myself for years. It sounds like a contradiction. It is the clearest truth you own.

What do you love, exactly? Press it for detail, as an investigator.

Is it the taste through the day? You know it is not. Through the day your mouth is dry, your throat is scratchy, your fingers smell, your clothes carry it into rooms you have not entered. Food tastes blunted. Mornings begin with clearing and coughing. The tenth dose of the day burns. The last one at night leaves an ashtray mouth you carry to bed. No one calls that heaven at three in the afternoon when the throat is raw.

Is it the whole cigarette? Smoke it with full attention next time you light. Not while talking, not while driving, not while thinking of something else. Just taste it. The first draw after a gap feels like relief. The second is duller. By the middle it is hot paper and stale ash. By the end you are glad to put it out. If the thing itself were heaven, would attention make it worse?

What you call heaven lives in a very small window. The strike of the match. The first draw after an hour without. For a few seconds there is a rush, a settling, a sense of ah, that is better. Then the stick burns on, the taste turns, the chest tightens, and you are left with ash and the vague wish you had not needed it, until the next pull begins to build.

That little flare at lighting is real. I am not telling you that you imagine it. You feel it as truly as you feel warmth from a fire. To deny what you feel would be to lie to you, and I will never do that.

What I want you to see, gently and for now, is how brief it is and how much it costs to rent it.

A moment of relief at the light, bought with being a smoker for the whole day. The hiding. The planning. The counting how many left till morning. The stairs that leave you puffing. The yellow on the fingers. The money burned week after week. The divided self on the garage step. The cough you clear before anyone hears. The choice of seats near doors, jobs with outdoor areas, holidays planned around where you can light.

Would a free pleasure need that bargain? Would you call any other pleasure a pleasure if you had to be chained to it all day for three seconds of lighting?

You love the lighting because after a gap the body cries to be fed, and the first puff quiets the cry. You hate being a smoker because for the rest of the day you carry the chain that makes the cry certain. Both feelings are true. They belong together. The heaven does not prove the cigarette gives. It proves how tightly the taking has hold.

Keep that picture. We will look at each supposed gift in daylight in the pages ahead, and you will see where the credit really belongs. For now it is enough to see that the enjoyment you name at the light does not stretch across the day. It cannot justify the day.

## The Taken-Care-Of Feeling

“If anything stressful happens in my life, a cigarette takes care of it.”

That sentence will be familiar. The argument on the phone. The bill on the mat. The child crying while the dinner burns. Your hand is already reaching while the problem is still ringing in your ears. You step out, you light, you draw, and within a minute your shoulders drop a fraction. You go back in able to speak more calmly.

I am not mocking that relief. I have felt it in traffic, in offices, at hospital corridors. It arrives quickly and it feels like help. If I told you that you do not feel it, you would rightly close this book.

I ask only that you notice two things side by side, as an investigator, not as a judge.

First, the feeling arrives and is welcomed. Second, the thing that stressed you sits exactly where you left it. The bill is still on the mat. The argument is still unmended. The sick relative is still sick. The deadline still waits. The cigarette took care of your feeling for a minute. It touched nothing it claimed to fix.

How can something that takes care of stress leave every cause of stress untouched?

Hold that question lightly. I do not want you to fight your stress moments or to test yourself by refusing a dose when you are pressed. All I want now is for you to allow that the credit you give may be false even while the feeling is felt. Both can be true: you feel calmer, and the calmer was not care.

A con does not work by giving nothing. It works by giving a small sensation and taking the credit for a larger cure. The hand reaches, the puff quiets something for a minute, and the mind writes takes care of it across the whole event.

Watch that writing happen in you today without trying to change it. See the reach. See the minute of quiet. See the untouched problem waiting on the desk. That watching is already the beginning of escape.

Do you notice how quickly you reach even when you know the problem will remain? That speed is not character. That speed is the trap moving your hand before your judgment can speak. We all learned that speed together.

## Two Quiet Partners Keeping You Lit

You may be thinking: very well, I see the hiding and the hand. But why can I not simply see it and stop seeing it? Why does the pull return an hour later as if I had learned nothing?

Because a trap that relied on hiding alone would fail. It needs two quiet partners working together, and once you can name them in passing you will never be mystified again.

The first is small and physical. Call it the Nipper.

The Nipper is the tiny creature left by each dose, nipping lightly when the dose fades. You know him well, though you never named him. He is not pain. He is not illness. He is an empty, slightly restless, slightly edgy little tug somewhere in the chest and stomach, a sense of something missing that you cannot quite place. A non-smoker does not feel him because nothing created him. A smoker feels him an hour after the last dose because the last dose created him.

He is trivial. A baby fretting for a minute. Left unfed he starves in days, and you would hardly notice him if you did not misread him. But misread him you do, because of the second partner.

The second is large and invisible. Call it the Smokescreen.

The Smokescreen is the lifelong brainwashing that tells you what the tug means. Parents, friends, films, packets, pubs, breaks at work — all taught you the same lesson: that tug means you need a cigarette, that a cigarette will pleasure you, steady you, reward you, join you in. The Smokescreen feeds the Nipper by interpreting his tiny nip as proof you cannot live without the very thing that caused him.

Do you see the partnership? The Nipper creates an empty, slightly restless, slightly edgy little tug. The Smokescreen says: that means light up. You light up. The tug quiets for a few minutes. The Smokescreen says: you see, it helped. The next dose guarantees the next tug. Round and round for years.

We fell for it together. I fed my Nipper for thirty years while my Smokescreen told me I was enjoying myself, calming myself, rewarding myself. I believed I was a smoker by nature. I was a non-smoker kept nipping and kept fooled.

You do not have to fight either partner. You do not have to be strong or clever. You only have to see them as they pass through your day. When the tug comes this afternoon, you will hear the old reading — I want one — and underneath it you will begin to hear the truer reading — the Nipper nips and the Smokescreen speaks. That seeing is enough for now.

This is the nicotine trap in its simplest form: a small physical nip plus a big false belief, keeping a good person lighting what he would never choose if he saw it clear.

Notice how the two explain what choice alone cannot explain. Choice cannot explain why you light the stale one you dislike. The Nipper plus the Smokescreen can: the nip demands quiet, the brainwashing names the dose as quiet. Choice cannot explain why one drag at lighting feels like heaven and the rest of the day feels like chain. The partnership can: the first puff stills the nip for seconds, the rest of the day rebuilds it.

You are not weak. You were never weak. You were conned, then nipped, then fooled about the nip. Who would blame a man for scratching when he was told the itch meant pleasure?

## Smoke As Normal While You Watch

You came to this chapter defending choice. Look what choice has required of you: cold steps, quick lies, mints and sprays, a hand that moves before you, heaven that lasts seconds and a chain that lasts the day, problems untouched while the feeling is credited.

A chooser would not live like that. A prisoner lives like that and calls the prison home because he has never seen the door.

I am not asking you to break out by force. Force is what failed you before, and I will never ask it of you. I am asking you to keep watching with open eyes while you continue exactly as before. Let the trap show itself in ordinary daylight. Each recognition loosens it a little more, without misery, without struggle.

Smoke when you want. Do not count. Do not miss one to prove you are serious. Proving only breeds fantasy about the missed one and gloom about the next. The watching needs a clear mind, not a deprived one. A deprived mind cannot investigate; it can only crave.

So here is our second agreement, and it stands beside the first. It protects the watching. Without it you will turn reading into testing yourself, and testing yourself will only stir fantasy and gloom.

2. DON'T STOP OR CUT DOWN YET
Smoke as normal until your last cigarette.

SUMMARY
- Hiding to smoke proves the act is not freely chosen.
- The first try was copying on false promise, not enjoyment.
- The hand moves before the reason, and the reason is fitted after.
- Liking the second of lighting can live beside hating being a smoker all day.
- The calmer feeling after a puff leaves the cause untouched.
- A small tug plus a false reading keeps the lighting going.
- Watching the trap clearly matters more now than changing the routine.
