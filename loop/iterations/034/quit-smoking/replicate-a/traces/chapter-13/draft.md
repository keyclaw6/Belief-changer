Chapter 13
EMPTY HANDS, FULL DAYS

IN THIS CHAPTER
- Cold kitchen, kettle, feet on lino before light
- Shop queue, closed shutter, milk and bread in the basket
- Work pause, cleared plate, evening chairs outside

*The day you feared would feel thin tastes fuller with no paper in it.*

The Kettle Boils First

You wake to the same alarm in the same flat with the same light at the window. For years we woke the other way, feet had not touched the floor before the hand found the pack, throat still closed from the night, first dose taken on empty lungs while the kettle stood cold.

This morning the order is different. Feet on cold lino. Kettle clicked on. Window cracked to the garden. You stand while the water mutters and you notice what paper used to cover. The sour mouth from last night is not there. The throat does not bite. The hands have nothing to hold and nothing to tap. Coffee smells sharper. Bread toasts and you taste the crust all the way through.

We used to call that first dose necessary. Look at it now with table eyes. Did it ever add flavour to the coffee? Did it ever make the toast crisper or the morning kinder? It filmed the tongue before the first bite and thinned the nose before the first smell. The bite you taste now was always in the bread. The sharpness you smell now was always in the cup.

I stood in that same cold kitchen with my chipped mug and laughed at how ordinary it was. No trumpet, no test, only toast and steam and a clear head for the list of the day. The phone rang and I answered it without stepping out. The post came and I read it without tapping ash on the step. Small things, and they stood whole without paper beside them.

You grin into the steam and say under your breath: YIPPEE! I'M FREE!

The Shop Without Stopping

Mid-morning you run out of milk and you walk to the corner shop the old way, coat half-zipped, keys in fist. We know that queue by heart, the rank of packets behind the shutter, the small white price tickets, the hand that used to add one more box without thinking because running short felt like running short of temper.

You join the line with bread and milk only. The shutter clicks for the man ahead. Paper smell puffs out for a second and you watch his fingers yellowed at the tips count coins. You do not envy him. You see the bill he carries home in his pocket and on his coat and on his curtains.

I remember counting that same bill and telling myself it was small. It was not small. It was daily, and it bought stain and ash and a mouth that needed mints before a kiss. It bought standing in rain under doorways and counting minutes at dinners while food went cold.

At the till you pay and you are out in the street again with the bag light in your hand. The street smells of rain and fried onions from the cafe and petrol from the bus, and your nose takes it all without film. A bus sighs. A dog shakes water from its coat. A child drags a scooter and laughs. You walk home for lunch that will taste of lunch, and the hand that once checked for the pack checks for the key instead.

The Pause and the Plate

Work still piles and meals still end. We once paid each finished page with a dose, as if paper signed the work. We once closed a meal by killing taste with paper, then blamed dinner for being dull.

Today you finish the paragraph and you stand. Kettle, window, three minutes with your shoulders dropped and your eyes on the yard. No tube, no ash, no clock-watching for the next. The shoulders drop because the pause holds you, not because lack was fed. The page stays done because you did it, and the satisfaction sits in the chest where paper used to take credit.

After supper the plate sits cleared and the taste stays. Gravy and pepper linger instead of turning to stale film. You carry the plates, you wipe the board, you keep talking without watching the door. Coffee comes and goes without a veil over it. The tongue stays clean and the talk runs on.

Later, heaters glowing on a patio with glasses clinking catch your eye through a window, and you see coats gathered and talk going on. We once believed that picture needed paper to be complete. Watch it straight. The glow warms, the drink cools the hand, the story makes the laugh. Paper only rode beside them and sent the bill.

I ate those meals for years and never let them finish. Now they finish, and the finish is longer than the flare ever was.

When the Thought Pops

It will pop. Not often, and with less voice each week, but it will pop in your own voice.

“A dose with coffee.” “One won't hurt with a drink.” “Just to get through this row.”

We heard those lines for years and obeyed them before thought finished. You hear them now for what they are, hunger talking in a suit, the last echo of a story that sold lack as help. The desk row still sits after ash would have fallen. The coffee still warms without paper beside it. The night still laughs without paper in it.

Do not argue and do not push the thought into shadow. Answer it the way you answered at the table, with the crushed butt behind you and the stain seen straight. Did three seconds ever pay for the week of counting that followed? Did heaven at lighting ever survive the aftertaste? You know from your own pocket and your own morning mouth.

You smile and say it plain: YIPPEE! I'M FREE!

I say it still when a lit tube drifts past on the street, not because I need it but because I like hearing how true it sounds. The thought comes, the answer comes quicker, and you turn to the next thing, keys, coat, kettle, work, talk. Each turning makes the next pop quieter until street and desk and kitchen are only street and desk and kitchen again, with no paper beside them.

SUMMARY
- Morning, shop, pause and plate prove the dose added nothing; the moment held its own taste and talk.
- Hunger still pops in your old voice at times, and answering it with the owned truth leaves it thinner.
- Ordinary days keep their coffee, food, work and company complete; nothing is missing and nothing needs paper.
