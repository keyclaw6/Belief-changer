Chapter 13
EMPTY HANDS, FULL DAYS

*The day you feared would feel thin tastes fuller with no paper in it.*

## THE KETTLE BOILS FIRST

You wake to the same alarm in the same flat with the same light at the window. For years we woke the other way. Feet had not touched the floor before the hand found the pack, throat still closed from the night, first tube taken on empty lungs while the kettle stood cold and the window stayed shut.

This morning the order is different. Feet on cold lino. Kettle clicked on. Window cracked to the garden. You stand while the water mutters and you notice what paper used to cover. The mouth is clear from sleep, not sour from last night. The throat does not bite. The hands have nothing to hold and nothing to tap, and they do not ask for work. Coffee smells sharper from the jar. Bread goes down and the crust cracks all the way through.

We used to call that first tube necessary. We said it opened the eyes and set the hands right. Look at it now with the tray behind you. Did it ever add flavour to the coffee? Did it ever make the toast crisper or the morning kinder? It filmed the tongue before the first bite and thinned the nose before the first smell. It took three minutes and sent you out with a mouth you wanted to rinse. The bite you taste now was always in the bread. The sharpness you smell now was always in the cup.

I stood in that same cold kitchen with my chipped mug and laughed at how ordinary it was. No trumpet, no test, only toast and steam and a clear head for the list of the day. The phone rang and I answered it where I stood, without stepping out to the step. The post came and I read it at the table, without tapping grey on the sill. The cat wound round my ankles and I did not wave smoke from her face. Small things, and they stood whole without paper beside them.

You shave and dress without the old rush to the door. The radio plays the news and you actually hear it, instead of hearing it through the mental count of when you can slip out. You find your shoes without patting every pocket for pack and lighter first. The mirror shows eyes that did not water in wind on the step. The coat that once needed spraying before you could hug your own kids smells of nothing but cupboard and wool.

You take the mug to the window and look at the garden while the toast cools. Frost on the shed roof. A blackbird on the fence. Your neighbour's chimney smoking, not you. You lift the mug and the steam warms the nose, and coffee goes down hot and bitter and full, and stays. No second taste comes to spoil it. No film comes to sit on it. The tongue stays as coffee left it.

Did the morning need a flare to start? Was the light less light because no paper burned in it? Was the list less doable because the hand did not shake ash on it? You know from your own mouth this morning. The light was light. The list was list. The hands did the work they always did, only without paying for the right to do it.

You grin into the steam and say under your breath: YIPPEE! I'M FREE!

## THE SHOP WITHOUT STOPPING

Mid-morning you run out of milk and you walk to the corner shop the old way, coat half-zipped, keys in fist, basket on arm. We know that queue by heart. The rank of packets behind the shutter, the small white price tickets, the hand that used to add one more box without thinking because running short felt like running short of temper. We counted the till as part of the errand, as if bread and milk were not a shop without it.

You join the line with bread and milk only. The shutter clicks for the man ahead. Paper smell puffs out for a second and you watch his fingers yellowed at the tips count coins for his box. You do not envy him. You see the bill he carries home in his pocket and on his coat and on his curtains and in his morning mouth. We carried that same bill for years and called it small. It was not small. It was daily, and it bought stain and ash and standing in rain under doorways and counting minutes at dinners while food went cold.

I remember the arithmetic I used to do at that till. I told myself a box was only coins, only today, only this once more. Then I did it again tomorrow and the next day and the next, and the coins became notes and the notes became a drawer of cellophane and a calendar of counts. I told myself I chose it. I chose it the way a man chooses to pay rent on a room he hates because he thinks there is no other room. There was another room all along. You are standing in it now with a light basket.

You nod to the woman behind the counter and you are not watching the clock. Fresh bread smells from the rack beside you and you breathe it in without film. The papers shout their headlines and you read one while you wait, instead of working out if you have enough left to last the evening. The man with his box steps out and lights before he has cleared the door, cupping flare in wind. You step out with nothing to cup and the wind is only wind on the face.

At the till you pay and you are out in the street again with the bag light in your hand. The street smells of rain and fried onions from the cafe and petrol from the bus, and your nose takes it all without film. A bus sighs to the stop. A dog shakes water from its coat. A child drags a scooter and laughs and the wheels rattle on the slabs. You walk home for lunch that will taste of lunch, and the hand that once checked for the pack checks for the key instead, and finds it.

Ask yourself on the walk, with the milk cold against your side: what did the extra box ever add to this walk? Did the street look richer through paper? Did the onions smell deeper? Did the bus come quicker? The street was always street. The shop was always shop. Paper only stood between you and both and charged for the standing.

## THE PARAGRAPH STAYS DONE

Work still piles and the clock still moves. We once paid each finished page with a tube, as if paper signed the work. Teaching a class, finishing a story, finishing a paragraph, driving to the store — each closed act got its pellet, and we thought the pellet closed it.

Today you finish the paragraph and you stand. Kettle, window, three minutes with your shoulders dropped and your eyes on the yard. No tube, no ash, no clock-watching for the next. The shoulders drop because the pause holds you, not because lack was fed. The page stays done because you did it, and the satisfaction sits in the chest where paper used to take credit.

I know that old chain well. I wrote a page and lit without looking, as if the lighting were part of the writing. I drove five hundred yards and lit, as if the driving needed a full stop of grey. I answered a hard letter and lit before reading it back. The work was mine in every case. The timing was mine. The eye that caught the wrong word was mine. Paper arrived after the work and claimed the glow that belonged to finishing.

Try it straight today and watch who owns what. You write the hard sentence, you find the right word, you put the full stop where it holds. Then you stand and stretch and look out. The stretch loosens the neck. The window cools the face. Three minutes pass and the mind settles and comes back clearer for the next sentence. Where in that chain was paper needed? The eyes did the seeing. The hands did the typing. The pause did the resting.

We believed the pellet marked the done. The done marks itself. A finished paragraph feels finished in the body before any hand moves to a pocket. You feel it now — that small squaring of the shoulders, that urge to stand and breathe at the sill. That was always the reward. We only taught ourselves to light over the top of it until we could not taste it.

You sit back down and the next paragraph comes quicker because the mouth is clear and the head is clear and no minute is spent counting when to step out. The morning holds more work, not less, because no work is cut in two by stepping out to pay. The afternoon break is the same. You take your tea to the step and feel air on the face and sun on the hands, free-handed, eyes open. No tapping, no guarding ember from wind, no cutting the break short because the tube has finished but you have not.

## THE PLATE FINISHES

Meals still end, and that ending once called for paper. We closed dinner by killing taste with paper, then blamed dinner for being dull. We left the table while talk ran on, stood outside, came back with cold food and a stale mouth, and told ourselves we had enjoyed both.

Today the plate sits cleared and the taste stays. Gravy and pepper linger instead of turning to film. You carry the plates, you wipe the board, you keep talking without watching the door. Coffee comes and goes without a veil over it. The tongue stays as dinner left it, and the talk runs on because no one has left to stand in the yard.

I ate those meals for years and never let them finish. I took two bites of pudding and went out for a pull, and came back to pudding gone flat and company moved on to another story I had missed. I paid for the meal and then paid again to miss it. I told myself paper rounded the meal. It cut the meal in half and kept the half I paid most for.

Watch a full meal finish tonight and notice what stays. Soup first, steam on the face, salt and heat all the way down. Bread to push the bowl clean. Main plate hot, pepper at the back of the throat, potatoes crisp at the edge. Talk going round, story answering story. No watching the clock. No calculating when politeness will allow stepping out. When the plates go, coffee comes, and coffee tastes of coffee because no paper has filmed it. Pudding comes and stays sweet to the last spoon, instead of turning to ash in the memory. The finish is longer than any flare ever was. It lasts in the mouth and in the room.

Did pudding need paper to be pudding? Did talk need paper to be talk? Did the table need grey falling in it to be a table? You know from your own chair tonight. Pudding was pudding. Talk was talk. The chair held you the whole time without asking for coins.

We thought we dined and then smoked. We smoked and therefore never quite dined. Now you dine to the end, and the end is yours.

## THE STREET AND THE WAITING ROOM

The day is full of small waits, and waits once meant lighting. Bus stop, queue at the post office, traffic lights, phone on hold, kettle again. We filled every gap with a flare because an empty hand felt like an empty minute.

Take the bus stop today. You stand with your bag at your feet and your hands in your pockets. The timetable says six minutes. Old habit says: fill it. New eyes say: watch it. A pigeon picks at a crisp packet. A van unloads bread for the cafe. An old man reads the same notice you have read a hundred times and shakes his head at the prices. The bus sighs round the corner with rain on its windows, and you step on and sit and look out. Six minutes passed without grey, and they were not empty. They were street.

I lived those gaps with paper for years. I timed lights by pulls. I timed queues by whether I could step out and keep my place. I left meetings to stand under dripping awnings and called it a break. The break was standing and looking and breathing at the kerb. Paper only rode beside the standing and billed for it. The kerb is still there. The standing is still there. The looking is still there. Only the billing has gone.

Drive if you drive. The same junctions, the same crawl at the lights. We used to light at every red and tap out of the crack in the window and wipe ash from the dash. Today hands stay on the wheel, eyes stay on the road, and the car smells of car, not of tray. The lights change when they change. The queue moves when it moves. You arrive with a mouth that does not need mints before you speak to the man at the desk.

Wait in the waiting room if waiting comes. Magazines with curled corners, clock ticking loud, names called. You sit and flick pages and listen. No stepping out to the car park to hurry time. Time does what it always did — it passes at its own pace whether paper burns or not. We thought paper hurried it. It only cut it into priced lengths and sold it back. The phone on hold plays its thin music and you wait with the receiver warm at the ear, feet up, eyes on the yard, until a voice answers. No flare needed to hold the line.

Ask on the bus, in the car, in the queue: did the wait hurt less because paper burned in it? Or did the wait grow longer because part of the mind counted pulls and ash and when to light the next? You have stood both kinds of wait now. The clear wait is shorter because nothing is counted in it but street and faces and weather.

## THE LOUD HOUR

A loud hour will come, because loud hours come whether paper burns or not. Phone down after a row. Bills spread on the table. Head loud. For years we read that loudness as an order. Hand to pocket before thought finished, flare, shoulders dropped for seconds, problem still on the desk after.

Live one loud hour straight and watch what holds without paper. The phone goes down. The room is loud in the head. You stand, you go to the window, you drink water, you walk to the next room and back. Shoulders drop because the pause holds you and the legs move and the eyes look at distance. The row still sits to be mended. The bills still sit to be counted. No ash has fallen on them to make them harder.

I sat at that desk a thousand times with paper. I lit and watched the edgy minute go quiet for seconds and called that quiet help. Then the minute returned on time asking for the next, now with a second noise — where is the next, when can I step out, how many left in the pack. The desk had two problems after instead of one, and I called the doubling a taking-care-of. It never filed a paper. It never mended a quarrel. It never paid a bill.

You will hear the old line in your own voice: a tube would take care of this. Hear it for what it is now — hunger talking in a suit, the last echo of a story that sold lack as help. The desk row still sits after ash would have fallen. The coffee still warms without paper beside it. The work still waits for hands, not for flare.

Do what does the work. Stand. Window. Water. Walk to the next room. Answer the letter in three plain sentences. Put the bills in one pile and date them. Ring the man back when the head is cooler and say what needs saying. The shoulders drop the same without the bill because the pause did the dropping all along. Paper only charged at the door.

I took loud hours without paper when I first walked clear, and I waited for the sky to fall. It did not fall. The hour stayed loud for a while, then thinned because hours do. People went on talking. Work went on waiting to be done. Music played whether paper burned or not. The only thing missing was the second noise, and missing it was not missing. It was quiet where noise used to be.

## THE EVENING CHAIRS

Evening comes with chairs outside and talk going on. Through a window you catch heaters glowing, glasses clinking, coats gathered, story making laugh. We once believed that picture needed paper to be complete. We stood in that glow with a tube and thought the tube made the glow.

Go out and sit in that glow without paper and watch straight. The heater warms the face. The drink cools the hand. The story makes the laugh. Talk runs, glasses go down and come up, night air moves over the table and carries food smell and rain smell and perfume without film. No one watches the door. No one counts minutes. No stepping out to tap ash while the punchline lands inside.

I sat those nights for years with paper in fist. I missed lines while lighting. I missed faces while watching ember. I came back with a stale mouth to a cold glass and called it belonging. Belonging was the chairs and the talk and the night. Paper only rented a corner of it and sent the bill to me.

You will see lit tubes moving in that glow. Hands yellowed, coats smelling, eyes watering in wind while they cup flare. Do not envy them. They are not holding more of the night than you. They are holding less, because part of their night is spent serving the tube — watching it, tapping it, guarding it from wind, counting if another is needed before last orders. You hold the whole night with empty hands. The whole night is more than the rented corner.

Did the bright night need paper to be bright? Was it the sun going down slow, the leisure earned, the company laughing, the drink cold in the hand, that gave, while paper only rode beside them? You watched that ride to the end at your own table, stain and grey and stale end. Was that brightness? Hold your seeing and you hold your leaving.

Stay to the end of the talk. Walk home with a mouth that tastes of supper and drink, not of tray. Coat smells of night, not of paper. Fingers smell of nothing but skin. The pavement is damp under the lamps and the houses are warm at the windows and your own door opens on a hall that does not smell. That is not less night. That is night without rent.

## WHEN THE THOUGHT POPS

It will pop. Not often, and with less voice each week, but it will pop in your own voice.

A tube with coffee. One with a drink when the glow is warm. Just to get through this row.

We heard those lines for years and obeyed them before thought finished. You hear them now for what they are, the last echo asking to be fed. The desk row still sits after ash would have fallen. The coffee still warms without paper beside it. The night still laughs without paper in it.

Do not argue and do not push the thought into shadow. Answer it the way you answered at the table, with the crushed butt behind you and the stain seen straight. Did three seconds ever pay for the week of counting that followed? Did heaven at lighting ever survive the aftertaste on the tongue? Did a single shared light ever stay single in your diary, or did Friday always bring the same pack? You know from your own pocket and your own morning mouth. The answers came from the senses, not from the story.

I say it still when a lit tube drifts past on the street. I still say YIPPEE! I'M FREE! not because anything pulls, but because I like hearing how true it sounds against the old line. The thought comes, the answer comes quicker, and I turn to the next thing — keys, coat, kettle, work, talk. Each turning makes the next pop quieter until street and desk and kitchen are only street and desk and kitchen again, with no paper beside them.

You smile and say it plain: YIPPEE! I'M FREE!

If doubt knocks in the first days, welcome it as proof the old story is losing, not as proof you need paper to be yourself. You sleep hours each night with no tube and wake still you. You sit through films and meals and flights with no tube and remain you. If paper were the self, those hours would be impossible. They are not impossible. They are ordinary. The daytime gnaw is the same night-time quiet interrupted by waking habit, nothing more. Leave it unfed and it thins.

If you ever take a puff after you stop, do not call yourself lost and do not take another to punish yourself. Take it as a rumble strip, a loud warning to hold your seeing tighter, and leave the pack where it is. A rumble strip does not drive the car into the ditch. It shakes the wheel and says look up. Look up. Hold the picture of the tray — plenty taken, nothing given in plain day — and do not let one blink rewrite clear mornings, full taste, steady breath, money kept, time kept. One warns. Two rebuilds. Leave it at warning.

Walk the ordinary street with empty hands. Kettle, keys, coat, bus, desk, plate, chairs. The days ahead hold the living, and the living holds without paper because it always did. Paper only rode beside it. Now the ride is over and the days are yours to finish to the end.

**SUMMARY**
- Morning coffee, toast, shop queue and street stand whole with no tube; taste, smell and talk were in the moment all along.
- Finished work and finished meals close themselves; the pause and the plate do the rewarding, not the flare.
- Loud hours and bright nights stay loud and bright without paper; the problem sits the same and the company warms the same, with no second noise added.
- Old lines still pop at times in your own voice, and answering with the owned truth leaves each pop quieter until days are only days again.
