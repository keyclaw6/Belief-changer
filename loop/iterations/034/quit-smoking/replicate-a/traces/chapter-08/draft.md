Chapter 8
MORNING, FOOD AND THE QUIET BREAK

IN THIS CHAPTER — Cold lino and the kettle, the window ajar, the finished plate, the kerb and the air on the face

*Morning, food and a break were always yours; smoke only stood beside them taking the wages.*

## THE FEET FIND THE FLOOR FIRST

We all know that first minute by heart.

The alarm rasps. The eyes are still half shut. The feet have not touched cold lino before the hand is already moving toward the packet on the chair. The lighter clicks before the kettle clicks. The first drag is taken on empty lungs and the head spins a fraction and we call that waking up.

Live that minute again with me, but live it straight.

Feet find cold lino. Legs stand. Mouth tastes stale from the night. Chest feels tight from yesterday's smoke. Eyes blink at grey light through the curtains. The body is not crying for a gift. It is showing the night's lack asking to be fed, and the mind reads the lack as meaning the day cannot start.

I stood in that minute for thirty years. I can tell you what started the day.

What started the day was standing, pissing, putting the kettle on, throwing the window ajar, letting cold morning into the kitchen. The kettle hums. The window frame is cold under the palm. The street below is waking — a bus sighing, a shutter rolling, a neighbour's door. The lungs pull morning in and cough once and clear. The hands are busy with cup and spoon. The eyes come open because light comes in, because blood moves when legs move, because sleep thins when we stand.

We credited paper with doing that work.

Watch tomorrow morning with clear eyes while you still smoke as normal, and test it. Do the kettle minute first before the light, just to watch. Feet to lino. Stand. Kettle on. Window open a crack. Wash the face. What wakes you? The cold water wakes. The standing wakes. The light wakes. The first deep breaths of kitchen air wake, even with last night's staleness still in the mouth. The dose, when it comes, does not add waking. It pauses its own nag for minutes while waking does its own work underneath.

You say to me, I hear you already:

“But the first one settles me. Without it my hands fidget and my head will not line up.”

I do not mock that fidget. What stirs is only an empty, slightly restless, slightly edgy little tug. A faint empty gnaw, a hand-memory that says now. It is not morning missing its master. It is night-lack asking for its coin, dressed by years of teaching as need for steadiness. A child wakes fidgety and does not light to line the head up. A non-smoker wakes fidgety and puts the kettle on and lines up. You did it yourself before the first paper behind the sheds.

Ask and answer only to yourself.

Did the kettle boil because paper burned, or did paper burn while the kettle boiled? Did light come through the curtains because ash fell, or did ash fall while light came? Did your legs hold you because smoke held you, or did your legs hold you while smoke took the credit? You know from your own kitchen that standing woke, water woke, air woke. What did the first dose add except a few minutes without the gnaw it had left behind?

Take that minute back with hands free and the minute stays whole. Cup in one hand, window latch in the other, breath coming deeper with each pull of morning that has no ash in it. No stepping to the back door in cold ankles. No apologising to the house with spray and mint. The morning was never lit. It was lived.

## THE PLATE THAT FINISHES ITSELF

We paid for done with smoke.

Taught a class, finished a paragraph, drove the grocery run, closed the plate — each small done called for its little pellet. The hand lit before standing. The doorway filled. The mouth dried. We called the dryness a full stop and the pause a party.

Keep the work joy intact and look at who threw the party.

I remember the paragraph best. I typed the last line, read it back, felt that small lift of done in the chest. Then the old minute called and I paid it before standing. I stood in the doorway, blew at cold air, and told myself the day had given me something. The day had given me something before the doorway. The paragraph gave it. My own hands on the keys gave it. The dose only sat beside done and held its hand out.

Food tells the same truth louder because the tongue cannot lie for long.

Sit to a meal you like while you still smoke as normal, and taste it on purpose. The first bite after hunger always tastes. The middle of the plate tastes. The last bite tastes, then hunger quiets and flavour settles and the mouth looks for what comes next. The mind, taught for years, reads the small lack between courses as meaning the meal is unfinished without ash. You light, draw, cough once, and call the dryness a seal.

Taste the truth straight.

Food put flavour in. Hunger made flavour keen. Your own tongue and nose did the work bite by bite. Ash never added salt or sweet or heat. It laid a film over the tongue and was thanked for the tongue. Does a child need a seal to know the plate is done? Does a non-smoker sit bewildered at a finished plate, unable to close the meal without a tube? You closed a thousand meals before you started, pushing the plate an inch, sitting a while, letting taste linger while talk went on. The lingering was the ending. The ending needed no sender.

Ask again, and let your own mouth answer.

Did the food taste before ash touched it, or only after? Did the paragraph please before the pellet, or only after? Did the grocery run feel done in the car park with bags lifted, or only in the doorway with paper lit? You know food tasted before the light, work pleased before the light, done felt done before the light. What did the coin buy except a pause in the nag while worth stood waiting beside it?

There is a small test you can do without changing anything else in your life. Finish the plate and stay where you are. Push the plate an inch. Keep the cup warm between the palms. Let taste sit. Notice how flavour stays a while and then softens on its own, how the body signals enough without instruction, how talk fills the minute that paper used to fill. No one at the table watches you for a trick. The table is warm already. Company warms without paper. Hunger quiets because food quiets it.

It never fixed the itch. It caused it.

Hold that against the plate and the doorway and both rearrange. The meal did not need a seal. The work did not need a crown. What needed feeding was the dip between doses, and the dip arrived because doses train it. Give the credit back where it belongs — to food, to your hands, to the finished line — and paper stands beside the minute with empty hands, asking wages for work it never did.

## THE BREAK WITH NOTHING MISSING

We feared the break most because the break felt like ours.

Ten minutes off the phones. Step to the kerb. Cold on the cheeks. Shoulders down. We called that ten minutes ours and believed paper made it ours. Without it, we said, the break would be standing with nothing, hands with nowhere to go, head with no off-switch.

I took those breaks for years and I stepped away from work to serve them and returned to find work had waited and air had done the softening.

Take the same ten minutes and strip paper out in your head while your body stays exactly where it is.

Step to the kerb. Stamp the feet. Feel the air on the face. Roll the shoulders. Watch the street. The chest pulls deeper because no fresh smoke sits on it, and the stairs breath that used to come short after the doorway comes a shade easier when the doorway is skipped. The hands hold the warm cup. The eyes follow nothing in particular. The head, uncluttered for once by timing the next light, notices the sky, the bus brakes, the shop bell. Time passes without being counted in drags. Ten minutes feel like ten minutes, not like an interval between feeds.

That is a break. That was always a break.

“But what do I do with my hands? What do I do with the edgy minute when the phones have been cruel?”

Do what non-smokers do with hands and cruel minutes. Hold the cup. Put hands in pockets against cold. Stretch the neck. Wash the face. Walk to the shop and back. The edgy minute is not proof the day requires a tube. It is the old reading shouting over a small tug. The phones were cruel before the light and stay cruel after unless hands pick them up and answer. What softens is not the trouble. What softens is the nag between doses, and the nag softens for minutes at the price of returning within the hour.

Ask straight, and the kerb answers.

Did air need ash to feel cold and kind, or did ash ride on air that was already cold and kind? Did shoulders drop because paper lifted them, or did shoulders drop because standing and stretching dropped them while paper burned? Did talk with the mate by the shutter please because smoke seasoned it, or did talk please while smoke taxed it with checks of pocket and lighter? You laughed before the light. You breathed after the ash fell. The middle puff was only ever sneaking a ride.

Once seeing is complete the day stands without props. Morning wakes by standing and water and light. Food closes by enough and lingering. The break restores by air and standing and a minute uncounted. Each held its own worth before ash touched it. Each holds it now while you watch.

You do not need to be taught to breathe and taste. The body knew before the first paper and knows still under the teaching. Trust it to do its old work while you keep your eyes open.

8. TRUST YOUR BODY TO BREATHE AND TASTE
Your body knows freedom without being taught.

SUMMARY
- Morning wakes by standing, water, light and air, not by the first dose on empty lungs.
- Food puts flavour in and enough closes the plate, while ash only films the tongue.
- The kerb break restores by cold, standing and a minute uncounted, not by a tube.
- The small tug between doses is lack asking for its coin, not proof the day needs paper.
