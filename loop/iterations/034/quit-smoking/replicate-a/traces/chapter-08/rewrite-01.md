Chapter 8
MORNING, PLATE AND KERB

**IN THIS CHAPTER** — The cold lino underfoot, the kettle steam at the window, the plate pushed an inch, the kerb air on the face

*Morning, food and a break were complete before paper touched them; smoke only stood beside them taking the wages.*

## THE FEET FIND THE FLOOR FIRST

We all know that first minute by heart.

The alarm rasps. The eyes are still half shut. The feet have not touched cold lino before the hand is already moving toward the packet on the chair. The lighter clicks before the kettle clicks. The first drag is taken on empty lungs and the head spins a fraction and we call that waking up.

Live that minute again with me, but live it straight.

Feet find cold lino. Legs take weight. Mouth tastes stale from the night. Chest feels tight from yesterday's smoke. Eyes blink at grey light through the curtains. The body is not asking for a gift. It is showing the night's lack asking for its coin, and the mind reads the lack as meaning the day cannot start.

I stood in that minute for thirty years. I can tell you what started the day.

What started the day was standing, pissing, putting the kettle on, throwing the window ajar, letting morning into the kitchen. The kettle hums. The window frame is cold under the palm. The street below is waking — a bus sighing, a shutter rolling, a neighbour's door thudding. The lungs pull kitchen air in and cough once and clear. The hands are busy with cup and spoon. The eyes come open because light comes in, because blood moves when legs move, because sleep thins when we stand.

We credited paper with doing that work.

Watch tomorrow morning with clear eyes while you still smoke as normal, and test it. Do the kettle minute first before the light, just to watch. Feet to lino. Stand. Kettle on. Window open a crack. Wash the face in cold water. What wakes you? The cold water wakes. The standing wakes. The light wakes. The first deep pulls of kitchen air wake, even with last night's staleness still in the mouth. The dose, when it comes, does not add waking. It pauses its own nag for minutes while waking does its own work underneath.

You say to me, I hear you already:

“But the first one settles me. Without it my hands fidget and my head will not line up.”

I do not mock that fidget. What stirs is only an empty, slightly restless, slightly edgy little tug. A faint empty gnaw, a hand-memory that says now. It is not morning missing its master. It is night-lack asking for its coin, dressed by years of teaching as need for steadiness. A child wakes fidgety and does not light to line the head up. A non-smoker wakes fidgety and puts the kettle on and lines up. You did it yourself before the first paper behind the sheds.

Ask and answer only to yourself.

Did the kettle boil because paper burned, or did paper burn while the kettle boiled? Did light come through the curtains because ash fell, or did ash fall while light came? Did your legs hold you because smoke held you, or did your legs hold you while smoke took the credit? You know from your own kitchen that standing woke, water woke, air woke. What did the first dose add except a few minutes without the gnaw it had left behind?

Take that minute back with hands busy and the minute stays whole. Cup in one hand, window latch in the other, both hands free and busy with heat and cold. Breath comes deeper with each pull of morning that has no ash in it. No stepping to the back door in cold ankles. No apologising to the house with spray and mint. The morning was never lit. It was lived.

Stay in that kitchen a little longer, because the con hides in the small sequence.

We shuffled to the chair, we lit, we stood at the sink with paper between fingers, we stared out while the kettle built to boil. We remember that stare as thinking, as gathering, as becoming ourselves. Look at what thinking happened. Nothing was gathered except ash. The kettle boiled because the element heated. The brain cleared because blood moved and sleep lifted. The stare added nothing except time spent feeding a dip while the day waited.

I remember my own sink stare. Cup gone cold because I stood too long at the window with paper, ash on the sill, kettle boiled and clicked off and I had to boil again. I called those five minutes my quiet start. They were not quiet. The tug nagged until fed, then nagged less for minutes, then began again. Quiet would have been kettle poured, cup warmed between palms, face washed, coat found. That takes the same five minutes and leaves no film on the tongue.

Try the order straight, still as a smoker watching, not changing anything else in your life. Tonight put the cup by the kettle before bed. In the morning feet to lino, stand tall a second, roll the shoulders, run cold water over wrists, open the window wide enough to hear the street. Put the kettle on and stand beside it while it hums. Hold the warm cup with both palms when poured. Notice the mouth still stale, the chest still tight from last night, the tug still whispering — and notice waking happening anyway underneath the whisper. Legs hold. Eyes open. Hands steady on the cup after the first sip. The day starts because you started it.

Does a non-smoker lie in bed unable to rise until a tube is found? Does a child need a squirt to leave dreams and find the bathroom? Did you? You rose for years before paper, feet to cold floors in other houses, other winters, and the day began. The need for a lit start arrived after paper, not before. It grows overnight because doses train it, not because morning requires it.

See who did the work and the credit returns home.

Morning gave light. Legs gave standing. Water gave shock and clarity. Kettle gave heat. Lungs gave breath. Paper gave a pause in its own nag and was thanked as the giver of morning. That thanking is the Smokescreen at work — taking wages for your standing, your washing, your pouring.

Keep that watched morning in your pocket. We will need it at the plate and at the kerb.

## THE PLATE THAT FINISHES ITSELF

We paid for done with smoke.

Taught a class, finished a paragraph, drove the grocery run, closed the plate — each small done called for its little pellet. The hand lit before standing. The doorway filled. The mouth dried. We called the dryness a full stop and the pause a party.

Keep the work joy intact and look at who threw the party.

I remember the paragraph best. I typed the last line, read it back, felt that small lift of done in the chest. Then the old minute called and I paid it before standing. I stood in the doorway, blew at cold air, and told myself the day had given me something. The day had given me something before the doorway. The paragraph gave it. My own hands on the keys gave it. The dose only sat beside done and held its hand out.

Food tells the same truth louder because the tongue cannot lie for long.

Sit to a meal you like while you still smoke as normal, and taste it on purpose. The first bite after hunger always tastes. The middle of the plate tastes. The last bite tastes, then hunger quiets and flavour settles and the mouth looks for what comes next. The mind, taught for years, reads the small lack between courses as meaning the meal is unfinished without ash. You light, draw, cough once, and call the dryness a seal.

Taste the truth straight.

Food put flavour in. Hunger made flavour keen. Your own tongue and nose did the work bite by bite. Ash never added salt or sweet or heat. It laid a film over the tongue and was thanked for the tongue. Does a child need a seal to know the plate is done? Does a non-smoker sit bewildered at a finished plate, unable to close the meal without a tube? You closed a thousand meals before you started, pushing the plate an inch, sitting a while, letting taste linger while talk went on. The lingering was the ending. The ending needed no sender.

Ask again, and let your own mouth answer.

Did the food taste before ash touched it, or only after? Did the paragraph please before that old pellet for done, or only after? Did the grocery run feel done in the car park with bags lifted, or only in the doorway with paper lit? You know food tasted before the light, work pleased before the light, done felt done before the light. What did the coin buy except a pause in the nag while worth stood waiting beside it?

There is a small test you can do without changing anything else in your life. Finish the plate and stay where you are. Push the plate an inch. Keep the cup warm between the palms. Let taste sit. Notice how flavour stays a while and then softens on its own, how the body signals enough without instruction, how talk fills the minute that paper used to fill. No one at the table watches you for a trick. The table is warm already. Company warms without paper. Hunger quiets because food quiets it.

Watch the after-plate minute closely, because that minute was sold to us as empty.

We pushed back chairs, we patted pockets, we excused ourselves to the step while others poured more tea. We called that step digestion, settling, letting the meal go down. Digestion went on inside whether we stood at the step or sat at the table. What settled was not the stomach. What settled was the tug that woke as soon as hunger quieted and looked for its old meaning. Fed hunger leaves a quiet mouth. The brainwashing taught us to read any quiet mouth as an order. Sat or stood, the stomach did its work. Paper only rented the interval.

I sat once through a whole Sunday lunch at my sister's without leaving, because the baby slept on my arm and I could not rise to the step. Plate pushed, cup refilled, talk rolling, taste still in the mouth an hour later, faint and sweet. No seal, no doorway, no dryness. The meal closed itself. The body knew enough. The tongue remembered flavour without ash to file it. I had not proved will. I had only stayed seated and let the minute show what it shows when not interrupted.

Linger like that on purpose and the plate teaches.

First, hunger sharpens taste, not paper. The hungriest bite tastes most, before any light. Second, enough quiets hunger, not paper. The push of the plate, the sigh, the loosening of shoulders come from food, not ash. Third, time softens flavour, not paper. Taste lingers, then thins, then becomes memory while tea warms the palms. Each step happens in non-smokers daily without thought. Each happened in you before you started. Paper inserted itself between step two and step three and charged a fee for passage that was already open.

It never fixed the itch. It caused it.

Hold that against the plate and the doorway and both rearrange. The meal did not need a seal. The work did not need a crown. What needed feeding was the dip between doses, and the dip arrived because doses train it. Give the credit back where it belongs — to food, to your hands, to the finished line — and paper stands beside the minute with empty hands, asking wages for work it never did.

Food put flavour in. Ash took the edge off flavour.

Let the plate finish itself from here on while you watch. You will find the lingering longer, the tongue cleaner, the talk easier when no one leaves for the step.

## THE TEN MINUTES ON THE KERB

We feared the break most because the break felt like ours.

Ten minutes off the phones. Step to the kerb. Cold on the cheeks. Shoulders down. We called that ten minutes ours and believed paper made it ours. Without it, we said, the break would be standing with nothing, hands with nowhere to go, head with no off-switch.

I took those breaks for years and I stepped away from work to serve them and returned to find work had waited and air had done the softening.

Take the same ten minutes and strip paper out in your head while your body stays exactly where it is.

Step to the kerb. Stamp the feet. Feel the air on the face. Roll the shoulders. Watch the street. The chest pulls deeper because no fresh smoke sits on it, and the old stairs breath comes steadier when the doorway is skipped. The hands hold the warm cup. The eyes follow nothing in particular. The head, uncluttered for once by timing the next light, notices the sky, the bus brakes, the shop bell. Time passes without being counted in drags. Ten minutes feel like ten minutes, not like an interval between feeds.

That is a break. That was always a break.

You pull clean air deep and feel the chest widen without a cough to pay.

Hear the objection as you speak it, because I spoke it for years:

“But what do I do with my hands? What do I do with the edgy minute when the phones have been cruel?”

Do what non-smokers do with hands and cruel minutes. Hold the cup. Put hands in pockets against cold. Stretch the neck. Wash the face. Walk to the shop and back. The edgy minute is not proof the day requires a tube. It is the old reading shouting over a small tug. The phones were cruel before the light and stay cruel after unless hands pick them up and answer. What softens is not the trouble. What softens is the nag between doses, and the nag softens for minutes at the price of returning within the hour.

Ask straight, and the kerb answers.

Did air need ash to feel cold and kind, or did ash ride on air that was already cold and kind? Did shoulders drop because paper lifted them, or did shoulders drop because standing and stretching dropped them while paper burned? Did talk with the mate by the shutter please because smoke seasoned it, or did talk please while smoke taxed it with checks of pocket and lighter? You laughed before the light. You breathed after the ash fell. The middle puff was only ever sneaking a ride.

Stay longer on that kerb in your head, because the break has layers we never tasted while counting drags.

First minute, the door shuts behind you and the noise drops. Phones muffled, voices muffled, heat left inside. Cold finds the cheeks first, then the ears, then the wrists. Shoulders that sat high for an hour drop half an inch because standing drops them. That drop is posture and air, not paper.

Second minute, legs want movement. Stamp, shift, walk three paces to the drain and back. Blood moves. Eyes water a little in wind and clear. The cup warms the fingers through thin gloves. The mind, no longer fed new orders, replays the last call once, then lets it thin. That thinning is time and distance, not paper.

Third minute, the street shows itself. A van double-parked, a kid dragging a scooter, pigeons lifting off the awning all at once. You watch without purpose. Watching without purpose rests the head that has stared at type for an hour. That rest is looking far after looking near, not paper.

Fourth through tenth, the body settles to its own rhythm. Breath slows. Jaw unclenches. Hands stop checking. Hunger or thirst names itself plainly — water, food, movement — not as a single order to light. Thought steadies enough to return inside and pick up the next task without dread. That steadiness is pause itself, not paper.

We never let those layers happen in order. We lit at minute one, coughed at minute two, checked the packet at minute three, stubbed and relit at minute seven because talk ran long, came back smelling of ash with the cup gone cold. We called that rest. Rest had left by the back alley while we fed the meter at the front.

Do the same ten minutes with the cup and no paper and count what returns.

Taste returns first. The cup tastes of tea, not of ash under tea. Smell returns next. Cold, diesel, rain on stone, someone's chips two doors down — sharp and separate, not wrapped in one stale film. Hearing returns. The shop bell, heels on wet pavement, the laugh from the doorway. Touch returns. Cold latch, warm cardboard, rough brick where you lean. None of these needed teaching. The body reports them when not filmed.

What about the tug in that ten minutes? It comes, a faint empty gnaw, a hand lifting half an inch toward a pocket. Then, unfed, it thins because attention is on the street, the cup, the talk. Non-smokers feel fidgets in breaks too — tired feet, full bladder, impatience to return — and call them signals, not orders. We were taught to call this one an order. The teaching was the volume knob, not the signal.

Look at other breaks around you with clear eyes.

The mate who does not smoke takes the same ten, stamps the same kerb, drinks the same tea, returns inside ready. No tube, no lighter, no apology. The driver eats an apple on the wall. The nurse stands with face to the sun for two minutes with eyes shut. Each takes hands, air, pause and returns. None stands bewildered with nowhere to put hands. Hands hold cups, lean on walls, push into pockets, gesture while talking. Hands knew their work before paper hired them.

Yours know still.

## THE BODY THAT ALREADY KNOWS

We have walked the three minutes that held us.

Morning feet to kettle. Plate pushed an inch with taste lingering. Kerb with air on the face and cup between palms. Each stood whole before paper. Each stands whole while you watch as a smoker. Nothing has been taken. Something has been returned to its owner.

Now see the pattern that joins them, because pattern is what frees, not moments.

In each minute the worth came first and paper came second to claim it. Waking came from standing, water, light. Paper came second. Flavour came from hunger and food. Paper came second. Rest came from standing, stretching, looking far. Paper came second. In each the tug woke between doses and was read as meaning the minute needed a dose. The reading was installed. The minute was innocent.

You walk back inside in a quiet escape that asks for no coin and no permission.

I am not asking you to memorise this or to practise breaks without paper. I am asking you to keep watching while you smoke as normal until we finish. Watch the kettle boil without crediting ash. Watch taste linger without sealing it. Watch the kerb restore without lighting it. Each watching weakens the reading a little more, until the reading falls off and the tug stands small and plain for what it is — a faint lack that passes whether fed or not, not proof of need, not you.

Ask the plain questions once more and let your own day close them.

If paper woke, why did legs, water and light wake before it? If paper sealed meals, why did food taste before it and enough quiet after it? If paper rested, why did shoulders drop on standing and breath deepen on air while it burned? There is only one reading that fits all three. The minutes gave. Paper took wages.

That is the lived baseline under the teaching. The body that wakes, tastes, pauses without equipment. You lived it for years before the first dose. You lent it out for years to a meter that never stayed fed. You are not building a new body now. You are moving back into the one that waited.

Trust it to do its old work while you keep your eyes open.

8. TRUST YOUR BODY TO BREATHE AND TASTE
Your body knows freedom without being taught.

SUMMARY
- Morning wakes by standing, water, light and kitchen air, not by the first dose on empty lungs.
- Food puts flavour in and enough closes the plate, while ash only films the tongue.
- The kerb break restores by cold, standing, watching and a minute uncounted, not by a tube.
- The small tug between doses is lack asking for its coin, not proof the day needs paper.
- Work pleased, taste lingered and air softened before paper; the credit belongs to the minute, not the dose.
