FREE FOR GOOD

*There was never anything to miss, and the sill proves it.*

## THE ASHTRAY WAS ALREADY EMPTY

You stand at the sink with the sill in front of you. The glass dish sits there washed and dry. No grey ring, no stub, no smell rising when the sun hits it. For years we kept that dish full and called the filling ours. We tapped, we stacked, we emptied it last thing and filled it again first thing, and we told ourselves the dish served us.

Look at it now with clear eyes. Did that dish ever fill itself? Did paper ever climb out of the pack and light itself while you slept? You know from your own hands. You were the hand that opened the box. You were the hand that lifted the dose to the mouth. You were the hand that paid at the shutter and carried the box home in rain.

The dish did nothing. We did the refilling.

That is why the ending is not a loss. It does plenty TO you. It does nothing FOR you.

Stand there a moment in plain day and let the old bill come back without flinching. Yellow at the fingertips. Sour on the tongue before coffee. Standing under dripping awnings while talk went on inside. Counting in the pocket on a night out. Cough on the stairs while a friend walked on breathing through the nose. We paid those prices daily and called them small because they came in coins. They were not small. They were daily, and they bought stain and ash and minutes cut out of meals.

It never fixed the itch. It caused it.

Did a dose ever file the papers on the desk? Did it mend the quarrel after the phone went down? Did it make bread rise or coffee deepen or the joke funnier? The desk sat the same after ash fell. The bread tasted less because the tongue was filmed. The joke landed while we stood outside watching ember. The credit we gave to paper belonged to the hour, the hands, the company, the pause at the window.

You have left the nicotine trap behind for good.

I smoked through loud hours and bright nights for years and told myself both needed paper. They never did. The loud hour stayed loud for a while and then thinned because hours do. The bright night laughed because people laughed, because glasses clinked, because heaters warmed faces. Paper only rode beside both and sent the bill to me.

Here is the picture to keep on the sill. The ashtray was already empty — you were the one who kept refilling it, and now you have put the habit down like a borrowed coat that was never yours. A borrowed coat does not keep you warm by right. It hangs heavy, it smells of someone else, and when you hand it back your shoulders stand lighter because they were always yours. You did not cut off an arm to return it. You set down what was never cut to fit you.

Ask, with the dish dry in front of you: what did refilling ever add to the day? Did the street look richer through paper? Did the wait pass quicker? Did the work close truer? You have mornings now that answer without argument. The light was light. The list was list. The hands did the work they always did, only without paying for the right to do it.

You wash the dish and you put it back dry. Not hidden in a cupboard as if you feared it. Not filled with coins as if you needed a lesson. Just there, clean, catching sun. A dish does not call. Hands do the calling, and your hands have learned a new order. Kettle first. Window open. Coffee hot. The sill stays dry because you leave it dry, and leaving it dry takes no effort once seeing is clear.

## WHAT YOU CARRY NOW

You carry no kit, no props, no timetable. You carry seeing.

We were conned twice over, and both cons are now plain. There was the small hunger that asked to be fed, and there was the story that named the feeding as help. Leave no dose to feed the Nipper and it stays dead. You see clear now because the Smokescreen has lifted.

If a faint stir passes at coffee or at the bus stop, you know it for what it is, an empty, slightly restless, slightly edgy little tug, and it fools no one now. We once read that stir as proof we needed paper to be ourselves. You sleep hours each night with no dose and wake still you. You sit through films and flights and long talks with no dose and remain you. If paper were the self, those hours would be impossible. They are not impossible. They are ordinary. The daytime stir is the same night-time quiet broken by waking habit, nothing more. Leave it unfed and it thins.

You remember waking stale, breathless and chained, and you know that bedroom is not yours now. The coat that needed spraying before you could hug your own kids smells of nothing but cupboard and wool. The car smells of car, not of tray. The hall does not smell when you open your own door. Those are not bonuses added on top. They are what is left when taking stops.

I tell you from behind the table, not from above you. I stood where you stand, with the chipped mug and the cold lino and the neighbour's chimney smoking, not me. I waited for the sky to fall in the first loud hour. It did not fall. People went on talking. Work went on waiting to be done. Music played whether paper burned or not. The only thing missing was the second noise — where is the next, how many left, when can I step out — and missing it was not missing. It was quiet where noise used to be.

You carry that quiet with you. It does not need winding. It does not need counting. It sits in the chest where the old count used to sit, and it holds through coffee and queues and deadlines. When the old voice says the quiet is fragile, look at the sill. The dish is still dry. The quiet held through yesterday and the day before without help. It will hold through today because today is built the same way, hour by hour, with hands doing what hands do.

We used to carry a pack, a lighter, a backup pack, mints to hide the smell, spray for the coat, arithmetic for the evening. List it now and hear how heavy it was. Pack in pocket. Lighter in other pocket. Counting if one box lasts till morning. Counting if the shop is still open. Counting if you can step out without seeming rude. That was the kit of a kept man. Your pockets now hold keys and coins and a phone. Your head now holds the day. That lightness is not a trick of the first week. It is what remains when the heavy kit is set down.

## BOTH ROPES ARE CUT

Both ropes of the tug-of-war of fear are cut because neither fear belongs to you now. We feared failing to stop and feared living without. Failing meant staying in the dish-refilling round. Living without meant mornings like this one, shop queues like this one, plates that finish to the end. Which of those did you ever need to fear? The dish is dry. The morning stood whole. The fear had no object except the con itself.

We pulled on both ends for years. One hand said I cannot stop. The other said I cannot live without it. Both hands belonged to the same master. The trap pulled the first rope by showing us twenty failed attempts and saying look how weak you are. It pulled the second rope by showing us coffee and breaks and nights out and saying look how empty they will be. We stood in the middle and ached and lit another to quiet the ache the pulling made.

See who held the ropes now. The first rope was the Willpower Method telling us stopping meant pain. The second rope was brainwashing telling us paper gave. Cut the giving lie and the second rope falls. Cut the pain lie and the first rope falls. There is no middle to hold. There is ground to stand on, kettle and keys and work and talk, all standing without paper beside them.

Did you fail before because you were weak? Were you wilful in lighting through cough and rain and shame, and wilful counts for you now? I failed over and over with gum and patches and counts and promises, and each failure taught me nothing about myself and everything about the method I was sold. The method made paper precious by making it forbidden, then sold me the preciousness as proof I needed it. That was not my weakness. That was the trap doing its work.

And did life without ask anything of you that you cannot do? Breathe at the kerb. Taste soup hot. Hear news on the radio. Answer a hard letter. Sit through a laugh without stepping out. You did all of those as a child before paper, and you did all of those in sleep and in films and on flights while still a smoker. The body knows the work without being taught. The day does not need a flare to hold it up.

So let both fears lie where they fell. If a tremor passes and says what if you cannot hold this, answer with the sill. The sill is dry. The morning stood whole. The shop queue stood whole. The plate finished. The loud hour thinned. What exactly is there to fail at when there is nothing to hold up? You are not holding up a weight. You set down a weight. Weights do not need holding once set down.

## WHEN THE THOUGHT POPS

You will hear old lines pop in your own voice. "One with coffee." "One when the glow is warm." "One to get through this row." Hear each for what it is now, hunger talking in a suit, the last echo of a story that sold lack as help. Did three seconds ever pay for the week of counting that followed? Did heaven at lighting ever survive the aftertaste on the tongue? Did a single shared light ever stay single in the diary, or did Friday always bring the same pack? You know from your own pocket and your own morning mouth. The answers came from the senses, not from the story.

Do not argue and do not push the thought into shadow. Answer it the way you answered at that farewell table with the stain seen straight and the crushed butt behind you. The coffee still warms without paper beside it. The night still laughs without paper in it. The row still sits to be mended by hands, not by flare. Then turn to the next thing — keys, coat, kettle, work, talk. Each turning makes the next pop quieter until street and desk and kitchen are only street and desk and kitchen again, with no paper beside them.

I say it still when a lit dose drifts past on the street. I still say YIPPEE! I'M FREE! not because anything pulls, but because I like hearing how true it sounds against the old line. The thought comes, the answer comes quicker, and I turn to the next thing. Each turning makes the next pop quieter until the street is only street again.

If doubt knocks in the first days, welcome it as proof the old story is losing, not as proof you need paper to be yourself. You sleep hours each night with no tube and wake still you. You sit through long mornings and long rides with no tube and remain you. Doubt says what if mornings thin out. Mornings have not thinned. They have thickened with taste and smell and time kept. Doubt says what if work loses its edge. Work has gained edge because no hour is cut in two by stepping out to pay.

Let doubt speak and answer it plain. What did paper ever do for the work except claim credit after? What did paper ever do for coffee except film it? What did paper ever do for the glow except rent a corner of it? The work was yours. The coffee was yours. The glow was yours. Doubt has no object once credit sits where it belongs.

If you ever take a puff after you stop, do not call yourself lost and do not take another to punish yourself. Take it as a rumble strip, a loud warning to hold your seeing tighter, and leave the pack where it is. A rumble strip does not drive the car into the ditch. It shakes the wheel and says look up. Look up. Hold the picture of the tray — plenty taken, nothing given in plain day — and do not let one blink rewrite clear mornings, full taste, steady breath, money kept, time kept. One warns. Two rebuilds. Leave it at warning.

Do not test yourself to prove you are cured. Do not light to check if you still like it. Do not stand in smoke to prove you can. There is nothing to prove. The dish is dry. The proof is in the living, kettle and bus and plate and chairs, day after day without counting. A man who has set down a borrowed coat does not put it on again to check if it still smells. He walks on in his own jacket and forgets the smell by the next corner.

## TWELVE LINES FOR THE POCKET

All you have to do is follow all the instructions.

You did. That is why this reads short. There is no new lesson here, only the lines you already own, set in one place so you can photograph them and walk.

If doubt knocks in the first days, welcome it as proof the old story is losing, not as proof you need paper to be yourself. Answer it plain, you have nothing to lose and everything to gain. You walked out easily, immediately and permanently, and there is no road back.

Keep these twelve where you kept the pack, in pocket and in head:

1. KEEP AN OPEN MIND
2. DON'T STOP OR CUT DOWN YET
3. BEGIN BY FEELING GREAT TO BE ESCAPING
4. FOLLOW ALL THE INSTRUCTIONS
5. IGNORE ANY ADVICE THAT CONFLICTS WITH THIS BOOK
6. DISREGARD ANYONE WHO QUIT BY WILLPOWER
7. REFUSE TO BE INFLUENCED BY OTHER SMOKERS
8. TRUST YOUR BODY TO BREATHE AND TASTE
9. SEE THE SELLER BEHIND THE SMOKE
10. MEET THE BEST CIGARETTE HEAD-ON
11. NEVER ALLOW JUST ONE OR A SPECIAL ONE
12. SMOKE YOUR FINAL CIGARETTE AND KNOW YOU ARE FREE

Read them once in the morning while the kettle mutters. Read them once at night while plates dry. Not to white-knuckle, not to count days, only to hear your own voice saying what your eyes already proved. The list does not hold you. Your seeing holds you, and the list speaks it.

When a lit dose drifts past on the street, do not envy the hand that holds it. See the bill it carries home in pocket and on curtains and in morning mouth. We carried that bill for years. You carry now the dry sill, the clear tongue, the whole talk to the end. Pity keeps the eyes clear where envy would film them. You are not being deprived. They are still refilling.

Carry the list folded small. Show it to no one as an argument. Let it sit beside keys and coins until the words come without paper. KEEP AN OPEN MIND will remind you why you heard this through. DON'T STOP OR CUT DOWN YET will remind you why belief came before behaviour. BEGIN BY FEELING GREAT TO BE ESCAPING will remind you why there was no doom in the reading. FOLLOW ALL THE INSTRUCTIONS will remind you why ease kept its promise. Each line will sound less like a rule and more like a memory. That is how you know it is yours.

## MORNINGS, SHOPS AND WORK THAT STAY WHOLE

Morning will keep coming with the same alarm and the same light. Feet on cold lino. Kettle clicked on. Window cracked to the garden. Coffee smells sharper from the jar because no film sits on the nose. Bread goes down and the crust cracks all the way through because no film sits on the tongue. The phone rings and you answer where you stand. The post comes and you read it at the table. Small things, and they stand whole without paper beside them.

You shave and dress without the old rush to the door. The radio plays the news and you actually hear it, rather than hearing it through the mental count of when you can slip out. You find your shoes without patting every pocket for pack and lighter first. The mirror shows eyes that did not water in wind on the step. The coat smells of nothing but cupboard and wool. You lift the mug and steam warms the nose, and coffee goes down hot and bitter and full, and stays. No second taste comes to spoil it.

Midday will keep coming with milk to fetch and queues to stand in. The shutter will click for the man ahead and paper smell will puff for a second. You will watch his fingers count coins for his box and you will nod to the woman and pay for bread and milk only. The street will smell of rain and fried onions and petrol from the bus, and your nose will take it all without film. The hand that once checked for the pack will check for the key, and find it.

Walk home slow and notice what the walk holds. A bus sighs to the stop. A dog shakes water from its coat. A child drags a scooter and laughs and the wheels rattle on the slabs. Fresh bread smells from the rack. Papers shout their headlines and you read one while you wait, rather than working out if you have enough left to last the evening. The bag sits light in the hand. Lunch ahead will taste of lunch. Ask on the walk what the extra box ever added. Did the street look richer through paper? Did the onions smell deeper? The street was always street. Paper only stood between you and both and charged for the standing.

Work will keep piling and the clock will keep moving. You will finish the paragraph and stand. Kettle, window, three minutes with shoulders dropped and eyes on the yard. The shoulders drop because the pause holds you, not because lack was fed. The page stays done because you did it. The next paragraph comes quicker because the mouth is clear and no minute is spent counting when to step out.

I know that old chain well. I wrote a page and lit without looking, as if lighting were part of writing. I drove short runs and lit, as if driving needed a full stop of grey. I answered a hard letter and lit before reading it back. The work was mine in every case. The timing was mine. The eye that caught the wrong word was mine. Paper arrived after the work and claimed the glow that belonged to finishing. Now the glow stays where it belongs. A finished paragraph feels finished in the body before any hand moves to a pocket. That small squaring of the shoulders, that urge to stand and breathe at the sill, was always the reward. We only taught ourselves to light over the top of it until we could not taste it.

## PLATES, LOUD HOURS AND BRIGHT NIGHTS

Plates will keep finishing. Soup first, steam on the face, salt and heat all the way down. Talk going round, story answering story. No watching the door. No calculating when politeness will allow stepping out. Coffee tastes of coffee. Pudding stays sweet to the last spoon. The finish lasts longer in mouth and in room than any flare ever did.

I ate those meals for years and never let them finish. I took two bites of pudding and went out for a pull, and came back to pudding gone flat and company moved on to another story I had missed. I paid for the meal and then paid again to miss it. I told myself paper rounded the meal. It cut the meal in half and kept the half I paid most for. Now you dine to the end, and the end is yours. Did pudding need paper to be pudding? Did talk need paper to be talk? You know from your own chair. Pudding was pudding. Talk was talk.

Loud hours will come because loud hours come whether paper burns or not. Phone down after a row. Bills spread on the table. Head loud. Stand, go to the window, drink water, walk to the next room and back. Answer the letter in three plain sentences. Put the bills in one pile and date them. Ring the man back when the head is cooler. The row still sits to be mended. The bills still sit to be counted. No ash has fallen on them to make them harder.

Live one loud hour straight and watch what holds without paper. Shoulders drop because the pause holds you and the legs move and the eyes look at distance. I sat at that desk a thousand times with paper. I lit and watched the edgy minute go quiet for seconds and called that quiet help. Then the minute returned asking for the next, now with a second noise added. The desk had two problems after rather than one, and I called the doubling a taking-care-of. It never filed a paper. It never mended a quarrel. It never paid a bill. You will hear the old line in your own voice that a tube would take care of this. Hear it for what it is, the last echo of a story that sold lack as help. Do what does the work, and the hour thins because hours do.

Bright nights will come with chairs outside and talk going on. Heaters glowing, glasses clinking, coats gathered. Go out and sit in that glow and watch straight. The heater warms the face. The drink cools the hand. The story makes the laugh. Stay to the end of the talk. Walk home with a mouth that tastes of supper and drink, not of tray. Coat smells of night, not of paper. Fingers smell of nothing but skin. That is not less night. That is night without rent.

I sat those nights for years with paper in fist. I missed lines while lighting. I missed faces while watching ember. I came back with a stale mouth to a cold glass and called it belonging. Belonging was the chairs and the talk and the night. Paper only rented a corner of it and sent the bill to me. You will see lit tubes moving in that glow. Do not envy them. They are holding less of the night, because part of their night is spent serving the tube — watching it, tapping it, guarding it from wind, counting if another is needed before last orders. You hold the whole night with empty hands.

## THE STREET AHEAD

This is ordinary life lived to the end, plate and paragraph and bus stop and chairs, and it was always yours. Paper only stood between you and both and charged for the standing. Now the ride is over and the days are yours to finish.

Take the bus stop today and the queue tomorrow and the traffic lights the next day, and let each wait be street and faces and weather. Drive if you drive with hands on the wheel and eyes on the road, and arrive with a mouth that does not need mints before you speak. Sit in the waiting room with magazines curled at the corners and let time pass at its own pace whether paper burns or not. Stand at the kettle and at the window and at the yard fence, and let three minutes be three minutes, not a priced length.

We thought waits hurt less because paper burned in them. They grew longer because part of the mind counted pulls and ash and when to light the next. The clear wait is shorter because nothing is counted in it but life going on. A pigeon picks at a crisp packet. A van unloads bread. An old man reads the same notice and shakes his head at the prices. The bus sighs round the corner with rain on its windows, and you step on and sit and look out. Six minutes pass without grey, and they were not empty.

Tell no one you are cured as if you survived an ordeal. Tell no one to stop as if you owned their hands. Let visible ease do the talking. When friends light and offer with a smile and a light that says one will not hurt, smile back and keep your hands where they are. You know what three seconds buys and what week follows. You know heaven at lighting never survived the aftertaste. You know Friday always brought the same pack. There is no need to preach. The clean coat and the clear laugh preach without words.

**SUMMARY**
- The dish on the sill stayed empty on its own; the refilling was learned habit, not need, and the hand that refilled can leave it dry.
- Morning coffee, shop queue, finished work and finished meals stand whole without paper; taste and talk were in the moment all along.
- The faint stir is unfed habit, not self; both failure fear and life-without fear belonged to the con, not to you.
- Old lines still pop at times, and answering with owned seeing leaves each pop quieter until days are only days again.
- The twelve pocket lines speak what eyes already proved; keep them where the pack lived and walk the ordinary street with empty hands.

So get on with enjoying your life.
