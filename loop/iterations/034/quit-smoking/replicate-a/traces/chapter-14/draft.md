Chapter 14
FREE FOR GOOD

**IN THIS CHAPTER**
- The empty ashtray on the sill in morning light
- The farewell table with stain, ash and crushed butt
- The pocket list of twelve spoken lines
- The open street with kettle, keys, bus and evening chairs

*There was never anything to miss, and the sill proves it.*

## THE ASHTRAY WAS ALREADY EMPTY

You stand at the sink with the sill in front of you. The glass dish sits there washed and dry. No grey ring, no stub, no smell rising when the sun hits it. For years we kept that dish full and called the filling ours. We tapped, we stacked, we emptied it last thing and filled it again first thing, and we told ourselves the dish served us.

Look at it now with clear eyes. Did that dish ever fill itself? Did paper ever climb out of the pack and light itself while you slept? You know from your own hands. You were the hand that opened the box. You were the hand that lifted the dose to the mouth. You were the hand that paid at the shutter and carried the box home in rain.

The dish did nothing. We did the refilling.

That is why the ending is not a loss. It does plenty TO you. It does nothing FOR you.

Sit with that ledger for a minute in plain day. Yellow at the fingertips. Sour on the tongue before coffee. Standing under dripping awnings while talk went on inside. Counting in the pocket on a night out. Cough on the stairs while a friend walked on breathing through the nose. We paid those prices daily and called them small because they came in coins. They were not small. They were daily, and they bought stain and ash and minutes cut out of meals.

It never fixed the itch. It caused it.

Did a dose ever file the papers on the desk? Did it mend the quarrel after the phone went down? Did it make bread rise or coffee deepen or the joke funnier? The desk sat the same after ash fell. The bread tasted less because the tongue was filmed. The joke landed while we stood outside watching ember. The credit we gave to paper belonged to the hour, the hands, the company, the pause at the window.

You have left the nicotine trap behind for good.

I smoked through loud hours and bright nights for years and told myself both needed paper. They never did. The loud hour stayed loud for a while and then thinned because hours do. The bright night laughed because people laughed, because glasses clinked, because heaters warmed faces. Paper only rode beside both and sent the bill to me.

Here is the picture to keep on the sill. The ashtray was already empty — you were the one who kept refilling it, and now you have put the habit down like a borrowed coat that was never yours. A borrowed coat does not keep you warm by right. It hangs heavy, it smells of someone else, and when you hand it back your shoulders stand lighter because they were always yours. You did not cut off an arm to return it. You set down what was never cut to fit you.

Ask, with the dish dry in front of you: what did refilling ever add to the day? Did the street look richer through paper? Did the wait pass quicker? Did the work close truer? You have mornings now that answer without argument. The light was light. The list was list. The hands did the work they always did, only without paying for the right to do it.

## WHAT YOU CARRY NOW

You carry no kit, no props, no timetable. You carry seeing.

We were conned twice over, and both cons are now plain. There was the small hunger that asked to be fed, and there was the story that named the feeding as help. Leave no dose to feed the Nipper and it stays dead. You see clear now because the Smokescreen has lifted.

If a faint stir passes at coffee or at the bus stop, you know it for what it is, an empty, slightly restless, slightly edgy little tug, and it fools no one now. We once read that stir as proof we needed paper to be ourselves. You sleep hours each night with no dose and wake still you. You sit through films and flights and long talks with no dose and remain you. If paper were the self, those hours would be impossible. They are ordinary. The daytime stir is the same night-time quiet broken by waking habit, nothing more. Leave it unfed and it thins.

You remember waking stale, breathless and chained, and you know that bedroom is not yours now. The coat that needed spraying before you could hug your own kids smells of nothing but cupboard and wool. The car smells of car, not of tray. The hall does not smell when you open your own door. Those are not bonuses added on top. They are what is left when taking stops.

Both ropes of the tug-of-war of fear are cut because neither fear belongs to you now. We feared failing to stop and feared living without. Failing meant staying in the dish-refilling round. Living without meant mornings like this one, shop queues like this one, plates that finish to the end. Which of those did you ever need to fear? The dish is dry. The morning stood whole. The fear had no object except the con itself.

I tell you from behind the table, not from above you. I stood where you stand, with the chipped mug and the cold lino and the neighbour's chimney smoking, not me. I waited for the sky to fall in the first loud hour. It did not fall. People went on talking. Work went on waiting to be done. Music played whether paper burned or not. The only thing missing was the second noise — where is the next, how many left, when can I step out — and missing it was not missing. It was quiet where noise used to be.

You will hear old lines pop in your own voice. "One with coffee." "One when the glow is warm." "One to get through this row." Hear each for what it is now, hunger talking in a suit, the last echo of a story that sold lack as help. Did three seconds ever pay for the week of counting that followed? Did heaven at lighting ever survive the aftertaste on the tongue? Did a single shared light ever stay single in the diary, or did Friday always bring the same pack? You know from your own pocket and your own morning mouth. The answers came from the senses, not from the story.

Do not argue and do not push the thought into shadow. Answer it the way you answered at that farewell table with the stain seen straight and the crushed butt behind you. The coffee still warms without paper beside it. The night still laughs without paper in it. The row still sits to be mended by hands, not by flare. Then turn to the next thing — keys, coat, kettle, work, talk. Each turning makes the next pop quieter until street and desk and kitchen are only street and desk and kitchen again, with no paper beside them.

If you ever take a puff after you stop, do not call yourself lost and do not take another to punish yourself. Take it as a rumble strip, a loud warning to hold your seeing tighter, and leave the pack where it is. A rumble strip does not drive the car into the ditch. It shakes the wheel and says look up. Look up. Hold the picture of the tray — plenty taken, nothing given in plain day — and do not let one blink rewrite clear mornings, full taste, steady breath, money kept, time kept. One warns. Two rebuilds. Leave it at warning.

## TWELVE LINES FOR THE POCKET

All you have to do is follow all the instructions.

You did. That is why this reads short. There is no new lesson here, only the lines you already own, set in one place so you can photograph them and walk.

If doubt knocks in the first days, welcome it as proof the old story is losing, not as proof you need paper to be yourself. Answer it plain, you have nothing to lose and everything to gain. You walked out easily, immediately and permanently, and there is no road back.

Keep these twelve where you kept the pack, in pocket and in head:

1. KEEP AN OPEN MIND
2. DON'T STOP OR CUT DOWN YET
3. BEGIN BY FEELING GREAT TO BE ESCAPING
4. FOLLOW ALL THE INSTRUCTIONS
5. IGNORE ANY ADVICE THAT CONFLICTS WITH THIS BOOK
6. DISREGARD ANYONE WHO QUIT BY WILLPOWER
7. REFUSE TO BE INFLUENCED BY OTHER SMOKERS
8. TRUST YOUR BODY TO BREATHE AND TASTE
9. SEE THE SELLER BEHIND THE SMOKE
10. MEET THE BEST CIGARETTE HEAD-ON
11. NEVER ALLOW JUST ONE OR A SPECIAL ONE
12. SMOKE YOUR FINAL CIGARETTE AND KNOW YOU ARE FREE

Read them once in the morning while the kettle mutters. Read them once at night while plates dry. Not to white-knuckle, not to count days, only to hear your own voice saying what your eyes already proved. The list does not hold you. Your seeing holds you, and the list speaks it.

When a lit dose drifts past on the street, do not envy the hand that holds it. See the bill it carries home in pocket and on curtains and in morning mouth. We carried that bill for years. You carry now the dry sill, the clear tongue, the whole talk to the end. Pity keeps the eyes clear where envy would film them. You are not being deprived. They are still refilling.

## THE STREET AHEAD

Morning will keep coming with the same alarm and the same light. Feet on cold lino. Kettle clicked on. Window cracked to the garden. Coffee smells sharper from the jar because no film sits on the nose. Bread goes down and the crust cracks all the way through because no film sits on the tongue. The phone rings and you answer where you stand. The post comes and you read it at the table. Small things, and they stand whole without paper beside them.

Midday will keep coming with milk to fetch and queues to stand in. The shutter will click for the man ahead and paper smell will puff for a second. You will watch his fingers count coins for his box and you will nod to the woman and pay for bread and milk only. The street will smell of rain and fried onions and petrol from the bus, and your nose will take it all without film. The hand that once checked for the pack will check for the key instead, and find it.

Work will keep piling and the clock will keep moving. You will finish the paragraph and stand. Kettle, window, three minutes with shoulders dropped and eyes on the yard. The shoulders drop because the pause holds you, not because lack was fed. The page stays done because you did it. The next paragraph comes quicker because the mouth is clear and no minute is spent counting when to step out.

Plates will keep finishing. Soup first, steam on the face, salt and heat all the way down. Talk going round, story answering story. No watching the door. No calculating when politeness will allow stepping out. Coffee tastes of coffee. Pudding stays sweet to the last spoon. The finish lasts longer in mouth and in room than any flare ever did.

Loud hours will come because loud hours come whether paper burns or not. Phone down after a row. Bills spread on the table. Head loud. Stand, go to the window, drink water, walk to the next room and back. Answer the letter in three plain sentences. Put the bills in one pile and date them. Ring the man back when the head is cooler. The row still sits to be mended. The bills still sit to be counted. No ash has fallen on them to make them harder.

Bright nights will come with chairs outside and talk going on. Heaters glowing, glasses clinking, coats gathered. Go out and sit in that glow and watch straight. The heater warms the face. The drink cools the hand. The story makes the laugh. Stay to the end of the talk. Walk home with a mouth that tastes of supper and drink, not of tray. Coat smells of night, not of paper. Fingers smell of nothing but skin. That is not less night. That is night without rent.

This is ordinary life lived to the end, plate and paragraph and bus stop and chairs, and it was always yours. Paper only stood between you and both and charged for the standing. Now the ride is over and the days are yours to finish.

So get on with enjoying your life.

YIPPEE! I'M FREE!

**SUMMARY**
- The dish on the sill stayed empty on its own; the refilling was learned habit, not need, and the hand that refilled can leave it dry.
- Morning coffee, shop queue, finished work and finished meals stand whole without paper; taste and talk were in the moment all along.
- The faint stir is unfed habit, not self; both failure fear and life-without fear belonged to the con, not to you.
- Old lines still pop at times, and answering with owned seeing leaves each pop quieter until days are only days again.
- The twelve pocket lines speak what eyes already proved; keep them where the pack lived and walk the ordinary street with empty hands.
