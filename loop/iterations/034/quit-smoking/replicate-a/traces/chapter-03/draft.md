Chapter 3
WHAT DOES IT ACTUALLY DO FOR YOU?

IN THIS CHAPTER
The ledger on the kitchen table, the plate just cleared, the pause between jobs, the hug that will not let go.

*No cigarette ever did anything for you — it only seemed to help because it had hurt you first.*

## The Scales That Keep You Lit

We all carry a private set of scales.

On one side we pile the harm. The cough in the morning. The stairs that leave us short of breath. The money burned week after week. The yellow on the fingers, the smell in the coat, the hiding in the garage we looked at together. The doctor's warning folded in the drawer.

On the other side we pile what we tell ourselves we get. The calm. The pause. The reward after work. The taste after a meal. The company on a night out. The little lift when we are flat.

And then we weigh.

"I know it harms me, but I enjoy it." "I know I should stop, but what would I do for stress?" "I know it costs, but it is my pleasure."

I know that weighing well. I did it for thirty years. I did it every time the price rose, every time I coughed blood-flecked phlegm into a sink, every time my children waved smoke from their faces. Yes, it harms me, I would say, but what would life be without it? Flat. Grey. No breaks. No reward. No help when nerves bite.

Listen to what that weighing assumes. It assumes there is something good on the second pan. It assumes the cigarette does harm, true, and also does good, true, and you must decide whether the good is worth the harm.

The fact is, the second pan is empty.

I am not asking you whether smoking does more harm than good. I am asking a plainer question, and I want you to let it sit without answering too quickly.

What good does it do at all?

If a man sold you a coat that let in rain, tore at the seams, stank of chemicals and cost you a month's wages every year, you would not stand weighing whether the coat was worth it because you liked the colour of one button. You would ask what the coat does for you as a coat. Does it keep you warm and dry? If not, the colour of the button is not a benefit. It is decoration on a failure.

So it is with the dose. Do not weigh harm against pleasure yet. First ask whether the pleasure is there at all.

Have you ever examined a single supposed gift without the smoke around it? Have you ever asked, of food, of a pause, of company, what did the paper tube add that was not already there? That is what we will do together now, calmly, without asking you to change anything you do.

## Say Aloud What You Think You Get

Be an investigator for a minute. Say your reasons aloud, in your own words, as you have said them to yourself.

"It relaxes me."

"It is my reward. My little party after I finish something."

"I like the taste, especially after a meal."

"It helps me concentrate. It gives me a break."

"It is sociable. It belongs with a drink and friends."

I have heard every one of those sentences from my own mouth. I said them loudly in pubs and quietly at kitchen tables. I believed them because I felt something when I lit. I am not telling you that you feel nothing. I am asking you to look at where that feeling really comes from.

Take your strongest one. Hold it up. Do not defend it yet and do not attack it. Just hold it as you would hold a dose between fingers and look at it.

If I asked you when you first started, as a boy or girl coughing behind the bike sheds, eyes watering, head spinning, would you have listed any of those gifts? Did you say, ah, this steadies my nerves? Did you say, what concentration, what taste, what company? You said nothing of the sort. You coughed and persisted because the older ones looked certain and grown, because the world told you this meant calm and belonging.

We learned the gifts afterwards. The trap taught us the words after it had caught us, and we fitted the words to the lighting the way a secretary invents minutes for a meeting that never happened.

Ask yourself, honestly and without gloom:

If these gifts were real, why do you need to keep reminding yourself of them while non-smokers live full days of food and pauses and company and never once feel the lack? If the gifts were in the cigarette, would not the non-smoker miss them?

And if the cigarette gives you what you name, why does the wanting return an hour later exactly as strong, whatever gift you just took? What gift must be taken twenty times a day and still leaves you empty?

I know this is hard to accept. Your mind will answer: but I do enjoy some of them. Surely you cannot deny I enjoy it.

I do not deny what you feel. I deny only what you are told it means.

## After Food, Between Jobs, Among Friends

Think of a meal just finished.

The plate cleared. The taste still on the tongue. The body settling. A natural full stop. You lean back. For a non-smoker that moment is complete in itself. The food was the pleasure. The pause is the body enjoying having eaten.

What does the smoker do at that same full stop? He breaks it to light. He fills his mouth with hot paper and stale ash. He dulls the very taste he claims to crown. He coughs, clears his throat, and twenty minutes later his mouth is dry and sour in a way the non-smoker's is not.

Did the dose add to the food, or did it interrupt it?

We tell ourselves the cigarette finishes the meal. The fact is, the meal was already finished. The pleasure was the food, the company, the sitting still. The dose only sneaked a ride on a pleasure that was complete without it, then claimed the credit and spoiled the aftertaste for its fee.

Think of work finished. Teaching the class. Closing the paragraph. Driving the long miles or the short run to the shop. You finish, you stretch, you feel that clean lift of done. That lift is yours. You earned it with attention and effort.

"Cigarettes were my reward for…well, almost everything. Teaching a class. Finishing a story. Finishing a paragraph. Driving 500 miles. Driving to the grocery store."

Do you hear it? Almost everything. The reward attached itself to any finished act, not because the act needed it, but because the trap needs to be paid after any stretch without. The party was not the work. The party was the lighting. The work was already the joy, and the dose stole the applause on the way out.

Keep the joy of the work intact. I am not asking you to like your job less or your drive less. I am asking you to see who did the work and who took the bow.

Think of the pause. The phone down. The kettle on. Two minutes with nothing to do. The non-smoker stretches, looks out, breathes, and returns. The smoker spends the two minutes feeding a need, watching the clock, half outside in the cold, half thinking of the next thing, then returns with a tighter chest and calls that a break.

Did the pause rest you, or did the lighting end the restlessness that the last dose had left, and call that ending rest?

Think of company. Laughter. The table full. You believe the dose joins you in. But company was company before you ever smoked. Drink, talk, warmth, belonging — none of those was made of smoke. The dose sat at the edge of the pleasure and took credit for the night, the way a man who jumps into a wedding photograph claims he made the wedding.

Isolate it for a second. Take the food, the finished job, the pause, the friends — and take the paper tube out of the picture. What is missing? The taste is still there. The done is still there. The rest is still there. The laughter is still there. What is missing is only the nip being quieted for a minute, and the ash that follows.

It is the other way around. You do not enjoy these moments because you smoke. You smoke during these moments and believe afterwards that smoking made them.

## Heaven At The Light

"the moment I light up I'm in heaven, but feel shitty afterwards. So whilst I love smoking I hate being a smoker"

If that is your voice, I have warmth for you, because it was mine. It sounds like a contradiction. It is exact reporting.

Press that heaven for detail, as an investigator. When does it arrive, and how long does it stay?

It arrives at the strike. The first draw after a gap. For a few seconds there is a settling, an ah, that is better. The shoulders drop a fraction. The tug quietens.

Then what? The second draw is duller. By the middle the stick is hot and the taste is stale paper. By the end you are glad to put it out. The mouth is sour. The chest is tight. The fingers smell. You feel, in your own true word, shitty afterwards — not lifted, not crowned, but used.

That little flare is real. I will never tell you that you imagine it. You feel it as truly as warmth from a fire.

What I want you to see is how small its home is, and how large the rent is.

The enjoyment you name lives at the light only. It does not stretch across the day. At three in the afternoon with a raw throat, at midnight with an ashtray mouth carried to bed, at dawn clearing and coughing before the first dose, there is no heaven. There is only being a smoker for the whole day to rent three seconds at the tip.

Ask yourself two plain questions and answer only to yourself.

If the thing itself were heaven, would attention make it worse? Smoke one with full attention next time, tasting only it. Does the pleasure grow as you attend, like music or food, or does it fall apart into heat and ash? What pleasure fades when you look at it?

And if that first puff truly gives, why does it need to be paid for with hating being a smoker for the rest of the day? What gift demands hiding, planning, counting how many till morning, choosing seats near doors, standing in rain, spraying the jacket, lying about fresh air? Does a gift make you hide?

Both feelings you named are true. You love the lighting because after a gap the body cries to be fed and the first puff quiets the cry for seconds. You hate being a smoker because for the rest of the day you carry the chain that makes the cry certain. The heaven does not prove the dose gives. It proves how tightly the taking has hold.

## The Hug That Will Not Let Go

Picture a man pulled shivering from a river, wrapped in strong arms, grateful beyond words. He clings. He thanks his rescuer. He says, you saved me, you steadied me, I could not have got through without you.

Then, in the cold light, he sees the same arms were the ones that held his head under moments before. The rescue was a pause in a drowning the rescuer himself was doing. The gratitude was real. The rescue was not.

That is your position with the paper tube, and I say it with complete warmth for the man in the water, because we all swam there. The cigarette is that rescuer. Not a person, not a friend, not an enemy with a face — the dose itself, playing both parts.

You reach in stress and feel taken care of for a minute. You reach after food and feel the meal crowned for a minute. You reach in company and feel joined for a minute. The arms close. The tug quietens. You feel grateful.

What you are not shown in that minute is the other half of the arms: the same dose that made the tug certain an hour later, that blunted taste, that broke the pause, that divided you onto the cold step. It does plenty TO you. It does nothing FOR you.

Say that slowly to yourself, not as a slogan, as a description of what you have just watched.

It does plenty TO you. The dry mouth, the tight chest, the stale end, the planning, the hiding, the counting, the cough cleared before anyone hears, the chain worn all day for three seconds at the light. That TO is enormous. It touches money, breath, clothes, evenings, mornings, love.

It does nothing FOR you. The food was the food. The done was yours. The pause was rest already. The company was company. The calm after the puff left every cause untouched on the desk where you left it. That FOR is empty. Not small. Empty.

We were conned into reading the quieting of a need as a gift. Once you see the need was made by the very thing that quiets it, the gift disappears and only the taking remains.

That is the switch. Not harm against benefit, weighed forever. Harm only, once the false benefit is removed.

3. BEGIN BY FEELING GREAT TO BE ESCAPING
There is no doom here, only freedom ahead.

SUMMARY
- The scales of harm against pleasure keep you lit because they assume a pleasure is there.
- The gifts you name were learned after you were caught, not reasons you started.
- Food, finished work, pauses and company were complete without smoke.
- What you love lives only at the light for seconds, then turns stale.
- Loving the light can live beside hating being a smoker all day.
- The rescuer that quiets the tug is the same thing that caused it.
