Chapter 12
ORDINARY DAYS

**IN THIS CHAPTER**
Morning kettle and bread, shop aisle and till, lunch table, passing paper, evening sofa, same streets walked plain.

*The plain day holds on its own; there is nothing to miss and nothing to police.*

### THE KETTLE BOILS AS USUAL

You wake to the same kettle, the same street noise, the same pale line at the curtain edge. Feet to floor. Tap on. Kettle ticking up. Cup down. Oats in the pan. Bread on the board.

Pour water. Cut bread. Sit down hungry and eat.

That is the whole morning. No post posted at the cupboard. No bargain struck before toast. Hunger calls, food answers, the bowl empties, hands wash the spoon, feet find coat and door.

We once read mornings as a test. Would the mouth hold till eleven? Would the drawer call at ten? Would the afternoon already throw its shadow over breakfast? That reading belonged to dosed years, when every quiet hour was measured against the next hit and every settled stomach was heard as lack.

Ask what this morning has to show to earn its place. Does bread eaten hungry keep you through to lunch? You are holding the answer in your own hands. Does porridge with berries leave the mouth clean and water tasting clean after it? You taste it now. Does tea drunk plain still warm the hands and open the day? The cup is warm in your hand while you read this.

I stood at my own sink on the first such morning and waited for orders. No orders came. A thin picture passed — foil, smell, hurry — and went on past like a bus in rain. No debate in the head. No arithmetic. Porridge, toast, apple, out the door.

Pack the bag the same way. Keys, wallet, papers, fruit for later. The bus comes at the same minute. The pavement smells of rain and bakeries and bus fumes. Notice how the legs walk without commentary. Notice how the mouth, uncoated, tastes the cold air. We used to walk to work already counting squares for ten o'clock. Now the head is empty of sums and full of the street.

If a hollow flicker comes at half past ten, look at it straight. Where does it call from? Broad hunger welcomes bread and soup and fruit and waits for the plate. The narrow picture names one powder only and cannot wait. A voice that changes with the clock and the cupboard is not an organ speaking. It is a lesson unlearning itself.

Do not feed the picture to check it. Do not wrestle it to prove anything. Turn to the hour in front of you: bag packed, keys found, desk opened, walk taken, call made. Drink water. Stretch the shoulders. Open the window a crack. Each plain breakfast lets mid-morning go quiet earlier. Each quiet mid-morning lets lunch taste stronger.

"Just one sweet beside tea and the day starts right."

We spoke that line for years. Ask your own mornings to answer it. When did one beside tea start the day right? It started hurry, coated tongue, ten minutes of chatter in the mouth, then a louder call at eleven. When did a plain bowl start the day wrong? It started steady hands, clear mouth, hours that did not ask for sealing. Which starter served the day? Which starter billed it?

The kettle boils as usual. So do you. Hunger, food, satisfaction, movement. Nothing sealed. Nothing missed. The day opens plain and stays open.

Shower, dress, teeth, door. Notice how ordinary the mouth feels when nothing coats it. Notice how tea tastes of tea. Notice how toast chews and rests. Small notices, piled hour by hour, rebuild what years of flood washed out: trust in appetite to speak and to stop.

You do not need to guard the morning. Live it. The morning keeps itself.

### THE AISLE AND THE TILL

Go to the same shop at the same hour down the same aisle.

Nothing has moved except your seeing. Packs still glow in rows. Cartoon faces still grin. Small bars still wait stacked by the till where idle hands hover. The bell over the door still rings for each incomer.

We walked this aisle for years with eyes half closed and hands half open, telling ourselves we chose each bar while the hand moved before the choosing. Now walk it with eyes open and hands quiet.

Push the trolley past the bright wall. Look once, steadily, at what the wall sells. Sweet only, loud only, gone only to return. Bread holds for hours. Soup holds for hours. A dosed mouth holds for minutes and calls again louder. The ticket tells one price. The mouth tells the other: film on teeth, thirst after swallow, hurry in fingers, picture already forming of the next close.

You need no detour and no armour. Take your usual route. Queue at the usual till. Stand beside the same glow holding your basket of dinner. If you ever feel faint, ill, or distressed around food, put this book aside and get care promptly and follow your clinician first.

That guard stands outside the argument. Inside the argument the shape is simple. You came for dinner, not for a dose. Dinner sits in vegetables, bread, fish, oil, eggs, fruit. The bar by the till never sat at a table and never closed hunger. It closed nothing. It opened the next opening.

Pick up potatoes and feel their weight. Smell the lemons. Choose cheese with a clear nose. The hands that once hovered at the bright wall now squeeze bread for freshness and turn fruit for ripeness. Same hands. Same hour. Different orders, because different seeing gives the orders now.

Look at the trolley ahead piled high with family packs. We were that trolley. Two big bags for the week that would not see Wednesday. A mother doing sums in her head about Friday. A father telling himself the small bars are for the children. A child already taught that love arrives wrapped.

Does that mountain look like enjoyment? Watch the faces, not the wrappers. Tight smiles. Quick glances at the pile. A joke that sounds a little too loud about needing supplies. We know the evening that follows that pile: rustle, hurry, crumbs brushed fast, regret rising before the swallow has settled. We know the morning that follows: coated tongue, flat hour, fresh vow made through a filmed mouth.

You left the Sugar Trap the night you saw the loop plain. They stand where we stood, still reading glow as kindness. Let pity rise, not envy. Envy says they hold something you lack. Pity says they carry something you laid down. Which reading fits what you saw on your own kitchen table that last night — coating, hurry, thirst, want?

Say nothing unless asked. Preaching never opened a mind that pictures still hold shut. If asked, speak plain and short and turn back to your basket: "I closed that. I eat when hungry and stop when satisfied." No lecture. No story. No warning about their trolley. A quiet buyer with a clear mouth and steady hands teaches more than a sermon at the till.

Pay with a clear mouth. Coins counted. Paper bags folded over full food. Bell ringing for the next in line. Walk out with bags that hold you till tomorrow. Notice the air outside tasting clean. Notice the hands swinging light. Notice the mind not already counting squares for the evening. Small notices. Plain day. Full basket.

The shop has not changed. The buyer has.

### A MEAL THAT CLOSES ITSELF

Eat lunch hungry at the ordinary table.

Market carrots, torn bread, lentil soup poured hot, oil, salt, an apple cut slow. Notice, savour, stop when satisfied. No chart. No count. The body leads and the plate tells you when.

We once called dessert love and feared eating would turn grey without it. Ask whose love that was. Was it the bar that warmed the table, or faces and hunger met and talk running long? Did a sweet seal ever close a meal calm? It closed it to hurry and thirst and picturing the next. When did soup hurry you? When did bread leave thirst and film? When did an apple eaten hungry call for its brother within minutes?

Isolate the hour and the answer writes itself. The meal settles for hours. The hit blanks for minutes and bills for hours after. One behaves like food. The other behaves like a bell.

I ate such a meal on day two — soup, bread, cheese, pears, water — and waited for the grey we were promised. No grey came. Colour came up stronger. Salt tasted saltier. Water tasted clean to the back teeth. Jaw unclenched. Eyes cleared. A laugh I had not planned came up with the clearing. That laugh was not hype. It was recognition: hours of quiet bought by nothing but hunger met.

Sit a little longer after the plate is empty. Let the stomach report. There is no hurry to clear, no hunt to start, no thirst to answer. Talk runs on. Hands rest. The window light moves across the cloth. We used to leave the table with mouths buzzing and minds already in the cupboard. Now the table lets you go when it is done with you.

Eat till the body says enough, like a small child who drops half a peach and runs off to play.

That picture is your authority now, not a slogan. A satisfied mouth stops mid-bite without grief. A lit mouth hunts a box after the box stands empty. Which mouth would you trust to tell you when enough is enough? Which mouth behaves like an organ and which behaves like a taught hour?

If tired hands open a cupboard from old steps at dusk, let the steps go unwalked. Pour water. Warm leftovers. Cut cheese. Sit and eat till satisfied and put the plate down. Satisfaction closes the bowl without sums. The taught hour rings; you do not answer; the ringing thins.

"But a meal without something sweet feels unfinished."

Whose finish is that? Ask your own table. When did the sweet finish finish anything? It finished calm and started hunting. It finished talk and started rustle. A true finish leaves the mouth clean and the mind on the evening ahead. A false finish leaves the mouth filmed and the mind on the cupboard. You have lived both finishes hundreds of times. You know which one finished.

Bread, soup, fruit, water. Chewed slow. Stopped at enough. Plate cleared. Mouth clean. Hours ahead unbooked. That is a meal that closes itself.

### WHEN A THOUGHT WALKS PAST

You cannot stop thoughts arriving. Nor should you try.

On the third afternoon, at the old hour, a picture will walk past: foil and smell, bright shelf, old chair. On the fifth evening, tired after work, it will knock again. That is not failure. That is old post being cleared.

We spent years believing a thought was an order. It is not. A thought of sweet is paper through the door. You read it, smile, answer, get on with the hour.

Do not push it away. Do not hold it close. Pushing feeds it with attention. Holding feeds it with savour. Seeing names it and lets it pass. See it for what it is — the Nibbler starving, grumbling as it thins — and answer at once with the thought you will keep for good:

BRILLIANT! I'M FREE!

Think it as gladness, not as effort. Think it at the till when packs glow. Think it on the sofa when the old hour rings. Think it with a clear plate at a birthday table. Each thought of sweet becomes a hit of gladness that you no longer feed the loop. The intruder becomes the reminder. The reminder becomes the prize.

Ask two questions and let your own evenings answer. If picturing helped, when did an evening of picturing close calmer? It billed the night till morning with rustle and thirst. If picturing proved need, why does true hunger welcome many foods while this picture names one powder only? Need eats dinner. Lessons eat pictures.

I tell you as one who heard that knock for a week: named, it shrinks. "There you are. Passing." Then turn to faces, work, walk, meal ahead. Each unanswered evening the bell rings duller. Each plain meal the tongue wakes more. Mornings come up clean and the mouth feels clean in a quiet ordinary way that needs no sermon.

A thought is not a dose. A picture is not a mouthful. Keep the picture seen and the hand stays quiet without commentary in the head.

"But if I keep thinking it, does that mean I still want it?"

Ask what wanting does. Wanting that is yours seeks food and rests when fed. This picture seeks one hit and wakes when fed. When did feeding it ever quiet it for long? Ten minutes of blanking, then louder calling. When did seeing it ever strengthen it? Seeing thins it, day by day, till the hour passes without a knock and you notice the quiet only after.

Some days the paper comes twice. Some days not at all. Neither proves anything about character. The postman walks his old round for a while after you move house. Let him walk. The house is empty of orders. You do not need to police hours or places. You need only to own the answer. The answer is already owned. You spoke it on vow night with hand on table. Speak it again in shorthand each time paper comes through the door. One line. Glad. Done. Hour resumed.

Paper comes. You smile. You answer. You live on. That is how a passing thought stays passing.

### THE EVENING SOFA

Evening keeps its chair, its lamp, its talk, its programme. Only the seal changes.

Sit where you always sit. Cup of tea. Blanket over knees. Street noise low. The old hour rings — half past eight, lights low — and for a second the hand pictures chocolate beside laughter when the lights go down.

See whose glow that was. Was it the bar that made the room warm, or the story and the company and the hour to rest? Run the same warmth without a dose and sweetness stays unchanged, because sweetness never came coated. Laughter stayed when the mouth was clear. Rest stayed when the night stayed plain. The bar only rode on an evening that already shone.

We dosed for years to close evenings. Ask what the close ever closed. Did the bar shorten a hard day or coat it and add a second hard hour? Did it deepen talk or hurry it between bites? Did sleep come sooner or later with a filmed mouth? You lived the answers: hurry, crumbs, thirst, mind already picturing the next close before the programme ended.

Stay through the middle of the programme without leaving the chair to hunt. Notice how the story holds without a wrapper to crack. Notice how tea cools and is drunk and is enough. Notice how the blanket stays clean and the side table stays clear. Hours we once cut into bites now run whole. Talk we once paused for chewing now runs uncut.

Look across the street at lit windows where others still close the day sweet. Bowls out. Wrappers bright. Hands moving fast. Does that look like enjoyment? Watch close: chewing to finish, not to taste; eyes half on screen, half on packet; a pause to bargain about having just one more. You are not missing that. That table is billed while it chews.

Let pity rise there too, not envy. Envy reads a lit window as warmth you lack. Pity reads it as noise you left. Which reading fits your own sofa last year — calm or coated? Settled or hunting? You know, because your sofa remembers.

Do not preach to them. Preaching breeds defence and drags you back into arguments that reopen your own doubt. If a friend presses a plate, smile and speak short: "No thanks — I closed that." No lecture. No history. No warning about sugar. If they ask more, tell one plain fact from your own week: mornings come up clean, meals hold for hours. Then turn back to talk, programme, tea. Visible quiet recruits better than words.

Keep your seat through the whole programme. Tea drunk slow. Mouth clear. Sleep coming sooner. Morning starting sooner. Notice the lamp click off without rustle. Notice the blanket folded without crumbs. Notice the teeth brushed clean without film. Small notices. Plain night. Full rest.

Sit till the room goes quiet. Kettle, street, breath, clear plate. Nothing to mourn. A small, slightly silly joy starts to rise when you feel the picture thin. Let it rise and settle. The sofa holds you without a seal.

### NOTHING ELSE MOVES

Change nothing else in your life.

Same café. Same walk. Same friends. Same work break at three. Same birthday table. The body returns to its own timing on its own; elaborate scaffolding only tells the mind something was lost.

We once rearranged days to dodge hours, as if hours held power. Hours hold no power. Beliefs hold power, and yours has changed. Go where you always went. Order what you always ordered, minus the sweet hit. Drink coffee as coffee. Eat cake-hour as bread and fruit and talk.

Ask what dodging would have to prove to earn its place. It would have to show that places pour doses down throats. They do not. Hands lift. Pictures order. Keep pictures seen and places stay neutral.

I kept my route, my bakery, my cinema seat. The bakery smells the same. The seat holds the same. Only the hand stays still, because the mind no longer reads smell as need. First visit the smell knocked loud. Second visit it knocked duller. Third visit it was background, like polish on wood. No commentary. Just a smell passing while I chose bread.

Take the same work break at three. Same stairs. Same faces round the kettle. We used to hear three o'clock as proof the blood needed sugar. Now three o'clock is tea and air and a stretch and ten minutes of talk. The hour did not change. The reading changed. A clock never ordered a bar. A picture did.

If wobble comes at an open door, let it be habit, not warning. Habit paces old steps by the doorway from long use. Warning protects from harm ahead. This pacing protects nothing. It only repeats steps learned when the bell rang nightly. Steps learned can stay unwalked tonight. An open doorway needs no courage when you see the cell was never home.

Two knocks will come in the next weeks in your own voice. Hear them now so they arrive pre-answered.

The first knocks on a hard day: "Foul day. One will see me through. Just this once to close it."

Ask whose help that one ever gave. Did the bar shorten hard days or coat them? Did the evening close calmer or louder? Did morning thank you? You know the ledger because you paid it: brief blanking, fast dip, roller days you said you wanted to leave. A hard day needs food, water, sleep, a kind word, an early bed — not a coated mouth and a second hard hour. Answer the knock with what the day taught: the dose never closed a day; it billed it.

The second knocks on a good day: "All clear now. One to prove command. One cannot hurt."

That proof is the oldest trap-door in the book. One lit dose re-tunes the bell for next week. One teaches the mouth again that evenings close sweet. When did one stay one last year without a fight that spoiled the hour — bargaining, chewing slow to make it last, hunting after with taste still up? Bread needs no count. Soup needs no count. Only a lit bell needs counting, and the count itself proves the lighting. Keep no lighting and no count is needed.

If a slip happens — a spoon in sauce unnoticed, a bite pressed on you before thought caught up — do not turn a jolt into a steering policy. Note what lit the bell — late night, bright counter, old hour — and walk on with eyes ahead. A rumble strip at the road edge warns; it does not steer. A driver who feels the rumble steadies and drives on. He does not swing for the ditch to study the strip. Guard the picture and the hand stays quiet. A crumb met with clear seeing revives nothing. A crumb met with mourning relights the picture. Let the lesson be about the bell, never proof the road owns the driver.

A seatbelt waits for an accidental jolt; it is not a tool for erratic driving. Your body absorbs a blip. Your mind must not book the next. That is the whole margin: body copes, mind does not book.

Walk on in ordinary days with ordinary baskets and ordinary plates. The grumble thins. Taste wakes. Hunger speaks plain. Satisfaction closes without sums.

BRILLIANT! I'M FREE!

13. NEVER REOPEN THE DECISION
A passing thought is a dead enemy, not an order.

**SUMMARY**

- Mornings start clear on bread and porridge eaten hungry, with no sweet seal needed to open the day.
- The same aisle and till stay neutral when the glow is seen as film and hurry on the mouth.
- Plain meals eaten hungry hold for hours and close at enough without counting or charts.
- Satisfaction stops mid-bite and runs off to the day, while a lit mouth hunts a box after the box stands empty.
- A passing picture is paper through the door, read and answered with gladness, never obeyed as an order.
- The evening sofa keeps its warmth because story and company owned the glow all along.
- Other trolleys piled high call for pity, not envy, and no preaching at the till or table.
- A missed crumb teaches about the bell and never steers the week toward booking.
- Same café, same route, same seat prove places never poured and hours never ordered.
