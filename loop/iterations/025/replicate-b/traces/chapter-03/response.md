Chapter 3
WHAT DOES IT DO FOR YOU

**IN THIS CHAPTER**
The ordinary Tuesday. The till receipt. The bathroom mirror. The desk drawer at four. The sofa and the empty packet. The two columns.

*BAD SUGAR does a great deal to you and nothing for you, and once you look for the benefit you find an empty column.*

**THE WRONG COURTROOM**

Good. You saw the hand move before the decision.

You came with a full cupboard and an open mind, and you watched yourself at ten at night reach before you had chosen, eat fast without joy, fold the packet over as if folding could undo it. That was not weakness. That was being caught. Keep that seeing with you. We need it for the next click, because this next click turns the whole case around.

Up to now you have been arguing the case in the wrong courtroom.

You have been asking, does BAD SUGAR harm me more than it pleases me. Is the harm big enough to make me change. Can I live with the harm if the pleasure is real. You weigh teeth against birthdays. You weigh weight against comfort on a hard day. You weigh worry against that bright minute at three in the afternoon when something sweet hits the tongue.

We all argued it that way. I argued it that way for years. It feels sensible. It feels adult. It feels honest to say, I know it is not perfect, but life has to have some joy.

The fact is, it is a con trick of a question.

While you weigh harm against benefit you have already granted the benefit. You have let the other side smuggle its main lie into court as an agreed fact. The judge has accepted without evidence that there is a pleasure on the scales, and now the only argument is how heavy the harm must be to outweigh it.

No wonder you never left that courtroom free. No wonder every scare faded by Friday. A scare can make you uneasy for a night. It can never remove a pleasure you believe is real. You simply tell yourself, I know it harms a little, but I need something sweet to get me through, I deserve a little joy.

So let us close that courtroom and open the true one.

I am not asking you whether BAD SUGAR harms you more than it helps you. I am asking you what it helps you at all.

Forget the harm for an hour. Put teeth and weight and worry to one side. Let the prosecution rest. Let the defence speak alone. Tell me one true benefit that remains when the noise clears.

You say you love sweet hits and would miss them. I hear you with warmth, because I said the same with complete sincerity. I am not mocking what you feel. I am asking you to test what you feel against your own ordinary day, as an investigator, not as a defendant.

What if there was never anything on the benefit side of the scales?

I know this is hard to accept. Of course it is. You have felt the flash. You have felt the sigh after the first bite. To hear there was nothing there can sound like I am denying your own tongue. I am not denying the flash. I am asking what the flash left behind an hour later, and whether what it left behind can honestly be called a benefit.

Be an investigator for one Tuesday. Bring your usual doses with you. Do not be good. Do not be heroic. Watch and log. The audit will do what argument cannot.

**AN ORDINARY TUESDAY, LOGGED HONESTLY**

Take an ordinary Tuesday. Not Christmas. Not a wedding. Not a holiday where everything glows. A plain Tuesday, the kind we live most of our lives inside.

Morning. You mean well. Tea or coffee. Perhaps a sweetened cereal or a pastry grabbed on the way because the house was rushed, children calling, keys missing, rain at the door. It tastes bright for a minute. Bright and quick and gone. By mid-morning your concentration frays at the edges. You feel edgy and blunt at once. You tell yourself you need sugary somethings to get me through to lunch.

Midday. A sandwich and crisps and a sweet drink, eaten fast over a screen, emails stacking, news playing. No real hunger beforehand to sharpen it, no real satisfaction afterwards to close it. Just a full, slightly sick feeling and a need to keep moving. You push the wrappers aside and promise yourself a cleaner evening.

Three in the afternoon. The desk drawer opens. Everyone knows that drawer. Pens, receipts, a half-finished pack at the back. The sweet top-up. Ten minutes of buzz and chatter, legs jiggling, talk a little faster, then the fog rolls back in thicker. A second reach before five, smaller, more secret, eaten without looking up. You have lived that yo-yo so often you call it normal work tiredness.

Evening. The bus home, the shops glowing, the checkout ambush of bars and packs placed exactly where tired hands reach. You buy a little extra for the house, telling yourself it is for others. Dinner is proper and warm, but it is dulled because grazing filled the edges. You eat less with hunger and more out of habit, then you clear, then you sit.

Then the sofa. Then the television murmur. Then the quiet walk to the kitchen you know by heart. The permitted sliver that becomes the box. You finish feeling flat and vaguely ashamed, promising a cleaner tomorrow. You brush your teeth hard, as if brushing could wash away the grey, and you lie awake a little longer than you meant.

Now lay that Tuesday out in two columns, as you would a household account.

In the left column put what BAD SUGAR did TO you that day. Be honest and concrete. Money out at the till for packs that vanished without memory. Time lost in queues and vending corners and late-night cupboard stands and morning-after reviews. Teeth bathed in sweet acid again and again. Mood swung high then low then high then low. Hunger confused so you could not tell empty from urge. Satisfaction missed so no meal quite finished you. Sleep a little lighter. Morning a little duller. Patience a little thinner with the people you love.

In the right column put what it did FOR you. Not what it promised before the bite. What it delivered that stayed with you after the swallow ended.

Read your own right column back to me.

Where is the lasting energy you can point to at nine at night and say, that is still here because of the sweet dose at three. Where is the lasting calm you can point to after the box and say, I am calmer now than a clear person who never touched it. Where is the lasting joy that made Tuesday richer, kinder, brighter, that you still hold in your hands when the wrapper is in the bin.

You remember paying. You do not remember receiving.

We remember the wrapper. We remember the reach. We remember the brief flash. We do not remember a benefit that remained an hour later and changed the day for the better.

Ask yourself, quietly, without defending anything:

If a friend sold you a ticket every day and every day the show was cancelled, how long would you keep paying?

If a tool broke every shelf it touched, would you call it useful because it shone in the shop?

And if BAD SUGAR truly delivered a benefit, why do you need to keep re-dosing the same grey day just to feel normal again?

Do you see the empty column? That emptiness is not your failure to enjoy properly. It is the evidence.

A free pleasure leaves something behind. A meal eaten hungry leaves satisfaction. A walk with a friend leaves warmth in the legs and lightness in the chest. Music leaves a tune you hum later. What does Tuesday leave? Wrappers without memory, buzz without remainder, reward without rest.

That is not a moral judgment. It is bookkeeping.

**WHAT IT TAKES: TEETH, MONEY, TIME, MOOD**

Let us look steadily at the left column, not to frighten you, but to stop pretending it is blank.

Start with teeth, because teeth are plain and honest. The fact is, frequent free sugars feed the acid in plaque that rots teeth. That is the plain mechanism in your mouth, dose after dose, sip after sip. Brushing and fluoride and habits matter, of course they do, and I am no dentist to you. I state only the shape of it. Sweet grazing keeps the acid working through the day. Teeth pay, quietly, visit after visit, filling after filling. Look in the bathroom mirror after a grazing day and you know that furry, edgy feeling on the teeth. That is not imagination. That is the left column made touchable.

Then take the wider picture we all share. The fact is, our normal intake sits far above the guidance meant to protect us. The public guidance draws a modest daily ceiling for free sugars, with an even lower level where extra benefit lies, and most cupboards and drawers and lunch bags sail past both without noticing. I tell you this as background about us all, and I am not writing your future here. Feeling fine while we sail past does not mean our intake is low. It means harm can warm so slowly we never feel the water heating. A Tuesday never looks dramatic. A thousand Tuesdays do the taking.

Money. Add the till receipts for a month and do it without flinching. Sweet drinks, bars, biscuits, desserts, bakery stops, petrol-station extras, late-night extras, the family pack that never reaches the weekend. It is never one treat. It is a daily tax. Coin by coin it becomes hundreds over a year for something you cannot remember enjoying. Keep the receipts for a week if you doubt me. Watch how often your hand pays for a flash you forget by the next hour. You work hard for that money. Would you throw it into the street for nothing? You are throwing it into a wrapper for less than nothing, because the street would at least leave you clean.

Time. Count the minutes you will never get back. The queue. The machine. The search for something sweet at ten at night. The detour to the shop in the rain. The standing in the kitchen half-listening for footsteps. The grey morning-after review where you replay the evening and vow to do better. Hours of life spent serving a habit that calls itself a pleasure. A free pleasure does not eat your hours. It fits inside them. This eats the edges of every hour and leaves you hurrying.

Mood. Watch the swing you already named in your own true words, the short term lift then fast drop. Bright minute, flat hour. Resolve, reach, regret. The roller coaster you want to get off. I want to get off the roller coaster — that is not my phrase, it is yours, spoken by thousands of tired Tuesday people who feel the same swing. That swing is not life. That swing is dosing. Life has ups and downs, yes, but they have reasons: news, weather, people, rest. This swing has only one reason, repeated: dose, flash, dip, dose again.

I lay this out at full strength because soft lies would insult you. BAD SUGAR takes money, time, dental health, steady mood, clear hunger, calm evenings. It takes daily, in small bites that never look dramatic on their own. It takes while smiling.

Then in the same breath I tell you plainly: do not change because that list scares you.

Fear never freed anyone. Fear makes you white-knuckle for a week and then run back for comfort. I do not want you quitting from fear. I want you seeing so clearly that fear is not needed. The left column is heavy, yes. It is the right column that sets you free.

For the question remains untouched by all that cost. Even if teeth were iron and money grew on trees and time stood still, the question would stand exactly where it stands now.

What did it do FOR you?

If a thing took nothing, we might shrug and keep it for sentiment. But this takes plenty. And for all that taking, what have you kept? Name one kept good you can hold up at midnight and say, this stayed.

You cannot, because there is nothing to hold.

**A GENUINE TREAT OR FUEL**

This is where we must meet your strongest defence with respect, because you have carried it sincerely for years and because I carried it myself.

You say, it is a treat. It is fuel. It gets me through. It rewards a hard day. It makes the occasion. It comforts. It helps me concentrate.

I believed every word of that. We all did. We learned those words before we could spell them. Sweet as love from kindly hands. Sweet as prize for being good. Sweet as energy in a wrapper from bright packs. The culture taught us, family habit taught us, advertising taught us, and our own brief flash after a dose seemed to prove it. Brainwashed is not stupid. Brainwashed is conned, and conned people deserve truth.

Let us test those taught words against lived Tuesday rather than arguing them in the air.

Take fuel first, because fuel feels most urgent. You say the sweet top-up lifts you at three. I do not deny you feel something. Watch what lifting means in your own flesh across the afternoon, not just in the minute after swallowing. You feel low, you dose, you feel brighter for minutes, you fall lower, you dose again. Is that fuel? Fuel in a lamp burns steady and leaves light behind. Fuel from a proper meal eaten hungry leaves satisfaction behind that carries you for hours. This leaves a need for more fuel within the hour just to stand still.

A car that emptied its own tank to move ten metres would not be called powerful. It would be called broken. A lamp that needed relighting every ten minutes would not be called bright. It would be called faulty.

You never rise above how a clear person feels before the whole cycle starts. You sink below it, then climb briefly toward it, then sink again. We mistook the climb for a lift. Ask any clear afternoon you remember from before the grazing took hold — a day out, a walk, a morning after real rest — and you know what steady feels like. It does not buzz and crash. It carries.

Take treat next, because treat feels most tender. You say the evening box is a deserved reward. You say it in your own true dialect: celebrating, rewarding myself, going to the cinema, etc with chocolate. Treat when you feel you deserve one. I hear the love in those words. I am not mocking love.

Watch what treating means on that sofa. A true treat is chosen calmly, enjoyed fully, remembered gladly, finished with a satisfied sigh. You choose it with a clear yes, you taste every bite, you put it down glad, you do not think of it again with shame.

Is that what happens at ten at night, standing up, half-listening for footsteps, eating fast without plate or chair, finishing grey before the swallow ends? You told me yourself in the plainest private line trapped eaters speak: I feel like i cant stop once I start. Does that sound like a reward, or does that sound like answering?

A reward does not need hiding. A reward does not need a rule week to contain it. A reward does not leave you promising never to reward yourself again tomorrow. A reward does not whisper, I have managed to be good this week, as if goodness were the price of sweetness. A free treat proves freedom. This proves the opposite every time you touch it.

Take comfort next, because comfort feels most human. You say a hard day needs something soft and sweet. I understand that ache. We all come home scraped by the day and want kindness in a bowl. But watch Tuesday again. Did the bowl make the scrape smaller an hour later? Or did it add a second ache — the flat fog, the self-reproach, the resolve to start again — on top of the first? True comfort leaves you more able to face tomorrow. This leaves you less able, more tired, more secret. That is not comfort. That is interest charged on a loan you never needed.

So I ask you to hold the taught phrase up to the light and look through it.

We were told BAD SUGAR was a genuine treat or fuel.

Say it slowly. A genuine treat or fuel. That is the whole claim in one object. If it were true, Tuesday would show it. The benefit column would hold one solid gold coin that no cost could argue away. A genuine treat would treat. Genuine fuel would fuel. Instead Tuesday shows wrappers without memory, buzz without remainder, reward without rest, comfort without comfort.

What kind of treat leaves you flatter than before you took it?

What kind of fuel needs refuelling within the hour to stand still?

What kind of comfort leaves you more ashamed than soothed?

It does plenty TO you. It does nothing FOR you.

Read that again and let it stand as flat fact, not as slogan. It does plenty TO you. It does nothing FOR you. The left column is crowded. The right column is empty. Once seen, that cannot be unseen.

You have not failed to enjoy it properly. There was nothing proper to enjoy. The glow you remember was never in the dose. It was in the moment the dose borrowed — the break, the sit-down, the evening pause, the company, the rest you deserved anyway. The dose was only ever sneaking a ride on your own tired need for a break.

**THE HAND IN THE DITCH**

Here is a question I want you to carry, quietly, without answering too fast.

What if the hand that lifts you out of the low is the same hand that pushed you into it that morning?

You feel low at three. You dose. You feel rescued. You feel grateful to the rescuer. We have all felt that gratitude. I felt it daily. Warm rush, sigh, thank goodness for something sweet.

But who dug the low you were rescued from?

Follow the day backwards with your investigator's eye. The low at three follows the flash at one. The flash at one follows the low at eleven. The low at eleven follows the sweet start at eight. Each rescue plants the next need. Each thank-you pays for the next push.

We thank the dose for ending a discomfort the dosing keeps creating. We call that relief pleasure. We call that relief fuel. We call that relief treat.

Is it possible we have been grateful to our pusher?

You do not need to settle the whole mechanism now. Other clicks will make the how completely clear, down to the tiny grumble and the big belief that feeds it. For now notice only the shape: low, dose, brief ease, lower low. The rescuer and the perpetrator wear the same bright wrapper.

If that shape is true, then the benefit column was never merely empty by accident. It was empty by design. The thing cannot give, because its only gift is a pause in the taking.

Ask yourself once more, with the Tuesday audit warm in your mind:

When has a sweet dose ever left you better than a clear person who never dosed, for longer than minutes, in a way you could point to at night and say, there it is, still here?

When has one permitted sliver ever stayed one, without the twitch for more arriving behind it, without the evening whisper that says just one more?

And if there is truly nothing FOR you there, what exactly would you be losing by walking away from it for the rest of your life, compared with what you would keep paying for the rest of your life if you stay?

Feel the weight of those two futures. On one side, grey Tuesdays repeated for the rest of your life for something that never gave you anything — money out, time out, teeth aching, mood swinging, hunger confused, evenings spent answering. On the other side, mornings, meals, shops and evenings met clear and calm for the rest of your life because nothing was taken from you — hunger honest, satisfaction complete, money kept, time kept, mood your own.

Same years. Opposite meanings.

The difference is not fear. The difference is arithmetic. Empty benefit against certain cost. No pleasure to outweigh, because no pleasure was ever delivered. That is why the old courtroom kept you trapped. It asked how much harm you could bear for joy. The true court asks what joy ever arrived. And your own Tuesday answers, none that stayed.

Rejoice in that answer. An empty benefit column is marvellous news. It means there is nothing to miss, nothing to mourn, nothing to crave with fondness. It means the door out is not a door into loss. It is a door out of paying.

3. JUDGE ONLY BY WHAT IT DOES FOR YOU
Forget harm versus benefit and ask for the benefit.

**SUMMARY**
- The harm debate grants a pleasure that was never proven, so the true test is the benefit alone.
- A plain Tuesday shows crowded cost in money, time, teeth and mood and no lasting gain that remains after the flash ends.
- Frequent sweet doses keep mouth acid working against teeth, while ordinary intake sails far past public guidance without feeling high, and feeling normal proves nothing about level.
- The taught words treat and fuel fail against lived afternoons and evenings, where dosing brings speed, secrecy and repeat rather than calm choice and remainder.
- The brief ease after a dose pauses a low the dosing cycle helped create, so gratitude points to the wrong source.
- Seeing an empty benefit column turns stopping from loss into escape, since nothing real is left to miss.
