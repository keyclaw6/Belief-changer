Chapter 5
PARTIES, PAUSES AND MILDS

*The party was the work done, the pause was the standing still — the stick only stood beside them and took the bow.*

### THE DISH BESIDE THE WORK

You have watched the desk stay exactly where it was while the tug went quiet. Keep that eye. We turn it now where the claim feels kindest, not hardest.

Rewards.

Say it in your own mouth, the way we say it when no one is arguing.

"Cigarettes were my reward for…well, almost everything. Teaching a class. Finishing a story. Finishing a paragraph. Driving 500 miles. Driving to the grocery store."

I thank you for that honesty. That sentence was mine. I paid myself in lights for every finished thing. A class taught, step into the corridor, light. A paragraph wrestled straight, lean back, light. A long drive done, the bags carried in, stand by the back door, light. The day became a string with a knot tied after each bead.

We called those knots rewards. Look at where the joy lived in them.

Take the class. You walk in with notes gone soft at the edges. Forty faces turn. You find the thread, you lose it, you find it again. A boy at the back who never speaks asks a sharp question and the room wakes. You answer from somewhere you did not know you knew. The bell goes. Chairs scrape. Paper shuffles. Your shoulders drop because a hard thing was done well.

Where in that hour was the tube. It was not in the room. It waited in the pocket. It added no word, no timing, no patience, no wit. The teaching was yours. The sweat under the collar was yours. The lift when the quiet boy nodded was yours.

Then in the corridor you lit and the lift seemed to come again, smaller, smokier, and we pinned the first lift to the second. That pinning was the whole con.

Take the paragraph. You stare at a sentence that will not stand. You cut three words. You read it aloud under your breath. You change a verb. Suddenly it stands. You sit back and feel that small clean click a made thing gives. Hands warm. Head clear for a second.

Did the click come from paper and fire. Did ash choose the verb. You know it did not. The click came from work meeting judgment. The dose arrived after, the way a man walks into a room after the song is sung and claims he sang it.

Take the grocery run. The list, the queue, the car park rain, the bags heavy on the wrist, the milk put away, the door shut. Done. A completed errand gives its own small steadiness. We all feel it, smoker or not. Then we lit on the step and let the smoke sign its name across a deed it never did.

Ask from your own day, not from me.

Did the class go better because ash fell afterward. Did the paragraph stand straighter because a filter was wet afterward. Did the bags carry themselves because a match was struck afterward. If the pellet truly made the work sweet, why did work you loved before you ever smoked already taste sweet, and why does the same work still click for men who never lit.

Answer plainly. You know.

We ran the same trick on driving, on stories finished, on miles covered. The pattern was always identical: do a thing, feel the lift of doing, light, feel the muting of the underneath gnaw, tie the two feelings with one knot and call the knot a reward.

It was never a reward. It was a photograph taken after the prize was given.

I lived that knot for years and I am not mocking your feeling. The lift after finishing is real. The click is real. The pride when the boy nods is real. I am warm to you for wanting to mark those minutes. I am vicious only to the thief that stood beside the marking and slipped its name onto your work.

Once you see the order, you cannot unsee it. The work pleases. The tube follows. The credit was stolen backward.

### WHAT THE PAUSE REALLY PAID

Closely tied to the pellet is the pause.

"I need it to stop for a minute. To step out. To draw breath."

I understand that want. The morning crowds, the screen crowds, the kitchen clangs — a man wants a door he can close for five minutes.

Watch what closed the door.

You stand. You leave the noise. Cold touches the cheeks. No voice wants anything. You look at sky or brick for a while. Shoulders come down a notch. Breath slows because no one is speaking to you.

Then the dose burns through the middle of that standing and claims the slowing.

Reverse the picture and it falls apart.

A non-smoker takes the same step. He carries no tube. He stands. He looks at the same sky. His shoulders do what yours do. His breath slows the same fraction. No one tells him he paused wrong for lack of smoke.

So what did smoke add to stopping.

Nothing but the lighting and the sucking and the papery mouth and the clock cut shorter because the tube, not you, decided when the pause ended.

Think of a dog given a pellet every time it sits. Soon it sits for the pellet, and we say the pellet taught the sitting. But a child sits on a step to watch rain without any pellet. The sitting was already good. We were the dog and the child at once — we sat because standing is good, then taught ourselves to sit only with a pellet, then praised the pellet for the sitting.

The pellet never taught the pause. It taxed it.

It taxed it in money, in breath, in standing apart with a hand that must hold paper and fire instead of a cup, a rail, empty air. It taxed it by cutting the pause to the length of a burn, not the length you needed. It taxed it by making you watch the packet level instead of the sky.

And mark this well. The pause gives the chest a minute of quiet that belongs to standing, not to smoke. That sentence will bear checking every time you step out today. What steadied — the stopping or the puffing. Your own shoulders will answer before the ash falls.

### BUT I LIKE THE TASTE

Now the objection we hold closest, spoken low because we half know its answer.

"But I like it. The first draw. The taste. The scratch at the back of the throat. You cannot tell me I do not like what I like."

I will not tell you that. I will ask you to look at what liking meant.

When did you first like it. Do you remember that first tube behind the sheds, in a car park, on a balcony while others watched. Coughing. Eyes watering. Throat burning. Nose wrinkled. Did you say, ah, delicious. Or did you say, I will get used to this, because the others seemed easy with it and you did not want to be the only one outside the circle.

We did not like the taste. We learned to call the signal of the dose by the name of taste.

Watch a man who never smoked taste your exhale by accident on a stair. He does not close his eyes and smile. He turns his face. Watch a child walk through a doorway where you just stood. He waves the air. Their noses report what ours agreed not to report.

And watch ourselves. We cover that taste all day. Mints. Gum. Coffee strong enough to drown it. Spray in the car. Wash of hands. If the taste were the pleasure, why do we spend half the day burying it. Why is the first kiss after a dose an apology. Why is the morning mouth the first thing we rinse.

You will say, but the lighting moment is heaven. I know that line from the inside.

"the moment I light up I'm in heaven, but feel shitty afterwards. So whilst I love smoking I hate being a smoker"

I do not mock that heaven. It arrives. It lasts seconds. Name it truly and it stops owning you.

That heaven is not flavour. It is the underneath gnaw going mute within seconds of the smoked hit. After an hour unfed, the tug is awake and unnamed. The first draw tops it. The muting floods through and we call the flood taste, pleasure, heaven. Then the top-up thins, the mouth turns papery, the throat turns stale, and the shitty afterwards you named so honestly arrives on time, every time.

If it were taste, it would not behave like that. Real tastes do not punish you an hour later for tasting them. Bread does not make bread more needed by being eaten. An apple does not leave a tug that calls for another apple in forty minutes. Only a fading dose behaves that way — fast up, fast down, hand back to the pocket.

Ask the questions whose only answer ends the taste story.

Does any food you love require you to suffer the first six tries before you can bear it. Do you need mints to hide the taste of anything else you claim to love. If the flavour were the gift, why does the gift need coffee to drown it and a window to air it.

You know. The liking was the muting misnamed. The heaven was the itch going quiet for minutes. The shitty afterwards was the price collected at once, not later.

Keep the warmth for yourself here. There is nothing wrong with your tongue. Your tongue was reporting truly all along, under the story. When the story thins, the tongue is free to report again — food tastes of food, air tastes of air, morning tastes of nothing but clean beginning.

It does plenty TO you. It does nothing FOR you.

Read that while a dose burns. The first sentence points to mouth, throat, chest, pocket, curtain, finger stain. The second points to class, paragraph, pause, taste — all claimed, none given.

### THE RING OF TINY HOLES

With reward and taste thinned, the last kind exception arrives in white and gold.

Lights. Milds. Lows. The careful pack that seems to admit the danger and soften it.

"I know the others are bad. I smoke the light ones. Less tar. Less nicotine. At least I cut down the harm."

I understand why that feels responsible. It was designed to feel responsible. Look at the design, not the promise.

Hold a mild by the filter to the light. Around the paper, in one or two neat rings, sit rows of tiny vent holes. In a lab machine they work exactly as drawn. The machine draws a fixed puff, clean air slips in through the holes, the smoke is diluted, the needle reads lower.

Now put lips and fingers where the machine never put them.

Lips close over the ring. Fingers close over the ring. The draw pulls harder because diluted smoke satisfies the tug less. The holes meant for air are covered by flesh. The harder draw pulls deeper. The reading taken by steel and rubber never happened in a human mouth.

Think of a mask issued for bad air, with small holes punched round the edge to let clean air in. Think of a man who, without knowing, tapes those holes shut with his own mouth and fingers every time he puts it on, then breathes harder to get through. Would you say he breathed cleaner because the holes were punched. Or would you say the holes were theatre the moment flesh covered them.

That is what the mild is. Theatre that vanishes on contact.

I put this flat, without scare and without lecture. Machines measured lower. Human smoking did not take lower in the way the numbers suggest, because covering and harder puffing answer the dilution as the tug demands. Not to the puff the same for every smoker — hands differ, draws differ, days differ — but the shape holds: lower on paper does not mean lower in the chest in the way the pack implies.

This is no call to change brand or seek a stronger tube. Stronger or lighter, the tube remains a day's supply box for the same want. The point is never which paper burns. The point is that mildness was sold as a gift while the body answered it with harder work.

Ask from your own hand.

When you switched to lights, did the day's number fall and stay fallen. Or did the draws grow longer, the stubs grow shorter, the packet empty at the same hour. Did mild taste mild because harm fell, or because air holes thinned the scratch while the tug called for the same topping.

You know from the ashtray, not from me.

And see who benefits from keeping the mild story alive. While it lives, a smoker can keep the pellet, keep the pause, keep the taste story a little longer, and feel careful while staying chained. Fear of harm is met not with escape from the con but with a whiter pack. The seller keeps the customer. The customer keeps the tug. Both call it caution.

I am harsh to that seller, never to you. You sought a smaller harm because you are a careful person. Your care was real. It was spent on theatre built to catch careful people. Seeing that hole is part of your escape from the con.

Once seen, the mild cannot be unseen. A light will often mean a heavier draw through holed paper, a mild often a harder puff for the same fading top — not to the puff the same for every smoker, for hands differ, draws differ, days differ. The softness on the throat was dilution, not kindness. The kindness never existed.

### WHAT REMAINS WHEN CREDIT IS TAKEN BACK

Stand these three together and the day looks different.

The pellet after work, the pause on the step, the mild in the fingers — three gifts examined, three credits moved back where they belong.

The class taught itself through you. The paragraph stood through you. The bags were carried through you. The standing steadied through stopping and cold air and a minute unclaimed. The mild softened nothing but the scratch that warned you truly.

So what did the tube ever add.

It added the lighting, the sucking, the papery mouth, the smell on the coat, the watching of levels, the forty-minute return. It added plenty to carry and nothing to keep.

Stay with one of those hours and walk it through to the end, pellet left out of the picture, so you can feel what the interval itself lost. Not the minute of lighting, but the sixty minutes after. You have lived this whole hour already on days when lighting was impossible, and your own memory will do the proving.

Take the corridor after the bell. Ten-four. Chairs scrape next door. Your room empties in a wash of sleeves and straps. The board still holds the last line you wrote. Chalk dust hangs. Your throat is dry from talking. The quiet boy lingers half a second at the door and nods before he goes. That nod is still warm in the chest.

Before, the next moves came fast and without thought. Bag shut. Door locked. Pocket checked with two fingers for thickness. Corridor, stairs, door to the yard, lighter cupped against wind, first draw while still walking. Eight minutes gone to standing and burning. Stub pressed out against the wall. Hands waved, mouth breathed out hard. Back up the stairs with three minutes to spare before the next bell. That was called a break.

Now leave that sequence out and watch the same sixty minutes run without a cut. I am not asking you to prove anything or to wrestle anything. Simply remember an inspection Tuesday, a parents' evening, a double period that ran straight through, when the yard was out of reach and the hour had to run whole. You know this hour. You have lived it.

Ten-six. You do not go to the yard. You stand at the window in your own room and push it wide. Cold comes in over the sill. You stack the exercise books into two piles, marked and unmarked. You wipe the board clean with three long strokes. The dust falls. The quiet boy's question is still in the head, and you write his name in the margin of the plan book so you can bring him in again tomorrow. No fingers tap the pocket. No eyes drop to count what is left. No small arithmetic runs underneath — will it last till lunch, shall I borrow, is there time to buy. That arithmetic used to run under every corridor minute like a radio left on. Today the radio is off and the corridor sounds different without it.

Ten-fourteen. The kettle in the staff room. You pour, you wait while it cools, you drink it standing by the sink while Mrs Allen tells the story of her bus breaking down. You laugh properly because the head is not already half outside. With the pellet running the hour you would have taken two sips, checked the clock, left the mug to skin over, gone down for the burn, come back to cold tea and drunk it too fast to taste. The tea did not change. The cutting changed. A hot drink drunk hot is a small thing, but small things stacked across twenty years are a life.

Ten-twenty-two. Back at the desk. You open three books from the top pile. Red pen. The first essay is muddled in the middle and you find the sentence where it slipped. You write in the margin, say this straight, and you feel that click again, the teacher's click of putting a finger on the fault. The second is good and you write well argued across the top with a flourish you enjoy. The third has the quiet boy's name on it, and his last paragraph holds the same sharpness as his question. You sit back for a second. No ash to tap. No stub to press. No papery turn in the mouth pulling attention sideways. The attention stays where you put it.

Notice what is not happening in this hour. No packet-level watch. In the old pattern the eyes dropped twenty times an hour to pocket, to bag, to desk drawer, to count by feel. Is it full, is it low, is it enough. That watching stole glances from books, from faces, from windows. Now the glances go where you send them. They go to the boy's paragraph. They go to the rain starting on the glass. They go nowhere at all for a minute while you think.

No stub and draw cycle. When the burn owned the pause every pause had a fixed shape — light, eight draws, shorten, press out, wave hands — and the shape decided the length. You did not pause till you were ready to return. You paused till the paper burned down. If the thought needed another two minutes, it did not get them. If the tea needed another minute to cool, it did not get it. The burn owned the clock. Now the clock is owned by need. You stand at the window for ninety seconds because ninety seconds is enough. You sit with the books for eighteen minutes because eighteen minutes is what marking three books takes. You chat for six minutes because the story is funny and the next class is not yet calling. Length follows life again, not paper.

No forty-minute call cutting the next work. With the cycle in charge the tug woke on schedule and cut across whatever was underway. Midway through explaining the next lesson plan to yourself, the gnaw stirred and the hand began its restlessness — pen down, chair back, glance at clock, calculation of minutes to the bell. The thought you were holding thinned while the tug thickened. You went down to the yard not because the work was done but because the interval was up. The work was left half-held and had to be picked up cold afterward. Now the next work is entered whole. You carry the plan for period three into period three without a gap. You walk in with the boy's name still fresh, the board wiped, the throat rested, the head uncut.

Walk it further. Ten-thirty-five. Corridor fills again. Doors bang. You pick up the new set of books and walk to the next room. Your coat does not smell of the yard. Your fingers do not carry the yellow hint you scrubbed every night. You do not stop at the washroom to rinse the mouth and spray. Those five minutes used to be tax paid on every pellet, there and back, rinse and hide, paid without ever being entered in the books. Today those minutes stay in the hour. You spend them leaning on the doorframe, watching the rain, letting the last class settle before the next begins.

Ten-forty-six. The next class starts. You write the date on the board. Your voice comes back easily. The throat is not catching on the first sentence. The head does not drift to the pocket during register. When a girl asks a dull question that needs a patient answer, patience is there because it was not spent coaxing a lighter in wind and counting draws. Small patience, but teaching is made of small patience stacked.

That is the interval theft, lived end to end. The pellet did not only sign its name across finished work. It chopped the time between finished things into short lengths that could never run whole. Forty minutes of building want, eight minutes of burning, five minutes of rinsing and hiding, then forty minutes again. The day looked like beads on a string, but the string itself was eaten between the beads. An hour that could hold marking, tea drunk hot, a laugh, a note in a plan book, a minute at a window, was instead made to hold watching, burning, rinsing, counting, and a cold return to work half-held.

Ask from that remembered Tuesday, not from theory. When lighting was impossible, did the work collapse. Did the corridor go flat because no pellet marked it. Or did the hour run longer, calmer, more continuous than usual, with attention staying where you put it and pauses lasting as long as they needed. You know, because you lived it. Inspection days were tiring for their own reasons, but they were never tiring from lack of pellets. They were often clearer than ordinary days, and you marked that clarity at the time without understanding it.

I lived years of chopped hours and called the chopping normal. I thought the restlessness between pellets was my nature — that I was a man who could not sit long, who needed air, who worked in bursts. I was not describing my nature. I was describing the forty-minute return. Show me a man fed a fading top-up every hour and I will show you a man who cannot sit long. Show me the same man with the cycle out of the picture and I will show you a man who marks three books straight, drinks tea hot, remembers a boy's name, and still has minutes to spare at the window.

See how the tax ran both ways. At the moment of reward, credit was stolen forward — work pleased, tube took the bow. Between rewards, time was stolen sideways — the hour was cut to burn-length, attention was hired out to packet-watching, pauses were shortened to stub-length, next work was entered part-empty. Two thefts, one pellet. Once both are seen, the day cannot look the same.

Speak your last defence aloud before I answer it, because I want to hear you, not a straw man.

"Even if all that is true, my little parties get me through. Take them and the day goes flat."

Flat compared to what. Compared to a day where every finished thing is taxed by a fading dose and every pause is cut to burn-length. Compared to a day where taste is buried under mints and mornings begin with coughing. Was that day bright. Or was it a dim day with brief flares we called bright because the dim between them was itself caused by the flares fading.

Watch men who never smoked finish the same things. Do their days look flat. The teacher walks the corridor grinning without lighting. The writer leans back from the paragraph and breathes without lighting. The driver shuts the boot and stretches without lighting. Their lifts do not need topping within the hour because no top-up thinned. Their pauses last as long as they need because no paper decides the length.

You are not less capable of lift than they are. We simply learned to rent our own lifts back from a lender, pellet by pellet.

There is a small hard truth inside this sweet section and I will put it plain because you can take it. Every pellet taught the brain one lesson: nothing is finished until smoke signs it. Taught long enough, unfinished minutes feel unfinished without the signature. That feeling is not proof the signature helped. It is proof the lesson stuck. Lessons can be unstuck by seeing.

And seeing is already happening. You came to this chapter guarding those pellets. You leave it with work still joyful, pauses still whole, taste still yours — only the signature shown as forgery. Nothing real was taken from your day. Only the tax collector was named.

5. IGNORE ANY ADVICE THAT CONFLICTS WITH THIS BOOK
Let this argument finish before you borrow another.

**SUMMARY**
- The lift after teaching, writing, driving and errands belonged to finished work, not to the light that followed it.
- The pause steadied because of standing, cold air and a minute unclaimed, not because of the puff in the middle of it.
- Liking was the fast muting of the underneath gnaw misnamed as taste, which is why mints and open windows follow it all day.
- Vent holes read lower on a lab machine but lips and fingers cover them and draws pull harder, so mild on paper did not mean mild in the way the pack implies.
- No brand change fixes that theatre; the point is the want the tube serves, not the paper it wears.
- The hour between lights was also cut to burn-length by watching, burning and rinsing, and runs whole when nothing cuts it.
- Work, pause and taste stay whole when credit is moved back where it belongs.
