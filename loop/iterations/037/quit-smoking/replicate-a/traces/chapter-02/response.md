Chapter 2
ARE YOU REALLY CHOOSING THIS

*You never chose to need cigarettes; you were tricked into needing them, and that need now chooses for you.*

### You Say You Choose It

"I like smoking. Nobody forces me. I could stop if I wanted to."

I hear you. I said the same for years, and I meant it. We all say it. It sounds like common sense. No one holds a cigarette to your head. You buy the pack. You take out the stick. You light it. Whose hand is that if not yours?

Let me ask you plainly, as one smoker to another, and answer honestly in your own head.

If you freely choose it, why do you need this book in your hand to go on choosing it? If you like it, why do you do it in hiding? If no one forces you, why does your hand move before you have decided?

Think of any small pleasure you truly choose. A second piece of cake because you feel like it. A longer walk because the evening is fine. Do you hide that from your partner? Do you stand in the cold to do it? Do you spray the curtains after, chew mints, wave your shirt in the doorway, and hope the kids will not smell it on you? Choice does not behave like that.

We tell ourselves we choose because the alternative frightens us. If we choose, we are in charge. If we are in charge, there is nothing to face. The fact is, we were conned out of charge a long time ago.

Did you choose the first ones? Coughing, dizzy, sick on the back step, pushing through nausea because the older lads did it and you wanted to look grown. No one chooses that for pleasure. We persisted because we believed there was something on the other side once we learned. That belief was fed to us by friends, by films, by adults stepping out for a moment that looked like rest. We breathed that air. It was not foolishness. It was phoney information.

And once we learned to inhale without choking, something else took over. We stopped choosing to smoke and started needing to smoke in order to feel normal. The hand learned the path to the pocket. The mind learned the story to cover it. That story is what I mean when I speak of the nicotine trap.

The nicotine trap is not a phrase to frighten you. It is a plain description of what happened. You walked in on false promises, the door closed quietly behind you, and now you call the inside your choice.

### The Garage Door At Midnight

Let me stand with you where choice would never need to go.

It is near midnight. The house is quiet. Your partner is asleep. The kids are asleep. You tell yourself you want some air. You slip to the garage, half-close the door against the cold, and light quickly, cupping the glow as if it might be seen.

You do not savour it. You hurry it. You listen for a footstep on the stairs. You blow toward the crack in the door. When it is done you grind it hard under your shoe, tuck the end deep in an old tin so no smell leaks, spray hard around your jacket, work a mint, wash your hands, wave your shirt cold at the door. You Febreeze the hell out of yourself and walk back in holding your breath a little, hoping.

Warm reader, listen to me. I am not scolding you. I have done every inch of that dance. So have thousands of decent, honest smokers I have sat with — teachers and drivers and nurses and fathers and grandmothers. There is nothing wrong with you. There is everything wrong with the need that makes a grown adult hide in his own garage like a schoolboy.

Ask yourself while you stand there with the spray still in the air.

Would a free pleasure make you lie to people you love? Would enjoyment need mints and excuses? Would choice need a half-closed door?

I know a man who kept a separate coat for smoking, hung in the garage, never brought inside. I know a woman who brushed her teeth three times before bed, not for hygiene but for concealment, then lay awake listening to her own chest. I know closet smokers who smoke only at work, only in the car, only when no one is looking, and tell everyone including themselves that they have it under control.

Control does not hide. Hiding confesses the trap.

Notice the divided self in that moment. One part of you lights. The other part cleans up after the lighting as if after someone else. You love the lighting minute and you hate being the person who needs it. Both feelings are honest. The split is not proof you are weak. It is proof you are caught — one voice speaking for the need, one voice speaking for you.

A free man does not need to deceive his own household to enjoy himself for five minutes. A chained man does.

### The Hand That Moves First

Watch your own day while you read, with the pack beside you as it always is. Do not change anything. Only watch.

The phone goes down after a sharp exchange. The meal ends. The car door shuts. The kettle clicks. Before thought has finished, the hand is already moving — pocket, pack, tap, light. You notice you are smoking after you are smoking.

That is not decision. That is a pull acting faster than decision.

We flatter ourselves that we weigh and choose each dose. We do not. We light to quiet a restlessness and call the quiet enjoyment. We mistake the speed of the reach for desire, and desire for choice.

If this were choice, you could take it or leave it with equal ease. Try the plain test in your mind, without doing anything yet. Picture the morning you cannot find the pack. Feel that small flare in the stomach. Is that the calm of a man missing a pleasure, or the edge of a man missing a fix? A pleasure missed leaves you a little disappointed. A fix missed makes you search drawers, borrow from a neighbour, step out in rain in slippers. Which man have you been?

You do not search drawers for something you merely like. You search because something in the body calls and the mind obeys before pride can object.

I am not asking you to fight that hand. Fighting was never the way out and I will never ask it of you. I am asking you to see it clearly for what it is. A choosing hand pauses. A hooked hand reaches.

When did your hand learn to move before you? Not when you were born. Not when you were a child breathing clear. It learned after the first hundred doses taught it the circuit. That learning was not your fault. But it is not your freedom either.

Free choice does not hurry. Hiding hurries. Reaching hurries.

### Heaven For A Second, Then Shame

Now let us be fair to your own experience, because I will never lie to you about what you feel.

There is a minute, the lighting minute, that feels like heaven. The match flares, the first drag bites, the shoulders drop a fraction. For a few seconds something in you says, ah, that is better. Then the stick burns down, the taste turns stale, the aftertaste sits sour, and you feel shitty afterwards. You love smoking and you hate being a smoker in the same hour.

I believe both halves. I am not here to mock what you feel or to tell you that you never enjoyed anything. That heaven second is real as a feeling. It arrives fast and it fades fast. It does not last the day. It does not even last the cigarette.

Notice what that tells you about choice.

A genuine pleasure leaves you glad you did it. Food you love leaves you satisfied. Company you love leaves you warm. This leaves you washing, spraying, chewing, regretting, and planning the next one. Why would a pleasure need to be cleaned off you?

And notice this too. If anything stressful happens in my life, a cigarette takes care of it — that is what you feel in the moment, a rapid taken-care-of feeling that you credit to the dose. I do not deny the feeling. You feel what you feel. Feeling it does not prove the dose fixed anything. It proves only how fast the credit is given, while the phone, the bill, the argument sit exactly where they were.

We do not need to settle yet what that heaven second is or why it fades. We will look at each credit in its turn, with your own day as evidence. For now hold only the split that choice cannot explain. Choice would not call heaven what leaves shame. Choice would not pay for a second of bright with an hour of stale.

You are not lying when you say the lighting feels good. You are misreading what that good proves. A caged bird that paces and then sits for a second has not chosen the cage because sitting felt better than pacing.

### Two Things Hold You, Not One

So what holds you, if not choice and not pleasure?

Only two things, and both are smaller than your fear makes them. I name them here in passing so we have words for what we see. They will be our vocabulary from here on, not a lesson to learn.

The first is tiny and physical. A little emptiness between doses, hardly pain at all. Not flu. Not agony. It is an empty, slightly restless, slightly edgy little tug that comes when the last dose fades and nags to be fed. Feed it and it quiets for minutes. Leave it and it nags a while, then passes on its own like a distant horn. I mention it only as the Nipper, a small starving creature you carry without knowing, already dying whenever you stop feeding it.

The second is larger and louder, and it is the real holder. The voices, pictures and excuses that interpret that little tug as proof you need a cigarette to cope, to pause, to belong. Everyone does it. One won't hurt. My work needs it. My nerves need it. That noise was installed early and topped up daily by the world around you. I mention it only as the Smokescreen, the belief-system that feeds the Nipper by telling you the dose gives what it only seems to give.

That is the whole shape of the nicotine trap as you live it. A small tug plus a loud story. The tug moves the hand. The story calls the moving choice.

See how this reframes your past. You did not fail before because you lack muscle. You failed because you fought the tug while believing the story. No one told you the story was the holder. Once the story is seen, the tug is revealed as trivial — a passing edginess, not a command.

You do not need to fight either of them now. You need only to keep watching with open eyes while you go on smoking as normal. Seeing is how you walk out through a door that was never locked, and that walking out is an escape into clear seeing, not a loss of anything real.

Free choice does not need a story to excuse it. The trap does.

### Tomorrow With Your Eyes Open

So let us make that watching concrete. Not as a theory. As tomorrow.

You will wake tomorrow and smoke as normal. I want you to. No waiting, no counting, no testing yourself. Keep your pattern untouched. Light when you would light. Put out when you would put out. The only difference is this: you will have names for what you see, and you will watch without getting into a wrestle with it.

Start with the kettle. Your feet find the floor. Before you are properly awake the hand is out. Do not stop it. Do not scold it. Watch it. Say to yourself, there goes the hand, there is the tug moving it, there is the story calling it choice. Notice how little pause there is between waking and reaching. A man choosing a pleasure lingers, decides, looks forward. A man answering a tug reaches. Which did you just do?

Stand at the kitchen window with it. Taste the first drag and then, deliberately, taste the middle and the end. The beginning is bright. The middle is papery. The end is sour. Notice how your attention slides off after the first two pulls. You are no longer smoking it so much as finishing it while your mind is already elsewhere — on keys, on traffic, on the day. Ask yourself, if this were enjoyment, would enjoyment go absent for three-quarters of the act? Would a pleasure need to be finished rather than savoured?

Then go about your morning. Mid-morning comes. The phone rings, the inbox fills, someone says something sharp. The hand moves again. Again, do not stop it. Watch the order of events. First the tug stirs, faintly, underneath. Then the story speaks over it, loudly, with your own voice: I need a break, I need air, I deserve five minutes. Then the feet move. Three events, that fast, every time. Once you have the words — the Nipper tugging, the Smokescreen talking — you will not be able to unsee the sequence. It repeats too cleanly to be choice. Choice is varied. This is identical.

At lunch you step out with the others. Watch the social part carefully, because this is where the story is cleverest. Someone laughs, someone offers, the circle tightens for a few minutes. You feel included. Ask yourself what included you. Was it the lit tube between your fingers, or the laugh, the pause from desks, the cold air on your face, the five minutes when no one wants anything from you? Notice that non-smokers stand in that same circle and feel the same ease without a dose. Their ease does not come from smoke. Why would yours? Let that question sit. Do not force an answer. Just let your own eyes report that the company did the work while the dose took the credit.

In the afternoon the car becomes a chapel. Door shut, engine on, window cracked an inch, light. Watch how automatic that chain has become. Door, engine, window, light — one movement in four parts. You did not decide it this afternoon. You decided it years ago and have been replaying it since. That is not a criticism. We all run such chains. The point is only to see that a replay is not a decision. A decision happens now. A replay happened long ago and keeps happening because the tug keeps calling and the story keeps covering.

Come evening, the hiding returns, and I want you to watch it with particular kindness. The back door, the garage, the spray, the mint. You know the dance. Tomorrow night, do the dance exactly as usual, but narrate it inwardly with the new words. Here is the tug asking. Here is the story saying, just slip out, no one will know. Here is the hand obeying. Here is the other part of me laying out the spray and the mint like an accomplice cleaning a scene. Two selves, one body, one small tug and one loud story between them.

Do you feel how different that watching is from fighting? Fighting would say, do not go, hold out, prove you are strong. Watching says, go if the pattern says go, but go with eyes open. Fighting tightens everything and makes the dose feel precious. Watching loosens the knot because you see the strings.

And as you watch, a second consequence arrives quietly, without fanfare. You begin to feel the difference between being weak and being conned.

A weak man cannot do hard things. Look at your life. You get up when tired. You pay bills. You look after others when you have nothing left. You endure boredom, pressure, disappointment, and you keep going. Smokers are among the most stubborn, persistent people I know — it takes a stubborn will to keep inhaling smoke after the body coughs and the throat burns and the clothes stink and the price climbs and still step out in rain to do it again. That is not weakness. That is persistence pointed in the wrong direction by false information.

Being conned is different. A conned man can be strong and still be caught, because the catch happened before he had the facts. You were caught as a youngster when you could not judge the offer. You stayed caught because every dose seemed to prove the story true — the tug eased for a minute, so the story said, you see, it helps. Of course you believed it. We all did. We mistook a quieted tug for a genuine help, and we called the quiet choice.

Can you feel the relief in that distinction? If you were weak, tomorrow would be another test you might fail. If you were conned, tomorrow is not a test at all. It is a study day. You are not on trial. You are gathering plain sight. The spray can on the shelf is not evidence against your character. It is evidence of the need. The hand moving first is not evidence you lack control. It is evidence control was removed by a circuit you did not design.

I want you to let that land in the body, not just the head. Stand tomorrow where you always stand, smoke what you always smoke, and feel the small shift from shame to clarity. Shame says, look what I do, what is wrong with me. Clarity says, look what the tug plus the story do through me, and I am now seeing it. Same garage, same night, same mint. Different eyes. The act is identical. The meaning has changed. And meaning, not muscle, is what frees.

You will also notice, if you watch kindly, how often the story borrows your virtues to keep you. It says you are considerate, so slip out and do not bother anyone. It says you are responsible, so take a break and you will work better after. It says you are sociable, so join the circle. The story flatters while it chains. A free choice does not need flattery. A con does.

So do not argue with the story yet. We will take each of its claims apart in its proper place, with your own observations as proof. For now, label it when you hear it. Ah, there is the Smokescreen talking. Ah, there is the Nipper tugging underneath. Label and light. Label and finish. Label and spray and wash and come back inside. No wrestle. No triumph. No failure. Only labelling, while smoking as normal.

What does that deliver by the end of one ordinary day? Not a triumph you can boast about. Something better. A quiet sense that the divided self is not you falling apart, but a trap operating in plain view. You light and you clean up after lighting, and you see both hands belong to one circuit. You reach before thought and you see reaching is not deciding. You feel the bright second fade to stale and you see bright-to-stale is not pleasure but a minute that cannot last. None of this asks you to change what you do. It changes what you know while you do it.

And knowing, kept up day after day while you smoke as normal, does what effort never could. It thins the story. Each labelled moment takes a little credit back from the dose and returns it to its true owner — the pause, the laugh, the air, the momentary quiet of the tug itself. You do not have to believe me about any of that yet. You only have to keep watching until your own tally grows too tall to ignore.

You entered this chapter defending choice. You will leave it with words for what removed choice. That is enough for now. We have not killed every excuse, and we will not try to. We have put the trap in view, named its two holders in passing, and learned how to watch them work without raising a hand against them. The rest of our work stands on that ground.

Keep your pack beside you. Keep your pattern as it is. Keep your eyes open a fraction wider than yesterday. You were never weak. You were never free while believing you chose. You were conned, faithfully, daily, by a small tug and a loud story — and a conned man who starts to see the con has already begun to walk out.

2. DON'T STOP OR CUT DOWN YET
Smoke as normal until your last cigarette.

**SUMMARY**
- Smoking stopped being a free choice when false promises closed behind you.
- Hiding, spraying and hurrying show a need is acting, not a pleasure being chosen.
- Your hand often lights before you decide, which choice would never need to do.
- The bright minute of lighting is real as a moment, but it fades fast and leaves shame and a stale mouth, not day-long good.
- A tiny physical tug and a loud learned story together keep the lighting going, not lack of strength or sense.
- Watching tomorrow while smoking as normal lets you see the divided self operate without fighting it.
- Being conned is different from being weak, and seeing the con is the start of walking out.
