Chapter 11
THERE IS NO JUST ONE

*Cutting down, keeping one for later and waiting till tomorrow all mean the same thing: staying inside.*

### THE BARGAIN AFTER THE BEST ONE

You have met the best one head-on and watched it give nothing.

That late patio with drinks and laughter did the giving. The paper only hired your hands while the giving happened elsewhere. You tasted the same hour with and without, and the hour stood taller without.

So why does a new voice start up, quieter, almost reasonable?

“All right. The night-out dose is empty. But surely I can keep just one for Christmas? Surely I can cut to three a day till work calms down? Surely tomorrow is a cleaner day to finish?”

I know that voice. We all know it. We stood over sinks and swore the next pack would be the last controlled one. We counted, we delayed, we saved special ones in tins as if a dose in a tin were not a dose.

Hear it for what it is. It is not you thinking. That bargaining is the nicotine trap talking, not you.

It has to bargain now because it cannot defend the best dose any longer. It lost the patio. It lost the morning and the meal and the break. So it moves to smaller ground. Not “smoking gives me pleasure” any more. Now “just a little smoking will not hurt.” Smaller claim, same owner.

Ask from behaviour, not from hope.

When you ever cut down, did you smoke less and think less about smoking — or smoke less and think about smoking all day? When you saved one for a special night, did the night stay special — or did the whole week lean toward that night like a plant toward a window? When you set tomorrow, did tomorrow ever arrive cleaner — or did it arrive with the same hand reaching before feet touched floor and a fresh excuse on its lips?

You know. Every bargain keeps attention on paper. And attention on paper is the trap fed.

Look at the shape of it. The bargainer never asks for the grey Tuesday dose over the sink, the rushed doorway dose in rain, the stale morning dose that burns the throat. He always asks for the pretty one. The Christmas one. The wedding one. The three-a-day controlled one. The tomorrow one when life will be calm.

Why only the pretty ones? Because if he asked for the whole chain — twenty a day, coughing, counting, patting pockets, stepping out mid-sentence for years — you would laugh him out of the room. He has to shrink the trap to smuggle it back in. He offers a corner and hopes you will not notice the prison attached.

You stood on that patio with glass in hand and still were not free to hear the sentence whole while paper hired your attention. That is the whole chain in one hour. Do you want that hour back on anniversaries?

There is no small trap and large trap. There is the trap kept alive and the trap left to die. Every bargain votes for alive.

### THE LAMP YOU MUST NOT RUB

Let me show you the special one plain, because this is where years are lost.

Picture a brass lamp on a high shelf, dusty, almost forgotten. You tell yourself you live without it now. Then a voice says, “Rub it once, just to prove you are master of it. One puff cannot rebuild anything after months.”

You rub. Smoke pours out. The walls rebuild. You are back inside the bottle arguing about how to get out again.

I watched this happen after five months clear. A man I knew — nineteen years dosing, then five months with nothing lit, mornings owned, shop queues easy, breaks taken with hands empty — takes one offered dose at a barbecue because drink is flowing and he wants to prove control. He coughs, feels dizzy, tells himself it was horrible. By nightfall he is bumming a second to cover the taste. By Friday he is buying a pack. By month’s end he is counting again as if the five months never happened.

I tell you that not to frighten you into staying. If you feel ill or worry about stopping, speak to your clinician. I deal only in what the dose gives you, which is nothing. I tell you because the story answers the only question that matters.

Did one dose prove control? Or did one dose rebuild the prison?

Ask from that barbecue, not from theory.

If one were proof of control, why did control need proving with paper? If one were nothing, why did nothing demand a second by nightfall? If five months made one safe, why did the safe man leave the barbecue counting?

You know because you have lived the smaller version a hundred times. The first dose after a gap never satisfies. It re-creates the itch it pretends to scratch. It never fixed the itch. It caused it.

This is why I speak of one with such finality. Not to threaten. To describe. One is not one. One is the whole chain re-threaded. The tug you starved for months learns in seconds that crying gets fed, and it starts crying on schedule again.

A slip, if it ever happens, is a warning rumble, not a crash. Do not torture yourself. Do not say, “I have blown it, so I may as well dose.” Notice what happened — the old script spoke, you answered for a second — close the door and walk on. But never plan a slip to test yourself. The lamp is not for testing. Leave it dusty.

When those future thoughts arrive — and they will arrive dressed as your own reason — hear them labelled now so they cannot pose as reason later:

“I’ll just have one to prove I’ve got control.” A non-smoker does not prove clear lungs by inhaling.

“I’ll have one because today is dreadful.” Paper never lifted a dreadful day; it only added timed exits to it.

“I’ll have one because today is perfect.” A perfect day needs no paper hired to stand in the photograph.

One is not an exception to the trap. One is the trap, rebuilt. The man who rubs to prove mastery proves only that he still believes the lamp holds something worth touching. You have already proved there is nothing in it worth keeping. That is the proof that lets you leave the shelf alone and get on with the barbecue, the talk, the plate, the laugh heard whole — the hour you had already learned to escape toward in your mind when you saw the patio plain.

### THE CLIFF YOU CANNOT FALL HALF OF

Cutting down wears kinder clothes, but it walks the same cliff.

Imagine standing at a cliff edge in fog. Someone says, “Jump, but only fall a little way. Control the fall. Three metres, not thirty.” You would laugh. Falling is falling. The ground does not negotiate.

Cutting down is trying to fall a little way.

We tell ourselves fewer doses means less slavery. Watch what fewer doses do in a real day.

You set ten, then six, then three. Each permitted dose becomes precious. You wait for it. You watch the clock for it. You inhale harder, hold longer, time the day around it. The diary starts neat — Monday: 6, Tuesday: 5 — then creeps: 5, 7, 9, a pack on Friday because the week was hard. The waiting makes each hit feel bigger, because the tug has grown bigger between hits. The preciousness proves nothing except that forbidden fruit tastes larger in the mind.

No diary ever left a doser free at eleven at night; it left him watching the clock for permission.

Ask from the diary, not from intention.

Did cutting down ever make you think less about doses? Or did it make every hour a negotiation? Did the small number hold for a month? Or did the number creep while the tug stayed exactly where it was, ordering the same exits with fewer permissions to obey it?

You know. We never cut down to being clear. We cut down to longer craving with better maths.

And lights, milds, smaller sticks? Same cliff, different coat. You cover tiny vent holes with lips and fingers without knowing and pull harder to get the dose you were always seeking. The machine counts less. The mouth does not. The throat knows. The tug knows.

If fewer meant being clear, the fewest would mean clearest, and none would mean completely clear. Why pay for the cliff in instalments when you can walk away from the edge?

See the pincer shut. If there were benefit in dosing often, you would not want to cut down. If there is no benefit in dosing often, there is no point dosing occasionally either. Occasional keeps the belief alive intact while removing the only thing that ever made the chain bearable — its regularity. You get all the thinking with fewer of the quietings. That is not control. That is the tug given a louder microphone and fewer biscuits.

Cutting down does not starve the little creature. It trains it to shout louder between feeds and teaches you to listen harder. Totality does the opposite. Totality lets the shouting die for lack of answer.

### TOMORROW IS THE FAVOURITE DAY OF THE TRAP

Then there is the softest bargain, the one that sounds almost sensible.

“I will finish tomorrow. After the wedding. After the deadline. When stress lifts.”

I understand why tomorrow shines. Today feels full. Tomorrow looks clean and empty, a fresh page with no tug on it.

But whose clean page is it?

Today you dose twenty times to quiet twenty tugs. Tomorrow you will dose twenty times to quiet twenty tugs. What changes in the night except the date? The tug does not attend weddings. The trap does not respect deadlines.

Ask from the calendar, not from comfort.

If doses helped with strain, would the most strained weeks not be the easiest weeks to stop, full of help? If tomorrow were truly cleaner, why has every past tomorrow arrived dirty with the same excuse? If waiting saved anything, what has a year of waiting saved except money spent and breath shortened?

That pull both ways — fear of staying and fear of leaving — is the tug-of-war of fear, and both ropes belong to the trap.

Fear of staying says, “You must stop, look at the cost.” Fear of leaving says, “You cannot stop, how will you cope, who will you be at parties?” Both sound like you. Neither is you. The trap holds one rope with threat and the other with false comfort, and you stand in the middle burning paper to bear the pull the paper creates.

Cut the ropes by seeing them. There is no coping to lose, because the dose never coped. There is no self to lose, because the doser was never the self — it was the self plus a tug. Tomorrow does not clean the tug. Seeing cleans it now.

Every tomorrow you set extends the bill for something you already know gives nothing. Continuing is the painful ongoing payment, not stopping. Stopping is walking off the field where the ropes pull.

Let me walk you through what that bill actually looks like, because tomorrow never stays one tomorrow. It staffs itself.

You say it on a Monday: after the wedding. The wedding is Saturday. Clean day, you think. No strain after the speeches. You will finish Sunday.

Watch Monday to Saturday with eyes open, not with hope.

Monday morning the hand finds the pack before feet find the floor. First dose on empty lungs. Tug count for the day: twenty. Evening count: fourteen left in the pack, notes already gone for the next. Thought spent: when to step out at work, whether the suit will smell, whether you can bum or must buy for Saturday.

Tuesday morning the same reach. Same first burn. Tug count: twenty. Evening count: pack finished, new pack opened. Thought spent: rehearsing Saturday — will they put heaters on the patio, will there be a lit circle, will you take one there and count it as not counting because it is a wedding?

Wednesday morning the same reach. The date on the phone has changed. Nothing else has. Tug count: twenty. Evening count: ash, dry mouth, throat raw by eleven. Thought spent: telling yourself the waiting proves control, while the waiting fills every gap between doses with listening for the next.

Thursday morning the same reach. Tug count: twenty. Evening count: counting again on the bus, maths about smell and cough. Thought spent: moving the clean day in your head from Sunday to Monday because Sunday will be tired after dancing.

Friday morning the same reach. Tug count: twenty. Evening count: buying extra for the weekend so you will not run out at the reception. Thought spent: half the day.

Saturday comes. The wedding. Sun on chairs. Talk loud. Glasses sweating. The same lit circle forms by the heaters. You dose with the circle and tell yourself this is the last party with paper, so it should be savoured. You miss half a speech watching your burn down. You step back so drift will not hit the woman beside you. You hold your drink awkwardly to shade the tip. By eleven mouth dry as paper, throat raw, coat hung out, head tight. Morning confirms without preaching: mouth papery, chest hauling, pack patted before feet move.

The date changed. The morning reach did not. The tug count did not. The evening count did not.

Then Monday arrives and tomorrow moves house. Now it says: after the deadline. Friday. Once the papers are in. Then clean.

Watch the second move with the same eyes.

Monday morning after the wedding: hand to pack before feet to floor. Tug count: twenty. Evening count: new pack. Thought spent: I have already paid for the wedding doses, I may as well pay till Friday.

Tuesday morning: same reach. Tug count: twenty. Evening count: same maths. Thought spent: will Friday be calm enough, will the boss sign, will the night after deadline deserve a last one to mark it?

Wednesday morning: same reach. Tug count: twenty. Evening count: same dry mouth. Thought spent: rehearsing Friday night — alone or with mates, with drink or without, inside or on the rail.

Thursday morning: same reach. Tug count: twenty. Evening count: throat raw again. Thought spent: moving the clean day again, from Saturday to Sunday, because Friday night will run late.

Friday — deadline day — the papers go in at four. You step out at five to mark it. Same flare at the light, seconds long. Same stale by the third pull. Same split attention through the evening. Same bus maths. Same Saturday morning reach.

Two postponements. Three weeks. Date changed twice. What else changed?

Nothing in the tug. Morning reach every one of twenty-one mornings. Tug count roughly twenty a day. Evening count a pack most days. Add it plain: over twenty-one days, about four hundred and twenty doses waited-for, lit, and paid for. Four hundred and twenty permissions negotiated. Four hundred and twenty interruptions to sentences, to work, to talk. Hours of thought spent — when, where, how many left, how to hide the smell, how to time the exit — all spent to keep alive something you had already seen gives nothing.

That is the part the bargain hides. Postponement does not pause the trap. It staffs it.

It does not keep you where you are while you wait. It sets you to work waiting. It hires your thumb to look for a click, your eyes to watch burns down, your mind to rehearse patios and deadlines, your pocket to pay, your morning to start with reaching. It adds paid tugs while giving nothing back. The wedding did not lend you calm to stop on. It lent you six days of extra tugs to staff the wait. The deadline did not lend you a clean page. It lent you two more weeks of counting to staff the delay.

Ask from those three weeks, not from intention.

Did the wedding Saturday arrive with fewer tugs because you waited for it? Or with the same twenty, plus a suit that smelled? Did the deadline Friday arrive with a quieter mind because you fed it till then? Or with the same reach, the same count, the same evening maths, plus four hundred extra doses billed?

You know because you just lived it forward. The calendar moved. The cage did not. The only thing postponement ever builds is more postponement, staffed by more doses.

This is why tomorrow is the favourite day of the trap. Tomorrow never has a tug in it when you picture it. Today always does. So you keep renting today at full price to protect a tomorrow that arrives as today the moment you wake. The trap collects the rent either way.

Stop paying rent for an empty room. The clean day is not after the date. The date never cleaned anything. Seeing cleans now, and seeing does not need Saturday or Friday to be true. You with hands empty at the rail on an ordinary Tuesday hear sentences whole. That hearing does not wait for speeches or signatures. It waits only for the ropes to be seen as ropes, both held by the same hands that sold you the paper.

You do not need a quieter week. You need no more staffed weeks. Let the wedding be a wedding and the deadline be papers, neither of them a reason to add four hundred tugs to a bill you have already marked as owing nothing.

### QUICK ANSWERS FOR THE LAST VOICES

Now let me answer rapid-fire, in your own words, so no whisper is left for later. When these sentences rise next month, you will hear them already answered.

“I’ll just cut to five a day. That’s sensible.”
Sensible for what? Five precious doses with all-day waiting, harder pulls, and the same tug ordering exits. You tried fewer. Fewer made paper bigger in the mind. Totality makes it nothing.

“I’ll keep only the wedding one, the Christmas one, the holiday one.”
A special one keeps the belief alive intact. If the best patio dose gave nothing, a December dose with tinsel gives nothing with tinsel. Special means the trap survives on anniversaries. Leave anniversaries to sun and leisure and company, which always did the giving.

“I went months once without, so one now can’t hurt.”
That is exactly the story that hurts. Months do not make one safe; one makes months irrelevant by replanting the tug. The man at the barbecue proved steadiness for five months. He proved the opposite with one. Guard the seeing and months stay yours.

“What if I slip? Does that mean I’m back to zero?”
No. A slip is a rumble strip, not a verdict. If a tyre touches it, you steer back, you do not drive into the ditch to prove you slipped. Guard the seeing, not the streak. Never plan a slip to test the seeing.

“Don’t trials show props help, and most tries fail alone? Don’t I need one?”
A trial counting months against a dummy and a survey counting how most tries happen answer two different questions. Neither answers you, here, once you see there is nothing to prop. I prescribe nothing and forbid nothing medical — I speak only of credit. The belief decides, not the aid. You are free with hands empty and attention back where it belongs the moment the credit is withdrawn.

“Isn’t this just brainwashing? You keep telling me it’s easy.”
Yes — in reverse. You were taught for years that paper calms, rewards, belongs with drink and friends. That teaching was never examined. I am asking you to examine it against your own Tuesdays and patios and diaries and find it false. If it is false, easy is not a trick. Easy is what is left when there is nothing to miss.

Hear the shape of every bargain now. It always offers a smaller trap as if smaller were outside. Smaller is inside with better lighting.

11. NEVER ALLOW JUST ONE OR A SPECIAL ONE
One keeps the trap alive.

**SUMMARY**
- Bargains after the best dose keep attention on paper, and attention on paper feeds the trap.
- One dose after months clear rebuilds the tug; it proves nothing about control.
- Cutting down makes each permitted dose more precious and lets numbers creep back up.
- Tomorrow never arrives cleaner because the tug does not keep calendars; waiting adds hundreds of paid doses and hours of thought for nothing.
- A slip is a warning to steer back, never a licence to plan another.
