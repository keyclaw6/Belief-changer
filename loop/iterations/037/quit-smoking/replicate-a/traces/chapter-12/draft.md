Chapter 12
YOUR LAST CIGARETTE

**IN THIS CHAPTER**
- Daylight on the kitchen table, the ashtray and the yellow tip
- The pull watched to its stale end
- The stubbed butt, the vow, the open door

*Your last cigarette does not make you free tomorrow — it proves you are free already.*

### ARE YOU CHAMPING AT THE BIT?

You have met the best one and watched it give nothing.

That late patio with drinks and laughter did the giving. The morning and the meal and the break stood whole without paper. The small bargains fell after the big one — no cutting down, no special one kept for Christmas, no cleaner tomorrow after the wedding or the deadline. You saw each bargain keep attention on paper, and attention on paper feeds the trap.

So I ask you plain, as I ask every smoker at this table:

Do you feel ready? You should be champing at the bit.

Not grim. Not braced. Eager. The way a prisoner feels when the door has been open all along and he finally sees daylight through it. If you feel that tug of excitement under the nerves, good. That is seeing doing its work.

If you do not feel it — if there is still a flat “what if life is grey without it?” sitting in your chest — do not summon hardness. Do not puff harder to get ready. Put this chapter down, go back through the patio and the bargains with eyes open, and come back when the seeing is clear. All you have to do is follow all the instructions.

I say that because I have sat where you sit. We all sat there, years inside, hand moving before thought, sure life without doses would be flat or impossible yet lighting the next one within the hour. We were not weak. We were conned. That con is now in pieces on the floor. You are stepping out of the nicotine trap, not jumping off a cliff.

“What if I am the one it will not work for? What if I miss it too much? What if stress hits tomorrow?”

I know those voices. We all know them. They sound like caution. They are the last hired hands of the con, asking for one more day’s wages.

Ask from your own Tuesdays, not from fear.

When did a dose ever solve the thing that stressed you? When did missing it ever come from the hour itself, rather than from the tug timed between doses? When you watched the best hour with and without, which hour heard sentences whole?

You know. The hour without heard whole. The problem papers sat untouched after the puff. The company laughed without help from ash.

After all you have seen, you have nothing to lose and everything to gain.

There is no friend to mourn here. There is a cell to leave.

### THE FAREWELL TABLE

Do not make this last one special. Make it ordinary. That is the whole point.

Take the next ordinary dose at an ordinary table in daylight — kitchen table, work bench, back step, wherever you would have lit anyway. No farewell tour. No extra pack bought to “do it properly.” No last night on the town to say goodbye. The trap loves a grand goodbye because grand means precious. Ordinary means true.

Smoke it as you always smoked, but with eyes open for once. This is the onlyбеж job left for your eyes.

Look at the tip as you light. Yellow stain already on the filter where your lips will go. First pull hot and papery on empty lungs. Watch the ash grow grey and flake. Notice the smell on your fingers within seconds, the dryness at the back of the throat by the third pull, the way attention narrows to burn-down and timing while the room goes on without you.

Keep watching to the stale end. That sour, breathless tail we always hurried or left half-done. Taste it on purpose this time. This is not punishment. This is seeing what was always there under the brainwashing.

“I love lighting up. The moment I light I am in heaven.”

I do not mock that heaven. We all named it heaven for a second. Ask from the light itself.

Did heaven last past the match flare? Or did it fade into counting, patting pockets, stepping out mid-sentence, Febreeze and mints and garage doors? Did the light add anything to food, to pause, to company — or did food and pause and company lend their pleasure while paper hired your hands?

You know because you just watched. The light promised heaven and delivered a timed exit. The room gave. Paper took.

It does plenty to throat and pocket and morning reach. It never once added to the hour.

Look again at that butt in the ashtray. Wet end, yellow, sour. That is the friend you feared losing. Does that look like a friend? Does that look like calm or pleasure or cope? That is a tube that squirts a dose, counted in a factory box for the day’s supply, hired to keep a tug alive.

Now stub it out. Hard. With meaning. Not in anger at yourself — warm to you always — but with contempt for the con that hired your hand for years.

As you stub it, say it inward, solemn as a vow and light as a laugh: I am done. I do not dose any more.

You can leave that hour behind easily, immediately and permanently.

Congratulations. You have just smoked your final dose.

Do not wait to feel different. You are different because you see different. The hand will not reach on its own any more because the reason for reaching is gone.

### WHAT THE TUG WILL DO NEXT

“What will my body do tomorrow morning?”

A fair question. Let me answer it flat, without drama and without promises I cannot make.

The only thing left crying is the Nipper.

That tiny physical creature, unfed, makes an empty, slightly restless tug — hollow, edgy, as if something needs doing with hands and mouth. It is not pain. It is not danger. It is a brief complaint from a loop that was fed every twenty minutes for years and now gets nothing.

Typically that complaint sharpens over the first few days, sharpest around day two or three, then fades within a few weeks. For most dosers it begins within hours and passes like a faint hunger that food was never meant to fix. Your body is your own and will have its own rhythm, and if you feel ill or worry about stopping, speak to your clinician. I deal only in what the dose gave you, which was nothing.

Do not mistake that tug for you. It is not proof you need paper. It is proof paper needed you.

Ask from the tug, not from dread.

When you felt that hollow tug before, what closed it? A dose closed it for minutes, then guaranteed it would return. When non-dosers feel restless, hollow, edgy between tasks, what do they do? They shift, breathe, drink water, get on with the next sentence. They do not time exits. Does the tug tell you paper solved strain — or does it tell you paper timed strain?

You know. The tug never pointed to the problem. It pointed to the last dose fading. A parking meter does not pay you for feeding it. It only stops ticking for a while.

So when the tug stirs tomorrow, do not argue with it and do not feed it. Notice it. Name it: there is the dying cry. Smile that you can hear it for what it is now. Within seconds attention moves back to kettle, keys, traffic, talk — the ordinary hour that was always whole without hired hands.

We fed that meter all day for years — coin after coin, puff after puff, fading in minutes, feeding again. We thought the coins bought calm. They bought the next rattle. Seeing buys quiet without coins.

This is why your mornings will not be empty. Feet find floor. Kettle clicks. Taste lingers after food instead of being scrubbed by smoke. Hands stay free on the break and the air hits the face whole. Nothing else in life needs changing. The body returns to breathing and tasting on its own because breathing and tasting were never broken — only hired out.

The tug dies for lack of answer. You live.

### THE TWO DOORS THAT STAY SHUT

Two thoughts will knock. They knock for everyone. Hearing them now means they arrive already labelled, not as your reason.

The first knock comes on a bad day.

Phone down after an argument. Papers still on the desk. Hand half-lifting on its own. Voice says: “If anything stressful happens, a dose takes care of it. Just one to steady me.”

Ask from the desk, not from the ache.

When did a dose ever lift a dreadful day? Did it move one paper? Did it soften one sentence of the argument? Or did it add timed exits, dry mouth, split attention and bus maths about smell to a day already full? If a perfect day needs no hired paper in the photograph, why would a dreadful day need hired paper in the wound?

You know. The dose never coped. You coped while dosing, then credited the dose. The rescuer was the perpetrator in kind clothes.

The second knock comes on a good day, and it sounds almost wise.

Drinks flowing. Tray of lights passing. Laughing circle by the heaters. Voice says: “You have been months clear. Prove control. One won’t hurt.”

Ask from that barbecue, not from theory.

I watched a man — nineteen years dosing, then five months with hands empty, mornings owned, queues easy — take one offered dose to prove mastery. He coughed, felt dizzy, called it horrible. By nightfall he took a second to cover the taste. By Friday he bought a pack. By month’s end he counted again as if the five months never happened. Did one prove control? Or did one rebuild the prison? If one were nothing, why did nothing demand a second by nightfall? If months made one safe, why did the safe man leave counting?

You know because you have lived the smaller version a hundred times. One is not one. One is the whole chain re-threaded. Months clear do not make one safe. One makes months irrelevant by replanting the tug.

A slip, if a tyre ever touches the rumble, is a warning to steer back — not a crash, not a verdict, never a licence to plan another. Notice, close the door, walk on. But never test the lamp to prove you are master of it. Leave it dusty.

Hear both doors for what they are. Not you thinking. The trap talking after it lost the argument.

When those thoughts rise next month, do not fight them and do not follow them. Reframe them with joy. Let every paper-thought trigger the same true answer, shouted inward with shoulders back:

You have carried the seeing through patio and morning and bargain and farewell table. You know the hour gives, paper takes. You know the tug fades when unfed and rebuilds when fed. There is nothing to mourn and everything to hold. So when the old script speaks, answer with the new seeing in one loud line:

YIPPEE! I'M FREE!

Say it till your chest believes what your eyes already know. Say it on the bad day and the bright patio alike. It is not bravado. It is reporting. The enemy is dead, the friend was never alive, the door is shut from the outside now.

You are not losing company. They are still there — sun, leisure, talk heard whole. You are not losing calm. Calm was yours between tugs. You are closing a cell you never chose to enter.

12. SMOKE YOUR FINAL CIGARETTE AND KNOW YOU ARE FREE
Close the trap with joy, not sadness.

**SUMMARY**
- The last ordinary dose shows its yellow tip, grey ash and stale end, and proves the pleasure was always the hour, not paper.
- The hollow tug after stopping is a small dying complaint that typically sharpens for a few days then fades, not proof of need.
- A bad day never got help from a dose, it only got interruptions added to it.
- One after months clear rebuilds the chain, it never proves control.
- Freedom is now, sealed with a vow, kept by seeing whenever the old thought knocks.
