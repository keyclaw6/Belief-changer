Chapter 3
WHAT DOES IT ACTUALLY DO FOR YOU

IN THIS CHAPTER
The scales on the kitchen table, the plate still warm, the step outside the door, the laugh in the circle, the hug that won't let go

*Name one thing it ever added to your food, your pause, or your company that was not already there without it.*

### THE SCALES WE ALL CARRY

We all carry the same private scales.

On one side we pile what smoking costs — money burned, breath short on the stairs, clothes that stink, the midnight dance with spray and mints, the cough in the morning, the worry at the back of the mind. On the other side we pile what we believe it gives — it calms me, it helps me think, it rewards me, it tastes good, it goes with coffee, with drink, with friends. Then we rock the scales back and forth and tell ourselves the second pile almost balances the first.

I understand that weighing. I did it for years. Every smoker does.

The fact is, those scales are rigged. They were handed to you rigged.

You were taught to ask, does the harm outweigh the pleasure. That question already hands the cigarette a pleasure it has never earned. It lets the stick sit on the scales as if it put something real there, and then asks you to pay a higher and higher price for it. Of course the weighing never ends. Of course you always light the next one within the hour. While you weigh harm against benefit, the benefit is never examined.

Let me move the scales off the table and ask the only question that matters.

Not, is it worth it. But, what did it ever do for you at all.

If a man sold you a bottle and said, this will make your food taste better, your breaks rest you more, your friends love you more, you would demand proof. You would try the food with and without. You would try the break with and without. You would watch the friends with and without. You would not take his word while he picked your pocket. Yet with cigarettes we never ran that plain test. We took the word of the crowd, the films, the adults on the step, and our own hurried hand, and we called the taking a gift.

You have already begun to see the nicotine trap for what it is — a small tug plus a loud story. Keep that seeing with you while you smoke as normal. I am not asking you to change what you do today. I am asking you to check the credit while you do it.

### SAY YOUR LIST OUT LOUD

Tell me plainly what you believe it does for you. Say it out loud where you sit, with the pack beside you. I have heard the list a thousand times because it was my list too.

"It relaxes me."
"It helps me concentrate."
"It is my reward."
"I like the taste."
"It gives me a break."
"It goes with drink and friends."
"It fills the dull moments."

Good. I thank you for your honesty. I will not mock one line of it. Every line felt true in the moment you said it. Feeling true is not the same as being true, and you are the kind of person who wants to know the difference.

Pick any one of them and hold it up to your own day. Not to theory. To yesterday.

You say it relaxes you. Yesterday when the phone went down hard and the hand shot out, what relaxed — you, or the tug underneath that had been stirring for an hour and then went quiet for four minutes after the dose. The bill sat where it sat. The sharp words sat where they sat. The papers on the desk did not move. Only the dose moved, in and out, and the tug quieted briefly because it was fed.

You say it rewards you. Yesterday after the class taught, the paragraph finished, the long drive done, the run to the shop done — each finished act paid with a light. Teaching a class. Finishing a story. Finishing a paragraph. Driving five hundred miles. Driving to the corner shop. Almost everything had its little party on the end of a match. Ask yourself who threw the party. Was it the stick, or was it you finishing the work.

You say you love smoking yet in the same breath you hate being a smoker. Both are honest. The moment I light up I am in heaven, but I feel shitty afterwards — that is how so many of us describe it. Bright at the match, stale by the middle, sour by the end, washing and chewing after. If the good were real and lasting, why would it need to be cleaned off you.

Look at that list again. Every reason names a moment that was already good or hard on its own — food, work done, pause, company — and then puts the cigarette on top of it. We never checked what the top layer added. We only felt the whole moment and gave the credit upward.

### WHAT DID IT ADD TO THE MEAL

Take the meal, because the meal never lies.

We finish eating. The plate is warm and empty. The taste is still in the mouth, rich and full. Then we push back, step out or crack the window, and light. We say, that completes it. That helps digestion. That is the best one of the day.

I ask you, as one smoker to another, to taste tomorrow with attention while you smoke as normal.

Taste the last mouthful before the light. Hold it. Notice the fullness on the tongue. Now light and draw. Notice what happens to that fullness. Does smoke sharpen it or smother it. Does the mouth feel cleaner after or papery and stale. Does the aftertaste sit warm or sour. You already know the answer from a thousand meals. The cigarette does not lift the flavour. It buries it under ash and then claims it finished the meal.

Try the plain test in your head without changing anything yet.

If the meal cigarette truly added taste, would non-smokers sit after good food feeling something missing. Do they push back from the table restless and incomplete. You have eaten with them. You have watched them sigh, linger over the last sip, pat the stomach, content. Nothing missing. Their meal closed on its own. Yours closed the same way, and the dose took the bow.

And what of the middle of that cigarette. The first pull is bright — I grant you that minute, that heaven second at lighting when the shoulders drop a fraction. Then what. The second third is papery. The last third is hot and bitter. Your mind is already on keys and traffic. You are not savouring. You are finishing. Would a genuine pleasure be abandoned by attention for three quarters of itself. Would taste need to be finished rather than held.

The meal was the pleasure. Hunger met food. That was complete before the match struck. The stick added nothing but a stale film, and then stole the credit for satisfaction your body had already earned.

### THE PAUSE AND THE CIRCLE

Now take the pause and the company, because those two keep more smokers than taste ever did.

We say, I need a break, and the dose gives me five minutes. We step onto the step, cold air on the face, no phone, no demands. We breathe out slowly. We feel the shoulders come down. We say, that is what the cigarette does for me.

I put it to you directly. What rested you.

Was it the lit tube, or was it stopping work for five minutes, standing, stretching the legs, looking at sky, being wanted by no one for a breath. Take the same five minutes tomorrow on the same step with the same cold on the cheeks and watch. The pause does the work. The pause always did the work. Non-smokers take that pause and feel the same settling without a dose. Their shoulders come down without smoke. Why would yours need smoke to do what air and stopping already do.

And the circle. Laugh on the patio, drinks out, someone offers, the ring tightens for a few minutes, you feel held inside it. We say, smoking is sociable. It belongs with nights out.

Ask yourself what held you in that ring.

Was it the paper between your fingers, or the laugh, the story, the drink in the hand, the late air, the five minutes when no desk wants you. Put a non-smoker in that same ring and watch his face. He laughs as loud. He stays as long. He belongs without a light. His belonging does not come from smoke. Why would yours. The company was the warmth. The night was the pleasure. The dose only stood beside them and had its photograph taken with them.

Here is the inversion you must let stand in plain words.

You did the work and the dose took the praise. You cooked the meal and the dose claimed the taste. You stopped and breathed and the dose claimed the rest. You laughed with friends and the dose claimed the belonging. It was the other way around the whole time. Cigarettes were your reward for almost everything because you pinned a reward to everything, not because the reward did anything. The party was the finished act, not the match.

Could a helper that helps nothing still feel helpful. Yes — if it quiets for minutes a tug it had itself kept alive, and lets the moment behind it do the real helping.

### THE HUG THAT WON'T LET GO

Picture this, because this picture tells the truth your scales never did.

You fall into dark water. A strong pair of arms hauls you out, holds you close, warms you. You cling to that rescuer with gratitude. You love those arms. You tell everyone those arms saved you.

Then one day you see, in clear light, that the same figure pushed you in a minute before, quietly, when you were not looking, and then jumped in after to pull you out in front of witnesses. The rescue was real as a feeling. The arms did lift you. But the danger was his work too.

Would you keep thanking him. Would you invite him home. Would you say, whatever his faults, he comforts me when I am drowning.

That is what the cigarette has been to us. We dangle in a low, edgy want between doses — an empty, slightly restless, slightly edgy little tug — and then the next dose lifts us out of that low for minutes. We feel rescued. We call the lifting pleasure, relaxation, reward. We never see the push, because the push came from the last dose wearing off, and the last before that, in a chain so smooth it feels like our own nature.

Keep the rescuer as the cigarette, never as yourself. There is nothing wrong with you for clinging. We all cling when we are hauled gasping to the side. The shame was never yours. The grip was.

Notice the grip in the hug. It holds a fraction too long. It does not let go when you want to step back to your food, your work, your children. It wants you back on the step in forty minutes. It wants you in the garage at midnight with spray in the air. It wants you searching drawers in slippers. A comfort that will not let go is not comfort. It is holding.

A friend lets you enjoy the meal and stay at the table. This hug pulls you from the table. A friend lets you rest and return. This hug interrupts rest to be thanked for rest. A friend shares your laugh. This hug stands in the middle of the circle and collects the warmth.

Once seen, that hug cannot be unseen. Every so-called gift arrives with the same shape — a brief lift out of a low that returns within the hour, asking to be thanked again. The lift is real as a sensation and false as a benefit. It never took you above the calm of a man who never started. It only returned you toward that calm for minutes, then dropped you back, a little lower, a little sooner.

So I ask you the three questions whose only honest answer ends the weighing.

Did the dose fix the hard thing, or did the hard thing sit untouched while the tug went quiet. Did the food taste fuller because of smoke, or duller, while hunger satisfied got the blame removed. Did the night hold you because of the stick, or did the night, the drink, the laugh hold you while the stick stood in the photograph.

Answer from your own mouth, not mine. You know.

### DONE TO YOU, NOT DONE FOR YOU

We have talked around the centre long enough. Let me put it in one line you can carry onto the step tomorrow.

Look at your hands, yellow at the nail, your coat that must be aired, your chest that rattles on the stairs, your evenings cut into forty-minute lengths, your mind that cannot hear bad news without reaching. That is plenty. No one needs to paint horns on it. It has been done to you daily, faithfully, dose by dose, while you paid for the doing.

Now set beside it what was done for you. Name one lasting help. The stress stayed. The work stayed done by you. The food tasted in spite of smoke. The pause rested because you paused. The friends laughed because they are your friends. The bright second at lighting faded to stale before the filter warmed. What remains on that side when credit is returned to its owners.

I tell you with all the certainty of a man who stood where you stand and who has watched thousands walk clear: It does plenty TO you. It does nothing FOR you.

Read that again slowly while the pack sits beside you. Let every TO be a picture — to your mouth, to your breath, to your pocket, to your midnight garage. Let every FOR be an empty chair — no calm that stayed, no taste that lifted, no work that needed it, no love that came from it. The first pile is tall and real. The second chair is empty.

That is why the scales can be put away. You are not weighing a small pleasure against a large price. You are weighing a large price against nothing. There is no decision to agonize over when one side holds air.

You do not need to fight the hand that reaches tomorrow. You need only to watch it with this line in your head and let your own eyes do the arguing. Label the tug when it stirs. Label the story when it speaks over it with your voice. Light as normal. Finish as normal. And each time ask, what did that do for me that the food, the pause, the laugh had not already done. Your tally will grow too tall to ignore, not because I told you, but because you saw.

3. BEGIN BY FEELING GREAT TO BE ESCAPING
There is no doom here, only freedom ahead.

**SUMMARY**
- The harm-versus-pleasure weighing hands the cigarette a benefit it never proved.
- Your spoken reasons all name moments that were already hard or good on their own.
- The bright minute at lighting is short and turns papery, then sour, while the mouth and mind pay after.
- Food closed on its own, the pause rested because you stopped, company warmed because people were there.
- The rescue feeling was a brief quiet of a tug the last dose had kept alive.
- Plenty has been done to your body, pocket and days, while nothing lasting was ever done for you.
